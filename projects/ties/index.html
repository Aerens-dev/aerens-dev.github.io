Ties

I buy ties in charity shops. One at a time, over several years, without ever writing anything down — and somewhere along the way it stopped being a few ties in a drawer and became about two hundred of them.

Which is a nice problem until you get dressed. There is no list. I could not have told you how many were green, or which ones had animals on them, or whether I owned two that were almost the same. Sorting that out by hand means handling two hundred ties and typing two hundred rows, and I was never going to do it.

So I photographed them instead, and asked an AI to do the looking. This is what came of it — the story first, the ties at the bottom.

That was more or less the whole plan: take the photographs, describe what you want, and let it work. No spreadsheet, no database, no form to fill in two hundred times.

The one idea worth stealing

There is a split running through this that I think is the whole trick, and it has nothing to do with ties.

An AI is very good at meaning. It reads a worn label, recognises that the small shapes are aeroplanes rather than birds, and knows that a bank's name on a tie means somebody had it made rather than that the bank makes ties.

It is bad at counting. Ask it what percentage of a tie is navy and it will tell you, confidently, a number it made up.

So it never gets asked. Everything that is a judgement goes to the AI, and everything that is a measurement goes to ordinary code that counts actual pixels. Every colour on this page was measured that way — none of them were described. That division is why I believe the result.

Navy with gold

The first tie I did is navy with small gold rings. Measure it honestly and it is about 97% navy: the gold covers less than one percent of the cloth.

But nobody has ever described that tie as navy. It is a navy-and-gold tie, and the gold is a rounding error. That gap — between what covers the most cloth and what you actually see — turned out to be the thing the whole catalogue had to get right, and it took several goes.

It now records both: the background colour, and then the colours of the marks on it, measured against each other rather than against the whole tie. Gold stops being a rounding error and becomes most of what is printed.

Some ties refuse to answer. An evenly striped tie is half one colour and half another, and picking a winner would describe a different tie — so for6 of these the catalogue simply says there isn't a background colour, which I like better than a confident guess.

It read owls as pineapples

My favourite mistake. Most of these were photographed with the tie lying sideways, because that is how you hold one in a shop. Turned the right way up, a tie I had catalogued as pineapples and thistles is unmistakably owls sitting on pine branches.

Sideways, an owl's body really does look like a pineapple. Nothing had broken; nothing had errored. It had simply been asked to look at a picture nobody would hold that way. Turning every tie upright first fixed it, and lost a lobster from another tie that turned out to be a small flag.

Notes, for the curious

Below the surface this was mostly an argument about measurement, and it is the half that would lose most readers — so it is down here rather than up there.

  • Colours are clustered in OKLab, a colour space built so that "how different do these look" is a plain distance. In ordinary RGB, two colours that look identical can be far apart and two that look nothing alike can be neighbours, which makes every threshold meaningless.
  • The measurements were wrong five times before they were right, and not once did anything crash. A list of colours looks like a list of colours whether or not it describes the tie. The gold rings were deleted by a noise filter; the blur where navy meets gold became its own colour and took two thirds of the pattern; the sheen on the silk became another; a hand holding a tie became the tie's main colour.
  • Every one of those is now a test — which is the only defence against a program that runs perfectly and answers wrongly. The owls are the version no test catches, because nothing was broken.
  • Nothing here is a stock photo or a placeholder. Every swatch is a value taken off one of my own photographs, and the file behind this page is 90 KB of plain text you could read in a text editor.
