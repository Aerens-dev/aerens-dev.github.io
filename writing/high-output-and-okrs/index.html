High Output Management & Measure What Matters

Andy Grove · John Doerr

31 August 2026 · book notes · management

Grove wrote a manual for running a factory in 1983 and then used it to explain how to run anything. Doerr took the shortest chapter in it and wrote a book about that one chapter, thirty-five years later.

Reading them together is the only way I would read either now: the first is where the ideas came from, and the second is what happened when one of them escaped.

Grove

Production is the model for everything

The limiting step

The three-minute egg decides when you start the coffee. Find the longest, least flexible step and schedule everything else backwards from it.

Catch it at the lowest-value stage

Reject the bad egg before you cook it, not after you plate it.

Indicators are windows you cut

You cannot see a process. You make specific holes and look through those, which means the holes you chose are the only process you know about.

Pair every indicator

The idea here most worth stealing on its own. A count is an instruction to raise the count, and whatever paid for it is invisible because nobody is measuring it.

The stagger chart

Forecast the same quarter again every month, and plot every forecast. What comes out is not a forecast, it is your own bias — how wrong you are and reliably in which direction.

Inspect against instability, not the clock

Leverage

Your output is your team’s output

The core equation, and it does not include anything you personally made.

Delegation is not abdication

You keep the responsibility, so monitor the process rather than the result. Which is the lowest-value-stage rule pointed at your own job.

Time is the medium

Batch similar work, treat the calendar as a production plan, and keep standard responses ready for interruptions. Grove’s point is that a manager’s day is manufacturing capacity and should be scheduled like it.

Meetings and decisions

Two kinds of meeting

Process-oriented ones recur and carry knowledge. Mission-oriented ones are called once to produce a decision. Mixing them ruins both.

The one-on-one

The highest-leverage hour available, and almost always run backwards.

Free discussion, clear decision, full support

The six questions

Planning is three steps

Environmental demand, present status, and what closes the gap. With the part people skip: today’s actions produce output a year from now, so you plan for the gap ahead and act immediately.

Organisation

The hybrid organisation

Mission-oriented units are responsive, functional units are efficient, and every company of size runs both. The tension is permanent rather than solvable.

Three modes of control

People

Task-relevant maturity

The idea that stops “what kind of manager are you” being a sensible question. Style is set by how new the person is to this task, not by your temperament or their seniority.

Can’t do it, or won’t

Reviews assess performance, not personality

The purpose is to improve performance, not to justify a number. Grove’s delivery advice is three words: level, listen, and leave yourself out.

Once money stops working

Output past a certain point comes from self-actualisation, and the way to get there is to make the work a game — clear rules, a way of keeping score, and a real chance of losing.

Training is your job

The chapter that escaped

Doerr

Ideas are easy, execution is everything — Grove’s line, and the thesis of the second book.

The shape

Focus, and what you decline

Align — everyone reads everyone’s

Half of them should come upward

Track — four moves, not one

Two kinds of ambition, two kinds of success

Never attach it to pay

Conversations, feedback, recognition

The replacement for the annual review, and Doerr’s argument that objectives need a delivery system or they are a spreadsheet.

Watch time

The best worked example in either book.

Health metrics

Pair an ambitious objective with a number that stops you winning it and losing the thing. Which is the paired-indicator idea again, thirty-five years later, and Doerr does not quite say so.

The two case studies

Operation Crush — Intel re-pointing an entire company against Motorola in weeks, with objectives as the mechanism. Culture — the chapter arguing that none of this survives an organisation that punishes a missed stretch goal, which is the honest caveat on all of the above.

What thirty-five years did

Reading them together, the thing that stands out is the direction of loss. Doerr added transparency, scoring, two kinds of goal and a replacement for the annual review — all genuine additions. What did not survive the journey is most of the rest of Grove: the paired indicator, task-relevant maturity, the one-on-one as the subordinate’s meeting, and the observation that a manager’s real product is other people’s output.

Which is a shame, because an organisation running objectives without those is running the scoreboard without the game.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 34 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

  1. 1You are cooking a three-minute egg, two-minute coffee and one-minute toast, to be served together. What do you schedule first?

    Not quite. That is the absence of a plan. Without identifying the limiting step there is nothing to schedule the others against.

    Not quite. Then it is cold when the egg lands. The order follows from the constraint, not from what is easiest to start.

    Right. The longest, least flexible step sets the start time for every other one. Grove runs the whole book off this breakfast.

  2. 2Why reject the bad egg before cooking it?

    Not quite. True, and it is the last and worst case rather than the principle. The principle applies between every pair of stages.

    Not quite. The inspection is not what costs — the discarded work is. A late inspection can be fast and still expensive.

    Right. The defect is identical at every stage; what changes is how much work has been added on top of it by the time you find it.

  3. 3What does Grove's black-box metaphor imply about your indicators?

    Not quite. The metaphor is about opacity by default, not in principle. Cutting windows is exactly how it becomes knowable.

    Right. Which is worth remembering when a number surprises you: you picked where to look, so surprise may be about the window rather than the process.

    Not quite. More windows cost attention and can crowd out the ones that matter. Grove's concern is which few, and paired.

  4. 4Why does a count of vouchers processed need a second number beside it?

    Right. Pair it with errors found in an audit and the trade becomes visible. Neither number means anything alone.

    Not quite. Trends are useful and are a different idea. A trend in an ungamed metric is still a trend in a metric that is being gamed.

    Not quite. The pairing is not redundancy. The second number measures a different thing — quality against quantity — rather than the same thing again.

  5. 5What does a stagger chart actually show you?

    Right. Each line is one month's guess at the same quarter. The shape they make together is a fact about the forecaster rather than about the quarter.

    Not quite. That is what any single forecast claims. The chart's value is the pattern across repeated forecasts, not the latest one.

    Not quite. Volatility would show up in the actual, not in the drift of successive forecasts toward it.

  6. 6When should the inspection rate go back up?

    Right. The rate should track instability, so it falls as a process settles and jumps the moment it is disturbed. A fixed rate is wrong in both directions.

    Not quite. By then the expensive thing has already happened. A change is a leading signal; a defect is a lagging one.

    Not quite. Unbiased sampling is a statistics goal. Grove's is economic: spend inspection where the defect probability actually is.

  7. 7What counts toward a manager's output in Grove's equation?

    Not quite. There is no weighting. The whole of it counts, which is what makes leverage rather than effort the thing to optimise.

    Not quite. Grove excludes the first term deliberately. Personal output is a way of feeling productive that does not appear in the equation.

    Right. And nothing they personally produced. Which is uncomfortable for anyone promoted out of doing the work well.

  8. 8What is the right moment to review delegated work?

    Not quite. That is not delegation. The frequency should follow task-relevant maturity rather than default to constant.

    Not quite. Ownership is not damaged by monitoring the process; responsibility stayed with you regardless, which is what makes abdication the wrong word.

    Right. Reviewing the finished thing means all of it may be wrong. Reviewing the plan costs a fraction and catches the same error.

  9. 9How does Grove want a manager to treat their calendar?

    Not quite. Availability without structure is how the day gets consumed by whoever asks first, which is not the same as being useful to them.

    Not quite. That is the default state he is arguing against — a calendar that is entirely reactive is capacity nobody planned.

    Right. A manager's day is manufacturing capacity, so it gets scheduled like capacity rather than filled as requests arrive.

  10. 10What goes wrong when the two kinds of meeting are mixed?

    Right. Process-oriented meetings recur and exchange information; mission-oriented ones are called once to produce a decision. The failure is holding the wrong kind, not holding meetings.

    Not quite. Length is a symptom. The structural problem is that the two have different purposes and different attendees.

    Not quite. They usually keep attending, which is worse — the time is spent and neither purpose is served.

  11. 11Whose meeting is a one-on-one?

    Not quite. Staying informed is the outcome, and it happens by listening. A manager-led agenda produces a status update instead.

    Right. The manager listens and asks one more question. Almost every one-on-one is run backwards, with the manager reporting downward.

    Not quite. Grove is specific about the asymmetry. Equal airtime in practice means the more senior person sets the topics.

  12. 12What does full support require?

    Not quite. Free discussion is for getting every real opinion out, not for converging. Some people will remain unconvinced and still support it.

    Not quite. The common failure is skipping a step and calling it consensus. A clear decision has someone who made it.

    Right. Requiring agreement means requiring unanimity, and waiting for unanimity is how the clear-decision step gets skipped.

  13. 13Which of the six questions is most often dropped?

    Not quite. Frequently ambiguous, and rarely forgotten entirely — the argument about it is usually visible.

    Not quite. Often vague, but the pressure of the situation tends to supply a deadline whether or not anyone names one.

    Right. The decision gets made and then propagates by rumour. The other five feel like part of deciding; this one feels like admin, which is why it goes.

  14. 14What is the part of Grove's planning process people skip?

    Not quite. Important, and it is the first step rather than the neglected one.

    Not quite. Frequently done badly and not the step he singles out. The timing relationship is the counterintuitive part.

    Right. Environmental demand, present status, close the gap — and the gap being closed is next year's, which means the action is this week's.

  15. 15How should a company resolve the tension between mission-oriented and functional units?

    Not quite. A common practice and an expensive one. It swaps which set of problems you have rather than resolving anything.

    Right. Mission units are responsive, functional units are efficient, and any company of size needs both. Two bosses is a consequence rather than a design flaw.

    Not quite. Committing to one gives up what the other provides. Grove's claim is that companies of size always end up with both regardless of the stated design.

  16. 16Which mode of control works when ambiguity is high?

    Not quite. They remove it only for what can be written down in advance, which is precisely what high ambiguity means you cannot do.

    Right. Contracts need specifiable obligations and markets need priceable exchanges; neither survives high ambiguity. Culture is the only one that does, which is why it cannot be installed in a hurry.

    Not quite. Markets fit self-interest and low ambiguity. Pricing something nobody can specify is the problem, not the solution.

  17. 17What determines the right management style for a given person?

    Not quite. A senior person doing something for the first time has low maturity at that task, and managing them as a senior person is how it goes wrong.

    Right. Not their seniority and not your temperament. The same person on two jobs in the same week needs two different managers.

    Not quite. Preference is not the variable. Someone can want autonomy on a task where they cannot yet succeed with it.

  18. 18Someone is underperforming. Why does the diagnosis matter so much?

    Not quite. Grove would put both at the manager's door. The reason to distinguish them is that the remedies differ, not the blame.

    Right. Train someone who is unmotivated and you have wasted the training; motivate someone who lacks the skill and you have made them try harder at something they cannot do.

    Not quite. That is a later question. The distinction earns its keep long before anyone is being dismissed.

  19. 19What is a performance review for?

    Right. Grove's delivery advice is three words: level, listen, leave yourself out. Once the review's job is to defend a decision about pay, the feedback stops being usable.

    Not quite. Reasonable, and vague enough to describe most things. The specific claim is that the purpose is improvement rather than justification.

    Not quite. A real administrative need, and the one that quietly takes the review over. Grove treats it as the most important task-relevant feedback anyone gets.

  20. 20How does Grove suggest getting output past the point where money stops motivating?

    Right. Self-actualisation is the driver at that level, and the sports analogy is his practical route to it. The chance of losing is the part that matters.

    Not quite. The whole point is that the curve flattens. Past that, more of the same input buys nothing.

    Not quite. Meaning helps and is not a mechanism. The game framing gives you something to build: rules, a score, and stakes.

  21. 21Why does Grove insist training belongs to the manager rather than a training department?

    Not quite. A cultural argument rather than his. Grove's case is arithmetic, which is why it is hard to argue with.

    Right. It is the highest-leverage item on his list and the one most reliably not done, because it is never urgent.

    Not quite. Sometimes true and not the argument. The argument is about leverage, which holds even where a professional trainer knows more.

  22. 22Grove's whole management-by-objectives chapter comes down to two questions and one warning. What is the warning?

    Right. Said in 1983, restated by Doerr thirty-five years later, and still the thing most implementations get wrong first.

    Not quite. Doerr's bottom-up argument, which came later. Grove's warning is specifically about compensation.

    Not quite. Also Doerr's addition. Grove's chapter is two questions and the pay warning, and very little else.

  23. 23What is the test of whether something is a key result?

    Not quite. Good practice, and it does not separate a key result from a well-written task. The number does.

    Not quite. Timeboxing matters and is not the test. A time-bound thing with no number is still not measurable.

    Right. That is the entire test. The objective is allowed to be qualitative and inspirational; the key results are what make it checkable.

  24. 24What makes the three-to-five limit a feature rather than an arbitrary cap?

    Not quite. A memory argument would be satisfied by writing them down. The point is prioritisation, not recall.

    Not quite. True and mechanical. Doerr's stronger claim is about the act of choosing rather than the arithmetic of dilution.

    Right. If the limit only excluded bad ideas it would cost nothing and mean nothing. The constraint bites precisely because the discards were worth doing.

  25. 25What does making objectives public actually solve?

    Not quite. A real benefit and a secondary one. The primary effect is that everyone can see how their work connects to the rest.

    Not quite. Peer accountability comes with tracking rather than with visibility, and leaning on it is how transparency turns into surveillance.

    Right. Including the chief executive's, which is the part that makes it credible rather than a reporting exercise.

  26. 26What goes wrong when every objective cascades from the top?

    Right. Roughly half should come up from the teams. Cascading is not wrong — cascading exclusively is.

    Not quite. Pure cascading is the most aligned arrangement possible. Alignment is what it buys and ownership is what it costs.

    Not quite. Sometimes true and not the structural problem. Even perfectly informed top-down objectives arrive without ownership.

  27. 27Which of the four check-in moves is hardest in practice?

    Not quite. Adding is the most comfortable of the four, which is why focus needs defending separately.

    Not quite. Easy, and sometimes too easy — updating a target you are missing is how a committed objective quietly becomes an aspirational one.

    Right. Continue is the default, update and start feel like progress, and stopping requires admitting something was wrong. An objective nobody ever stops is a wish.

  28. 28An objective scores 0.7. Is that good?

    Right. Conflating the two is the most common failure. The same number is a warning in one column and a success in the other.

    Not quite. Only for aspirational objectives. A committed one is expected at 1.0, and missing it means something needs investigating.

    Not quite. That reading makes stretch goals impossible, because everyone will propose targets they can hit.

  29. 29What happens when bonuses depend on hitting the objectives?

    Not quite. They become more conservative rather than more precise. Precision in service of hitting the number is not the kind you wanted.

    Right. It is rational rather than dishonest, which is why exhortation does not fix it. Grove said the same thing thirty-five years earlier and in fewer words.

    Not quite. They work harder at the negotiation over what the target should be, which happens before any of the work.

  30. 30What is Doerr's argument for CFRs?

    Right. Transparency comes from the objectives and accountability from the continuous conversation. The annual review is one backward-looking hour dominated by the last six weeks.

    Not quite. The complaint is about frequency and structure rather than fairness. A fair annual review is still one conversation a year.

    Not quite. Adjacent, and the argument here is about the cadence of feedback rather than the currency of reward.

  31. 31Why did YouTube choose watch time over views?

    Right. Chosen deliberately and held for four years. It is Grove's paired indicator arrived at from the other end — a measure selected because it could not be faked.

    Not quite. Commercially true and not the argument in the book, which is about which number resists being gamed.

    Not quite. Views are measured accurately. The problem is that they can be won honestly by something you did not want to reward.

  32. 32A health metric alongside an ambitious objective is a rediscovery of which earlier idea?

    Right. The same move thirty-five years later — a second number so you cannot win the target and lose the thing. Doerr does not quite say so.

    Not quite. That is about forecasting bias across repeated predictions, which is a different problem entirely.

    Not quite. A scheduling idea from the production half of the book, unrelated to how a measure gets gamed.

  33. 33What does the culture chapter add to the rest of the book?

    Not quite. Operation Crush is a company under threat rather than in growth, which cuts against that reading.

    Not quite. A tempting extension and not the claim. Culture is treated as the precondition rather than as another target.

    Right. The honest caveat on everything above it — you can install the mechanics perfectly and still get sandbagging if the culture makes a miss dangerous.

  34. 34What is the direction of loss between the two books?

    Not quite. One chapter was. An organisation running objectives without the rest is running the scoreboard without the game.

    Not quite. Unfair to the second book. Public objectives and the committed-versus-aspirational split are not in Grove at all.

    Right. Transparency, scoring, two kinds of goal and CFRs are genuine additions. The paired indicator, task-relevant maturity and the one-on-one did not come with them.
