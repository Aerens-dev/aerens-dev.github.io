How to Fail at Almost Everything and Still Win Big

Scott Adams

31 August 2026 · book notes · systems

A cartoonist’s memoir that turns out to be a book about systems, written by someone whose main qualification is a long and specific list of things that did not work.

Goals are for losers

A goal is a state you are not in. So you are in failure continuously until one moment, and then you have no goal. A system is something you do on a schedule that raises your odds in a way you cannot specify in advance.

Passion is bullshit

Passion is what people report after something works, because it is a better story than luck and persistence. Adams’ claim is that the causation runs backwards from where the interviews put it.

The talent stack

The idea in here most directly about anyone building a portfolio. You do not need to be world class at one thing. Be in the top quarter at several things that combine, and the combination is what nobody else has.

Every skill roughly doubles the odds

Manage your energy

The master metric, and the one that makes the rest operational. Diet, sleep, exercise and schedule are not a health chapter here — they are the input to everything above them.

Failure is a resource

Collect them deliberately. Adams’ own list is long and he treats each entry as an input rather than a wound.

Simplify rather than optimise

An optimised system is a chain where every link is required, so one change takes the whole thing. A simple one survives being disturbed, which is the only condition it will ever actually operate in.

Willpower is a finite resource

Quality is downstream of volume

Luck can be managed

Not the outcome. The odds. You cannot make the window larger and you can arrive at it more often, which is the honest version of making your own luck.

Fitness is the base

You are a moist robot

His framing, and deliberately unflattering: you are programmable, so change the inputs instead of arguing with yourself about resolve.

Affirmations

The chapter where the book gets strange, and he says so first. Worth keeping, because the mechanism he actually proposes is not mystical at all.

Knowing when to quit

The counterweight the genre usually leaves out.

The skills he thinks are worth having

Public speaking, psychology, business writing, accounting, design, conversation, overcoming shyness, a second language, golf, proper grammar, persuasion, technology, and voice technique. Notable for how few of them are a job, and for golf.

One thing worth saying out loud: this book and the Grove and Doerr notes disagree in public. Adams says goals are for losers; Doerr wrote a book about goals. The disagreement is smaller than it looks — Doerr’s objectives are a system for choosing and revisiting, which is Adams’ point with a spreadsheet attached — but it is not nothing, and I would rather have both on the shelf than pick.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 15 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

  1. 1What is structurally wrong with a goal, on Adams' account?

    Not quite. A reasonable criticism from elsewhere, and not his. His is about which state you spend your time in.

    Right. That is the whole argument. Lose thirty pounds is false about you every day until it is briefly true; eat right is true of you on any given evening.

    Not quite. He has no objection to ambition. The complaint is about the shape of the thing, not its height.

  2. 2Which way does Adams say the causation runs?

    Not quite. He does not claim that. They correlate strongly — he disputes which one is doing the causing.

    Not quite. That is the version he is arguing against, and the one every profile of a successful person is written in.

    Right. And the interview afterwards reorders it, because passion is a better story than luck and persistence. Energy is what he thinks actually predicts anything.

  3. 3What is rare about a talent stack?

    Right. Adams' own is an ordinary cartoonist who knew some business and was somewhat funny. Not one of the three is remarkable and Dilbert is.

    Not quite. The top quarter is deliberately an ordinary bar. If it were rare the idea would offer nothing over being world class at one thing.

    Not quite. They frequently are not, and that is part of the appeal — an unrelated skill still multiplies the combinations available to you.

  4. 4What follows from the doubling claim?

    Not quite. The opposite of his claim. Adjacency is helpful and not required — a cartoonist who understands accounting has options a better cartoonist does not.

    Not quite. That is the spike strategy the stack is offered as an alternative to.

    Right. It is the mechanism under the stack: each addition multiplies rather than adds, so the fifth is worth more than the first four together.

  5. 5Why does Adams treat diet, sleep and exercise as structural rather than as a health chapter?

    Not quite. He does not make that move. It is instrumental — the base of a stack rather than the top of one.

    Not quite. That is a willpower argument, and he thinks willpower is the wrong thing to lean on at all.

    Right. Which is what makes energy the master metric of the book, and why the practical move is matching the task to the state you are actually in.

  6. 6What is the test of whether a failure was worth having?

    Not quite. Also worth asking, and closer to Duke than to Adams. His test is about what remains afterwards.

    Right. Not whether it worked. The residue is the return, and treating each attempt as an input rather than a wound is what makes a long list of failures an asset.

    Not quite. A good question about decision quality and a different one. Adams is asking what you now have that you did not.

  7. 7Why does Adams prefer simple systems to optimised ones?

    Not quite. They do not, and he concedes it. The optimised one wins in the case that never happens.

    Right. And being disturbed is the only condition any system will actually operate in. Most advice is written by people describing something that has not yet met anything.

    Not quite. True and beside the point. The argument is about surviving perturbation, not about communication.

  8. 8How does the willpower claim relate to systems over goals?

    Not quite. A sensible tactic that follows from the energy chapter. The systems argument is stronger: arrange things so the hard work needs no resolve at all.

    Right. If willpower is a depleting resource, a plan that spends it is a plan that fails in the afternoon. So the design constraint is to spend none.

    Not quite. He is uninterested in that comparison, because it points at a trait rather than at something you can build.

  9. 9What is Adams actually recommending here?

    Not quite. He is claiming quality is produced by quantity rather than traded against it, which is a stronger and stranger claim.

    Not quite. The standards apply at selection. What changes is how many candidates the selection gets to choose from.

    Right. It is an argument about where to spend effort, not a lowering of standards. The selection happens after the fact because it cannot happen before it.

  10. 10What part of luck is under your control?

    Not quite. Too strong. Systems matter precisely because the odds are movable even though the outcome is not.

    Not quite. That is the claim the chapter exists to deny. Preparation changes the distribution and not the draw.

    Right. You cannot make the window bigger. You can arrive at it more often, which is the honest version of making your own luck.

  11. 11Where does fitness sit in Adams' structure?

    Not quite. That inverts it, and inverting it is how people drop it first when they get busy.

    Not quite. It is not one of the stacked skills. It is the thing the stack stands on.

    Right. Load-bearing rather than motivational. When the bottom layer goes, everything resting on it goes with it.

  12. 12What does the moist-robot framing imply you should do?

    Right. Deliberately unflattering, and the same claim as the willpower section from the other side: you are programmable, so reprogramme rather than exhort.

    Not quite. He does gesture at determinism, but the usable claim is about inputs. Whether free will exists does not change the method.

    Not quite. The opposite conclusion. Programmability is what makes change tractable — it just relocates where the effort goes.

  13. 13What is the mechanism Adams actually proposes for affirmations?

    Not quite. The version he is at pains to distinguish his from, and the reason the chapter has the reputation it does.

    Right. Which is testable and much less odd than the reputation. The middle step is the entire claim, and he says himself the chapter sounds crazy.

    Not quite. A plausible mechanism and not his. He points at attempt count rather than at a feeling.

  14. 14Why is quitting a hard skill rather than a failure of nerve?

    Not quite. Real, and a different problem. His point is harder: even without sunk-cost bias the evidence at that moment does not separate the cases.

    Right. Which is why just keep going is not advice. If the two were distinguishable at the decision point there would be no decision.

    Not quite. The genre's usual position, and the one he includes this chapter as a counterweight to.

  15. 15What is notable about the skills Adams lists?

    Right. Public speaking, psychology, business writing, accounting, design, conversation, a second language, grammar, persuasion, technology, voice — and golf, because of the rooms it puts you in.

    Not quite. Several are not taught anywhere formally, including overcoming shyness and conversation, which is part of why he bothers listing them.

    Not quite. Accounting, technology and golf are not. The list is deliberately scattered, because unrelated skills are what make a stack rare.
