Drive

Daniel H. Pink

31 August 2026 · book notes · motivation

Pink’s argument is that reward and punishment are not wrong, they are narrow — and that we kept using them long after the work stopped being the kind they fit.

Three operating systems

1.0 is survival. 2.0 is reward and punishment — carrots and sticks — and it genuinely worked, because it was built for routine work. 3.0 is intrinsic: autonomy, mastery and purpose, which is the rest of this page.

The problem is not that 2.0 failed. It is that it is still running over work that stopped being routine.

The anomaly

Linux, Apache and Wikipedia are built by people paid nothing, most of whom have jobs already. Motivation 2.0 has no account of them at all — not a weak one, none.

The candle problem

Candle, box of tacks, matches. Fix it to the wall so the wax does not drip. Glucksberg offered money for speed and the paid group was three and a half minutes slower. Tip the tacks out of the box, so no insight is needed, and the paid group wins easily.

Rewards narrow the beam

Which is the generalisation, and why the result is not a curiosity. A reward concentrates attention. That is exactly what you want when the answer is in front of you, and exactly what you do not want when it is off to one side.

Seven ways it goes wrong

Extrinsic rewards can extinguish intrinsic motivation, diminish performance, crush creativity, crowd out good behaviour, encourage cheating, become addictive, and shorten your horizon.

The Sawyer Effect

Pay children to draw and they draw less afterwards. Reward turns play into work.

If-then, and now that

The damage is in the promise. A reward dangled in advance steers the work toward itself. The same reward handed over afterwards, unannounced — especially praise — does very little harm.

Pay enough to take money off the table

None of this is an argument for paying badly. Pay unfairly or too little and money is the only thing anyone thinks about.

Type I and Type X

X is organised around what the work pays. I is organised around the work. Type I is made rather than born, it does not disdain money, and it outlasts the other one.

Autonomy over four things

Task, time, technique, team. Naming them is the useful part, because it turns a vague good into four questions with answers.

Mastery is a mindset

Dweck. Whether ability is a fixed quantity or something effort makes decides what a hard afternoon means.

Mastery is a pain

Pink is blunt that the flow research does not make this pleasant. It is years of deliberate practice, and most of it does not feel like progress while it is happening.

Mastery is an asymptote

You approach it and never arrive. Which is the frustration, and also the entire appeal — an achievable version would be finished.

Flow, and Goldilocks tasks

Csikszentmihalyi. Match the challenge to the capability: above it is anxiety, below it is boredom, and the band between them is where the work does itself.

Purpose, and where it shows

Profit as an insufficient organising idea. The test is not whether an organisation has a purpose statement; it is whether the purpose is visible in what gets measured, what gets said, and who is allowed to set a target.

Goals set for you

Kasser found that people chasing extrinsic goals — wealth, fame, image — were more anxious and more depressed even when they hit them. Targets are prescription strength: Sears’ repair quotas and Enron’s numbers were both met.

Two questions

Clare Boothe Luce told Kennedy that a great person is one sentence. That is the famous half. The other one is smaller and does more work.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 17 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

  1. 1What is Pink's actual complaint about Motivation 2.0?

    Right. He is explicit that carrots and sticks genuinely suited twentieth-century routine work. The failure is that it was kept past the job it fitted.

    Not quite. That is closer to the baseline-pay argument, which is a separate idea about the level rather than about the mechanism.

    Not quite. He argues the opposite. 2.0 was well matched to the work it was designed for, which is why it survived so long.

  2. 2Why are Linux and Wikipedia awkward for Motivation 2.0?

    Right. Not a weak explanation — none. Which is the cleanest kind of counter-example, because it is a fact rather than an argument.

    Not quite. Scale rules that out. The interesting thing is how ordinary the participation is, not how exceptional.

    Not quite. Recognition is still an extrinsic reward, so that account keeps the 2.0 machinery intact. The point is that the machinery is not needed.

  3. 3Glucksberg's incentivised group solved the classic candle problem three and a half minutes slower. What changed when the tacks were tipped out of the box?

    Right. That reversal is the finding. The only variable is whether the solution is in front of you, and the reward is good for one case and bad for the other.

    Not quite. The difference showed clearly, in the paid group's favour. A floor effect would have erased the gap rather than reversing it.

    Not quite. It did not hurt at all — the paid group won easily. Half the value of the experiment is that the effect flips sign rather than shrinking.

  4. 4What is the general mechanism behind the candle result?

    Not quite. That is nearer the Sawyer Effect. The candle problem shows the effect on a task nobody was doing for fun.

    Right. Which is why the same incentive is right for an algorithmic task and wrong for a heuristic one. The LSE meta-analysis across fifty-one studies lands the same way.

    Not quite. Anxiety would degrade both kinds of task. The evidence shows the reward improving one of them, so narrowing rather than stress is the better account.

  5. 5Which of these is on Pink's list of what if-then rewards can do?

    Not quite. That is the finding the whole first section rules out, in an experiment where it made people measurably slower.

    Not quite. The reverse. A contingent reward is a form of control, and being controlled is what autonomy is the absence of.

    Right. One of the seven, alongside extinguishing intrinsic motivation, diminishing performance, crushing creativity, crowding out good behaviour, encouraging cheating and becoming addictive.

  6. 6What is Pink's addition to the fence-painting story?

    Right. The familiar half is rewards turning play into work. The return arrow is the half that is usable, and it is the one nobody quotes.

    Not quite. The drawing studies used children because they are convenient, not because the effect is theirs.

    Not quite. It persists. Children paid to draw drew less afterwards, which is what makes it worth worrying about.

  7. 7Where does the damage from a reward actually sit?

    Right. The same reward handed over afterwards and unannounced does very little harm, because it could not have shaped anything.

    Not quite. Praise promised in advance for a specific outcome is an if-then reward too. The form is what matters, not the currency.

    Not quite. Size is a separate question, and larger has sometimes been worse. What matters here is whether it was contingent and known in advance.

  8. 8What is the goal of paying well, on Pink's account?

    Not quite. That is a pay-as-lever argument, which is the model the book is arguing against for non-routine work.

    Right. Not to make pay motivating — to make it a settled fact so that the other three things have room. Unfair or insufficient pay makes everything else unavailable.

    Not quite. A real effect, and not the claim here. The claim is about removing a distraction rather than sending a message.

  9. 9Which is true of Type I behaviour?

    Not quite. That is a caricature. Baseline pay has to be adequate and fair before intrinsic motivation is available at all.

    Not quite. Explicitly rejected. If it were innate the book would have no practical half.

    Right. Pink is careful about this. Type I people care about pay and recognition; the difference is what the behaviour is built on.

  10. 10What are the four T's?

    Not quite. Trust and training matter but are not the axes. The four are about what you get to decide.

    Right. Naming them is the useful part: it turns a vague good into four separate questions, each of which has an actual answer in a given job.

    Not quite. Targets belong to the goal-setting argument, and tenure is not something autonomy is granted over.

  11. 11On Dweck's account, what does a fixed mindset do to a hard afternoon?

    Right. Which is why it ends the attempt. The same difficulty under a growth mindset is evidence that you are doing the thing that produces ability.

    Not quite. Occasionally, and not reliably. The characteristic response is withdrawal, because effort is read as evidence of insufficient talent.

    Not quite. The interpretation happens during the difficulty, which is what makes it decide whether there is an outcome at all.

  12. 12What does Pink say about how mastery actually feels along the way?

    Right. He is blunt that the flow research does not make this pleasant, which is unusual for the genre and the reason the section is worth keeping.

    Not quite. That is the fixed mindset restated. The difficulty is the mechanism rather than a sign you should have found something easier.

    Not quite. Flow is the good hour, not the decade. Treating it as the normal state is how people conclude they picked the wrong pursuit.

  13. 13Why does Pink call mastery an asymptote?

    Not quite. True and not the claim. The point is the destination is unreachable in principle, not that the last few percent are hard.

    Not quite. That is closer to a fixed-mindset reading. The asymptote applies to everyone regardless of where they start.

    Right. An achievable version would be finished, and a finished pursuit stops pulling. The gap is the subject rather than the curve.

  14. 14What makes a task Goldilocks-shaped?

    Not quite. That is the boredom side of the channel. Effortlessness is the result of the match, not the condition for it.

    Right. And the band moves as you improve, which is why the same job that once produced flow stops fitting.

    Not quite. Importance is not one of the axes. Flow is about the match between challenge and skill, and can happen on trivial tasks.

  15. 15How do you test whether an organisation actually has a purpose?

    Not quite. Profitability is not the question. Purpose maximisation is about what the organisation is organised around, not what it achieves.

    Not quite. That is the thing being tested, not the test. Every organisation has one of those.

    Right. Goals, words and policies. The third is the one usually missing — language and metrics get rewritten long before anyone is trusted with their own target.

  16. 16What did Kasser find about people who chase extrinsic goals?

    Not quite. Not the result, and a much less interesting one. They often hit the targets — the wellbeing did not follow.

    Not quite. Persistence was not the issue. The problem showed up after arrival rather than on the way.

    Right. That last clause is the finding. Attainment does not repair it, which rules out the explanation that they were simply failing.

  17. 17Pink offers a big question and a small one. Which does more work day to day?

    Not quite. It is the memorable half and it is answered once in a life. Useful for direction, useless as a daily practice.

    Right. The famous half is Clare Boothe Luce's one sentence, answered once. The small one is answered tonight and again tomorrow, which is what makes it operational.

    Not quite. Not from this book. Pink's pair is the sentence and the daily comparison against yourself.
