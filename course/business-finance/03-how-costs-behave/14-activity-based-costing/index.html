Activity-based costing, and when it is not worth it

Replace one base with the drivers that actually cause the cost — what that reveals, what it costs to run, and the version that survives.

Tutorial 03 of 05 · 30 minutes

  • Say when ABC is not worth building, and what to do instead

The question

The last tutorial ended with a cross-subsidy and no way to prove it. ABC is the way to prove it, and the reason most companies that built one no longer run it.

Both halves of that sentence are worth understanding, because the second half is what makes the first one usable.

ABC replaces one base with several drivers

The traditional structure is one pool and one base. ABC breaks the pool into several — a pool per activity — and charges each on the driver that causes it.

Setups are caused by production runs, not by units. Order processing is caused by orders, not by revenue. Inspection is caused by inspections. Once each pool is charged on its own driver, a product that triggers many setups and small orders picks up the cost it creates instead of hiding inside a volume average.

That is the whole idea. Everything else is implementation.

The mechanism: resources to activities to products

ABC runs in two stages, and the middle layer is what distinguishes it.

Stage 1. Assign resources — salaries, rent, depreciation — to activities. Half a team’s time goes to processing orders, a third to handling exceptions, the rest to reporting. This is usually the survey step.

Stage 2. Assign activities to cost objects — products, customers, channels — on activity drivers: number of orders, number of exceptions, number of reports.

Traditional costing goes straight from resources to products and has to pick one base for the journey. ABC inserts a layer where the causation actually lives.

What it reveals: the whale curve

Do this for customers rather than products, sort them by profitability, and accumulate. The result is a shape consistent enough to have a name.

Cumulative profit rises steeply, peaks somewhere between 150% and 250% of the reported total, runs flat, and then falls back to 100% as the tail is added. The most profitable fifth of customers generate far more than all the profit; a long tail destroys the difference.

The peak is the number that changes conversations. It says the company is already earning much more than it reports, and giving a chunk of it back to customers it would be better off serving differently — or repricing, or letting go.

Two cautions. Much of the tail’s cost is capacity that does not disappear when the customer does, so firing the tail rarely delivers the peak. And a loss-making customer today may be a large one in three years.

What it costs: a second accounting system

Here is why so few full ABC models are still running.

The general ledger posts itself. It is a by-product of trading — invoices, payroll, payments — and it stays current without anyone deciding to keep it current.

An ABC model does not. It needs activity definitions, time-allocation surveys, driver counts, and someone to redo all of that when the process changes. It is a second system with no transactional source, maintained by goodwill, and goodwill runs out. The classic failure is a model built during a project, believed for eighteen months, and quietly wrong for the four years after that.

Time-driven ABC, the pragmatic retreat

Kaplan’s own answer to that failure, from around 2004, drops the surveys.

Time-driven ABC needs two estimates: the cost per minute of capacity for a department, and the minutes each activity takes. Multiply, and you have the cost of every activity without asking anyone how they spend their week.

Two things fall out for free. Updating means changing a rate or a time estimate, not rerunning a survey. And because you cost the time used rather than allocating the whole department, the difference between capacity supplied and capacity used appears as unused capacity — which is the number the original method hid, since it always allocated 100% of the pool.

For most organisations this is the version worth building, and for many the right answer is smaller still: run it once, as an analysis, learn what the cross-subsidies are, fix the prices, and do not build a system at all.

Worked example: two customers, same revenue

Both buy €200,000 a year at a 30% gross margin, so €60,000 of gross profit each. Service costs are pooled at €400,000 and traditionally charged on revenue, which gives each customer €40,000 and a €20,000 net margin.

Now the activity drivers:

Delivery 160,000 drops 200 40 300

Support calls 2,400 36,000

Net 43,200 (94,000)

Same revenue, same gross margin, and one customer makes €43,200 while the other loses €94,000. Beta orders twenty-two times as often in small quantities and calls fifteen times as much. The revenue-based allocation charged them both €40,000 and reported that both were fine.

The action is not necessarily to fire Beta. It is to raise the minimum order size, move them to a self-service support tier, or reprice — all of which are only visible once the driver counts are in front of you.

Do it

  1. Pick one shared service you consume — support, deployment, procurement. Guess its total annual cost.
  2. Name the two or three activities it actually performs, and the countable thing that triggers each.
  3. Estimate your team’s counts. Multiply through. Compare to whatever recharge you are actually being sent.
  4. Then do the time-driven version: minutes per activity × cost per minute. See whether the two answers agree.

If the answers differ wildly, the recharge base is the reason, and you now have the arithmetic to say so.
