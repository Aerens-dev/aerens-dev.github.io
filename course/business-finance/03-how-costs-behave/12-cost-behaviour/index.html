Fixed, variable, stepped - and why fixed is a claim about a horizon

Three shapes against volume, the two conditions that make “fixed” true, and a crude split that is usually good enough.

Tutorial 01 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Classify a cost by its shape against volume rather than by its name
  • State the horizon and volume range over which a cost is fixed
  • Split a mixed cost into fixed and variable parts from two observations

The question

Someone asks what it costs to serve one more customer. The answer on the report is €340. The answer that matters is either €40 or €12,000, depending on whether that customer is the one that makes you hire another support engineer.

Cost behaviour is the difference between those three numbers.

Three shapes against volume

Plot cost on the vertical and volume on the horizontal, and there are three shapes worth naming.

  • Fixed — flat. Total does not move with volume; cost per unit falls as volume rises, which is where economies of scale come from.
  • Variable — a sloping line through the origin. Total moves with volume; cost per unit is constant.
  • Stepped — flat, then a jump, then flat again.

Notice that only the variable one is a straight line through the origin, and notice that per-unit and total behave oppositely for a fixed cost. Most confusion about cost behaviour is somebody using “cost” to mean per-unit in one sentence and total in the next.

“Fixed” is a claim about a horizon

Rent is fixed. Over a month, certainly. Over a year, yes — the lease runs. Over three years you can break it, sublet, or move somewhere smaller.

So fixed is never a property of a cost. It is a property of a cost relative to a decision horizon, and the honest form of the sentence is “fixed over the next eleven months, which is the remaining lease term”.

This matters because the horizon of the decision is often longer than the horizon someone quoted the cost over. A three-year contract renegotiation should not treat rent as fixed. A question about next month should.

Most fixed costs are actually stepped

Push the volume far enough and every fixed cost breaks. One support engineer handles up to about 40 accounts; the 41st needs a second engineer, and the cost jumps by a whole salary rather than by a fortieth of one.

This is the shape almost everything real takes: a team, a shift, a licence tier, a server, a floor of a building. The step size is the smallest unit you can buy, and the step is why “what does one more cost” has two answers.

Two things follow. The marginal cost of one more unit is usually near zero, right up until it is enormous. And the interesting question about capacity is never how much are we using but how close are we to the next step.

The relevant range is where the model is true

Because of the steps, a linear cost model — fixed plus variable per unit — is only true across a band of volume. That band is the relevant range, and it is set by the observations the model was fitted from.

Inside it, the arithmetic works. Outside it you are extrapolating a line through a region that contains steps nobody measured. A model fitted at 400 to 700 units a month says nothing reliable about 2,000.

Whenever someone hands you a cost per unit, the useful question is what volumes it was derived from.

Mixed costs and the high-low split

Most real cost lines are mixed — a standing charge plus a usage charge, a salaried team plus overtime, a cloud commitment plus on-demand.

The crudest useful way to split one is high-low: take the highest and lowest observed volumes, divide the cost difference by the volume difference to get the variable rate, then back out the fixed part from either point.

It is genuinely crude — two points, both of them extremes, and an outlier at either end wrecks it. Regression is better and takes a spreadsheet minute. But high-low can be done on the back of a meeting agenda, and a rough split you have is worth more than an exact one you will not do.

Worked example: the support team

Six months of support cost against accounts served.

Month Accounts Cost €
January 310 41,200
February 340 42,600
March 395 45,100
April 420 46,300
May 455 61,800
June 470 62,500

High-low on the full range: (62,500 − 41,200) ÷ (470 − 310) = €133 per account. Fixed part = 41,200 − (310 × 133) = −€53, near enough zero.

So the model says: no fixed cost, €133 per account. That is wrong, and the table shows why. Between April and May the cost jumps €15,500 on 35 extra accounts — €443 each — while every other month moves at about €45 each. Somebody was hired in May.

Fit high-low inside each band instead:

Band Variable per account Fixed
Jan–Apr 46 26,940
May–Jun 47 40,415

Now it makes sense: about €46 of genuinely variable cost per account, and a stepped fixed base that went from €26,940 to €40,415 when the fifth engineer started. The relevant range was 310–420, and May left it.

The first answer — €133 an account — is the kind of number that gets used to price a contract. It is the average of two different cost structures and it describes neither.

Do it

  1. Take twelve months of one cost line and the volume driver you think moves it. Plot them. Eyes first, arithmetic second.
  2. Look for a step. If there is one, note what happened that month — a hire, a lease, a tier change.
  3. Run high-low inside each band separately, and write down the relevant range each result applies to.
  4. Then answer the real question: how many more units until the next step?

Step 4 is the number worth having in your head, and almost nobody has it.
