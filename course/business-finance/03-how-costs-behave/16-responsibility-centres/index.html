Cost, profit and investment centres

Three ways to make a unit accountable, the controllability rule almost nobody applies, and the measure that makes managers refuse good projects.

Tutorial 05 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Say which centre type a unit is and what question it can be asked
  • Separate controllable from non-controllable cost on a real report
  • Explain why an ROI target causes a division to reject a project worth taking

The question

A division returning 20% on capital is offered a project returning 15%. The group’s cost of capital is 10%. The division turns it down, and the manager is behaving entirely rationally given how they are measured.

That is not a story about a bad manager. It is a story about a measure, and this tutorial is about how those measures are chosen.

Three kinds of centre, three questions

Responsibility accounting assigns each unit a type, and the type determines what its manager can be asked.

  • Cost centre. Answers for spend. Did you spend what you said you would? Most internal functions.
  • Profit centre. Answers for spend and revenue. Did you earn more than you spent? A business unit with its own customers.
  • Investment centre. Answers for spend, revenue and the capital tied up. Was the capital worth committing? A division with its own balance sheet.

Each type adds a lever and, with it, a duty. You cannot ask a cost centre about return on capital, because it does not control the capital.

Controllability: judge people on what they can move

The principle is simple and honoured mostly in the breach: hold a manager responsible for what they can influence.

A cost centre report typically mixes spend the team chose — headcount, contractors, suppliers — with spend it did not: recharges on a headcount driver, group overhead, currency movements, allocated IT.

The fix is not to hide the second kind. It is real cost and someone should see it. The fix is to report both and measure on one: a controllable subtotal that the conversation is about, and an allocated section below it that is reported for completeness.

Most cost-centre reports do not do this, which is why so much of the monthly conversation is spent on movements nobody in the room caused.

Transfer prices, and why they cause fights

The moment one internal unit sells to another, a price has to be set — and it moves profit between them without changing group profit by a cent.

The options are the usual three. Market price works when a genuine external market exists, and is the cleanest. Cost-plus is common and lets the selling division pass on inefficiency. Negotiated reflects real bargaining and consumes real management time.

The reason the argument gets fierce is precisely that the group is indifferent. Nobody at group level feels the outcome, so nobody arbitrates with any urgency, while both divisions have their bonuses attached to it. This is a design problem, not a personality one — and it is a good reason not to make units into profit centres unless the trading between them is genuinely arm’s length.

ROI, residual income, and the project a manager refuses

Return on investment — profit ÷ capital employed — is the classic investment centre measure, and it has a well-known defect.

A division earning 20% is offered a project earning 15%. Taking it lowers the division’s average to something below 20%, so a manager measured on ROI refuses. The group loses a project earning five points above its cost of capital, because the measure rewards a ratio rather than an amount.

Residual income fixes it: profit − (capital × cost of capital). The project earns 15% against a 10% charge, so its residual income is positive and the manager takes it. Every project above the cost of capital increases the measure, which is exactly the behaviour the group wants. EVA is a proprietary variant of the same idea.

Residual income has its own weakness — it is an absolute, so a large division posts a large number and comparison between divisions is harder. That is a smaller problem than turning down good projects.

Choosing the type is choosing the behaviour

The last idea is the one to take away. Designating a unit is not a reporting decision, it is a behavioural one.

Make a team a cost centre and it will minimise spend — including spend that was worth making. Make it a profit centre and it will chase revenue, including revenue that does not suit the group. Make it an investment centre and it will guard its asset base, including by not investing.

A support function turned into a profit centre starts selling to other departments and charging them for things it used to do freely. That is not a distortion of the design; it is the design working. The question is always whether the behaviour the type produces is the behaviour you wanted.

Worked example: the project the division refuses

A division with €10,000,000 of capital employed earning €2,000,000 of profit. Group cost of capital is 10%.

Before Project alone After
Capital employed 10,000,000 2,000,000 12,000,000
Profit 2,000,000 300,000 2,300,000
ROI 20.0% 15.0% 19.2%

Measured on ROI, the manager’s number falls from 20.0% to 19.2%. They refuse.

Before After
Profit 2,000,000 2,300,000
Capital charge at 10% (1,000,000) (1,200,000)
Residual income 1,000,000 1,100,000

Measured on residual income, the number rises by €100,000 — which is exactly the project’s €300,000 of profit less its €200,000 capital charge. They accept.

The group is €100,000 better off either way. Only the measure differs, and the measure decided the outcome. This is the cleanest example in the course of a number changing a decision rather than describing one, which is what track 7 is entirely about.

Do it

  1. Work out what type your own unit is. Not what it is called — what it is actually asked about at the monthly review.
  2. Take last month’s report and draw a line: controllable above, allocated below. Note what share is below the line.
  3. If your organisation transfers anything internally, find out how the price is set and who set it.
  4. Then the interesting one: name a behaviour your unit exhibits that is a rational response to how it is measured rather than to what is good for the company.

Step 4 is uncomfortable and it is the whole subject. Everyone has an answer.
