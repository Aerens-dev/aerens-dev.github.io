Direct vs indirect, and the overhead allocation problem

Direct means traceable, not important — and everything else has to be charged out on a base somebody chose.

Tutorial 02 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Classify a cost as direct or indirect and say what the test actually is
  • Say how the choice of allocation base changes which product looks profitable
  • Spot a cross-subsidy from the mismatch between cause and base

The question

Your company sells two products. One is simple and sells in volume; the other is fiddly, sells rarely, and needs constant attention. The management accounts show the simple one making a 34% margin and the fiddly one making 8%.

There is a good chance that is backwards, and the reason is a single line in a spreadsheet nobody has looked at in four years.

Direct means traceable, not important

A cost is direct if it can be traced to one cost object — a product, a project, a customer — economically. Steel in the frame. Hours booked to the job.

A cost is indirect if it cannot: the factory roof, the plant manager, the finance department, the ERP licence.

The test is measurability, not size and not importance. The roof costs far more than the steel and it is still the indirect one. “Direct” is not a compliment and “overhead” is not an accusation.

Note also that the classification depends on the cost object. A team lead’s salary is indirect to a product and perfectly direct to a department.

Overhead has to go somewhere

Indirect costs are gathered into a pool and charged out to products on some basis, because a cost that stays in the pool never reaches a price and a business that prices only its direct costs loses money on everything.

So the mechanism is forced. What is not forced is the allocation base — the thing you divide by. Labour hours, machine hours, headcount, floor area, revenue, units, or a flat split.

That choice is made by a person, is rarely revisited, and decides the whole answer.

The allocation base is the whole argument

Take a €480,000 pool and two products. Charge it on labour hours and product A carries €62 a unit while C carries €38. Charge the identical pool on machine hours and A carries €34 while C carries €66.

Nothing in the factory changed. C went from the cheap product to the expensive one because of a column heading.

There is a principle for choosing, and it is the only one that matters: the base should be what causes the cost. If the pool is mostly machine depreciation, power and maintenance, it is caused by machine hours and charging it on labour hours is simply wrong. If the pool is mostly supervision, labour hours are closer.

Most bases in use are not chosen this way. They are chosen because the data was already in the system.

Cross-subsidy is the quiet consequence

When the base does not match the cause, one product carries cost another product caused. This is cross-subsidy, and it has a consistent direction.

Overhead pools are mostly driven by complexity — setups, small runs, special handling, engineering changes, support calls. Bases in common use are mostly driven by volume. So the high-volume simple product absorbs cost created by the low-volume complex one.

Which produces the pattern the whole tutorial started with: the simple product shows a healthy margin it has not earned, the complex one shows a poor margin it does not deserve, and the company responds by pushing the simple product and cutting the price of it, which makes things worse.

One plantwide rate, or a rate per department

The cheapest structure is a single plantwide rate: total overhead divided by total base. One number, easy to maintain, and it averages together departments that behave nothing alike.

Departmental rates give each department its own pool and its own base — machining on machine hours, assembly on labour hours. More work, and it stops the averaging.

The rule of thumb: the more departments differ in what drives their costs, and the more products differ in how much of each department they use, the worse a single rate performs. If everything flows through every department in the same proportion, one rate is fine and anything more is bookkeeping for its own sake.

Worked example: the two products

Overhead pool €480,000. Product A: 8,000 units, 0.5 labour hours each, 0.2 machine hours each. Product C: 1,000 units, 2 labour hours each, 4 machine hours each.

Base Total base units Rate
Labour hours 4,000 + 2,000 = 6,000 €80 per hour
Machine hours 1,600 + 4,000 = 5,600 €85.71 per hour
Product On labour hours On machine hours
A, per unit 0.5 × 80 = 40 0.2 × 85.71 = 17
C, per unit 2 × 80 = 160 4 × 85.71 = 343

C’s allocated overhead more than doubles. If C sells for €400, the labour-hours view leaves plenty of margin and the machine-hours view leaves almost none.

Which is right depends entirely on what is in the €480,000. If it is mostly depreciation, power and maintenance on machines, C is genuinely consuming it and €343 is closer to the truth. A is being made to look expensive by a base that has nothing to do with the cost.

And note the volume asymmetry: A has eight times the units and two-thirds of the labour hours, so under labour-hours allocation A carries €320,000 of the pool. Under machine hours it carries €137,000. That €183,000 swing is invisible in the P&L, which is correct in total either way.

Do it

  1. Find one overhead recharge that lands on your cost centre and ask what its base is. Headcount, floor area, revenue share, or a flat split.
  2. Write one sentence on what actually causes that cost. Then compare.
  3. If they differ, work out roughly which way the cross-subsidy runs: are you paying for someone else’s complexity, or is someone paying for yours?
  4. Then look at your product or service margins and ask whether the lowest-margin one is genuinely the worst, or the one carrying the allocation.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 5 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

  1. 1The factory roof costs more than the steel in the product. Which is the direct cost?

    Not quite. Size has no bearing on it. Direct and indirect describe whether a cost can be traced, not how much of it there is.

    Not quite. Both are real costs of production. Only one of them can be attached to a particular unit without an allocation.

    Right. The test is measurability rather than size. A roof serves everything made under it, so it cannot be traced to one unit.

    Not quite. The steel needs no base at all — it is traced. A base is exactly what the indirect cost requires.

  2. 2Why is overhead charged out to products rather than left in a pool?

    Not quite. Allocating it changes where it appears rather than how large it is. The pool is the same size either way.

    Right. Pricing on direct cost alone loses money on everything, since the pool is real money someone has to recover.

    Not quite. Some overhead does end up in inventory, but the reason for charging it out is to make product costs usable.

    Not quite. Inventory valuation rules absorb production overhead, but plenty of overhead is expensed as incurred instead.

  3. 3What principle should decide the allocation base?

    Not quite. Stability is comfortable and can be badly misleading. The base should track cause even when the result moves about.

    Right. A pool of machine depreciation and power is caused by machine hours, so charging it on labour hours is simply wrong.

    Not quite. That is how most bases are in fact chosen, and it is the reason so many of them are wrong.

    Not quite. Evenness is not a virtue here. If one product causes most of the cost, an even spread misstates both products.

  4. 4Which direction does cross-subsidy usually run, and why?

    Not quite. Age is not what drives it. What matters is whether a product's share of the base matches its share of the cause.

    Not quite. That is the reverse of the usual pattern. Volume-based allocation loads the high-volume line, not the fiddly one.

    Right. Pools are driven by complexity while common bases are driven by volume, so volume absorbs cost complexity created.

    Not quite. Geography enters only if the base happens to correlate with it. The systematic bias is volume against complexity.

  5. 5When is a single plantwide overhead rate good enough?

    Not quite. A small pool limits the damage, which is a reason to care less rather than a reason the single rate is right.

    Not quite. Neither framework prescribes the internal rate structure. This is a management accounting choice, not a reporting one.

    Right. If the mix is the same everywhere there is nothing for departmental rates to distinguish, so the extra work buys nothing.

    Not quite. That is exactly when it performs worst, because one rate averages structures that behave nothing alike.
