Contribution margin and break-even

One model worth memorising, the decision rule it gives you, and the three conditions that put the fixed costs back in.

Tutorial 04 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Compute contribution per unit, break-even volume and margin of safety
  • Use the contribution rule to price a marginal order
  • Name the conditions under which that rule stops being valid

The question

A customer offers €70 a unit for a one-off run of 2,000. Your cost sheet says each unit costs €88. Sales wants to take it, finance says it loses €36,000, and both of them are reading the same number.

If there is one model in this course to keep in your head, it is the one that settles this.

Contribution is what a sale leaves behind

Contribution = price − variable cost. Not price minus full cost. Only the costs that exist because of that sale.

At €100 with €62 of variable cost, each sale leaves €38 behind. That €38 goes towards fixed costs; once fixed costs are covered, every further €38 is profit.

The word is exact and worth using. The sale does not make €38 of profit. It contributes €38 towards a pile that has to be filled before there is any profit at all.

Break-even is fixed cost over contribution

Fill the pile and you break even. So:

Break-even volume = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit.

€380,000 of fixed cost at €38 a unit is 10,000 units. In revenue terms, divide fixed cost by the contribution ratio — €38/€100 = 38% — giving €1,000,000.

The margin of safety is how far current volume sits above that: at 13,000 units you are 3,000 above break-even, a 23% margin of safety. It is the honest answer to “how far can sales fall before we are in trouble”, and it is a better question than most of the ones asked in a board pack.

Operating leverage cuts both ways

The higher the share of fixed costs, the more a change in revenue is amplified into profit. That amplification is operating leverage, and it equals total contribution ÷ profit.

A business with €380,000 fixed and €494,000 contribution has profit of €114,000 and leverage of 4.3. A 10% revenue rise becomes a 43% profit rise. A 10% fall becomes a 43% fall.

This is why software companies look extraordinary on the way up and alarming on the way down, and why a business that outsources everything is duller in both directions. Neither is better; they are different bets on volume, and the leverage number tells you which bet you are holding.

For a marginal decision, ignore fixed costs

Back to the €70 order. Full cost is €88, of which €62 is variable and €26 is allocated fixed overhead.

The €26 is paid whether or not you accept the order. It is not caused by the decision, so it does not belong in the decision. What the order changes is €62 of cost and €70 of revenue: +€8 a unit, €16,000 on the run.

Finance’s €36,000 loss is the difference between the price and an allocation. Sales is right, for the right reason, which is not the reason sales usually gives.

The general rule: a decision should be evaluated on the cash flows it changes. Allocated fixed cost is, by construction, not one of them.

Where the rule breaks

The rule is right and it is narrow. Three conditions void it.

Capacity. If the run consumes the last of your capacity, accepting it means turning something else away. The cost of the order is then €62 plus the contribution you gave up — the opportunity cost — and that can easily exceed €70.

Price leakage. If existing customers learn about €70, the price is no longer a one-off. The relevant cost includes the margin lost on everything else you sell.

It is not really one-off. Marginal pricing applied repeatedly is just pricing, and a business that prices at variable cost never covers its fixed costs. Every one of these is agreed as an exception and then repeated.

The rule holds for a genuinely marginal decision with spare capacity and no spillover. Check all three before using it, because the failure mode is not a small error.

Worked example: taking the order

Current position, before the special order:
Volume 13,000 units
Price 100
Variable cost 62
Contribution per unit 38
Total contribution 494,000
Fixed costs 380,000
Profit 114,000

Break-even is 380,000 ÷ 38 = 10,000 units. Margin of safety is 3,000 units, or 23%. Operating leverage is 494,000 ÷ 114,000 = 4.3.

Now the order: 2,000 units at €70, capacity available.
Contribution per unit 70 − 62 = 8
Extra contribution 16,000
Extra fixed cost 0
New profit 130,000

Take it. Profit rises 14% on a price that is 30% below list.

Now change one thing: capacity is 14,000 units, so 1,000 of the order displaces 1,000 units of normal sales.
Contribution gained, 2,000 × 8 16,000
Contribution lost, 1,000 × 38 (38,000)
Net (22,000)

Refuse it. The order did not change; the capacity did. Same arithmetic, opposite answer, and the only new input is whether the factory was full.

Do it

  1. For one product or service you know, write down price, variable cost per unit, and total fixed cost. Getting the variable/fixed split right is most of the work — go back to the high-low tutorial if it is mixed.
  2. Compute contribution per unit, break-even volume, and margin of safety.
  3. Compute operating leverage. Then ask what a 10% revenue drop does to profit, and whether anyone in the business would describe that as the answer.
  4. Then find a discount your company has agreed recently and test it against the three conditions. Spare capacity? No leakage? Genuinely one-off?

Most agreed discounts fail at least one, which is worth knowing before the next one is proposed.
