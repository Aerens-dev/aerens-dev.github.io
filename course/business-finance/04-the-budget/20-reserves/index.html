Baseline, contingency reserve, management reserve

The PMI stack, who may spend each layer, why releasing the top one is a change rather than a drawdown, and what a project with no contingency really has.

Tutorial 04 of 06 · 30 minutes

  • Build the stack from work package estimates up to project budget
  • Say who authorises each reserve and on what grounds
  • Explain what happens to variance reporting when a reserve is released

The question

A project is €180,000 over its estimates and the sponsor says “that is what the reserve is for”. Whether that is true depends on which reserve, who identified the risk, and whether releasing it counts as spending money or as changing the plan.

Those are three separate questions and they have three different answers.

The PMI stack, bottom to top

The PMBOK Guide builds a project budget in layers, and it is worth learning in that order because each layer answers a different question.

  • Work package estimates. What the work is expected to cost, aggregated up through control accounts.
  • Plus contingency reserve. Money for identified risks.
  • = Cost baseline. The time-phased plan. This is what performance is measured against, and it is what earned value uses.
  • Plus management reserve. Money for unidentified risks.
  • = Project budget. The total authorised funding.

The line that matters most is the middle one. The cost baseline includes contingency and excludes management reserve — so a project reporting on budget is reporting against a figure that already has its known risks funded.

Contingency is for known unknowns, and it is yours

Contingency reserve funds risks that were identified, assessed and written into the risk register. The steel price might rise; the integration might need a second round; the permit might be delayed.

You do not know which of them will happen, and you do know the list. That is what “known unknown” means, and it is why the amount can be estimated — from the register, by expected value or by a Monte Carlo run over the risk ranges, which is what AACE’s contingency practices are about.

Management reserve is for what is not on the list. Not a bigger version of contingency, a different category: the risks nobody thought of, which by construction cannot be estimated from a register.

Management reserve is not the project manager’s to spend

The authority follows the category.

Contingency sits inside the baseline, and the project manager can normally draw it down when an identified risk occurs, following whatever the risk management plan says. Most organisations require the risk owner to show that the risk was on the register and that the drawdown matches the planned response — sensible, since otherwise contingency becomes a slush fund.

Management reserve sits outside the baseline and belongs to the sponsor or the change control board. Getting it requires a formal request, and the request is answerable in a way a contingency drawdown is not.

So “that is what the reserve is for” is only an answer once you know which one. If the €180,000 is a risk that was on the register, it is a drawdown. If it is something nobody foresaw, it is a request to the sponsor.

Releasing management reserve is a change, not a drawdown

Here is the consequence people miss, and it matters for how the project reads afterwards.

Management reserve is released into the cost baseline, through a change request. The baseline gets bigger. Which means the line the project is measured against moves, and next month’s variance is calculated against the new, larger figure.

That is correct — the project genuinely has more authorised money and it would be meaningless to keep measuring against a baseline that no longer reflects the funded plan. But it also means a project that has drawn management reserve twice can be reporting a healthy variance while having consumed considerably more than it originally asked for.

The number that survives this is the project budget, and the question worth asking is not “are we on baseline” but “how much of the reserve is left”.

A project with no contingency has a hidden one

Finally, the failure mode that produces most of the trouble.

Contingency is visible and therefore politically vulnerable. It looks like padding, and an executive under pressure cuts it — “if you need it, come and ask for it.”

What happens next is not that the project runs without contingency. It is that every individual estimate quietly acquires its own margin, because the people producing them are not fools. The contingency is still there; it is now distributed, invisible, unquantified, and impossible to release when the risk it was covering does not occur.

That is strictly worse in three ways: nobody can say how much there is, it cannot be reallocated to where the risk actually landed, and it never comes back. A visible contingency line is a control. A hidden one is only a cushion.

Worked example: where the €180,000 goes

A project with estimates of €2,400,000, contingency of €240,000, and management reserve of €180,000.

Layer €
Work package estimates 2,400,000
Contingency reserve 240,000
Cost baseline 2,640,000
Management reserve 180,000
Project budget 2,820,000

Now three overruns, and each is treated differently.

€90,000, supplier price rise, on the risk register. A contingency drawdown. The baseline does not move; contingency falls to €150,000; the project reports on baseline. Nothing needs a sponsor.

€180,000, a regulatory change nobody anticipated. Not on the register, so not contingency. A change request to the sponsor for the management reserve. If approved, the baseline becomes €2,820,000, the reserve becomes zero, and the project reports on baseline again — against a bigger baseline.

A further €60,000, cause unclear. There is nothing left. This is a request for new funding, which goes wherever the original €2,820,000 was approved, and it is a very different meeting.

Notice what the reporting says at each stage. After the first two the project is “on budget” both times. After the second, it has consumed its entire reserve and has no protection left, and the variance report does not say so. The line that would have said so is reserve remaining, and it is worth adding to any project report that lacks it.

Do it

  1. For any project you can see, find its estimates, its contingency and its management reserve as three separate figures. If they are not separable, that is the finding.
  2. Work out what percentage contingency is of the estimates. Under 5% on anything novel is optimistic; over 30% usually means the estimates are weak.
  3. Find out how much reserve has been consumed and how much of the schedule is left. Half the reserve gone at a quarter of the way through is the signal.
  4. Then ask what happens in your organisation when contingency is cut in review — and whether the estimates that follow are larger.

Step 4 is how you find out whether your organisation has visible contingency or hidden contingency.

Check yourself

    Not quite. It is the other way round. Contingency belongs to the project and management reserve is held above it.

    Not quite. Relative size varies with the project and says nothing about the categories. The difference is whether the risk was foreseen.

  3. 3An unforeseen regulatory change costs €180,000. What does the project manager do?

    Not quite. Contingency funds risks that were identified in advance. An unforeseen event falls outside what it was priced for.

    Not quite. The baseline allows for identified risks through contingency. Absorbing an unforeseen cost hides it rather than funding it.

    Right. It was not on the register, so it is not a contingency drawdown. Management reserve is released by the sponsor or the board.

  4. 4What happens to variance reporting after management reserve is released?

    Not quite. It would if the reserve were simply spent. Release moves it into the baseline, which is what makes it a change.

    Not quite. Reporting continues against the baseline. What changed is that the baseline is now larger than it was.

  5. 5An executive removes the contingency line, saying to ask if it is needed. What follows?

    Not quite. That is the intended reading and rarely what happens. The people producing estimates protect themselves instead.

    Not quite. The headline total falls; the money reappears inside the estimates. Net cost usually rises rather than falls.
