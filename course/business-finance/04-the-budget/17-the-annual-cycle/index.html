The annual cycle: who asks, who decides, when the numbers harden

Five months, four phases, three roles — and a number that stops moving about six weeks before anybody votes on it.

Tutorial 01 of 06 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Place the current month in the budget cycle and say what is still open
  • Name who sets the format, who decides the total, and who is bound
  • Say why a request made in August outperforms a better one made in November

The question

In November you put together a careful case for €90,000 of tooling. It is costed, it has a payback, and it is refused. In August a colleague mentioned a vaguer idea in a corridor and it is in the plan.

Nobody was being unfair. The two requests arrived at different points in a process, and the process is the subject of this tutorial.

Four phases, about five months

A calendar-year budget typically runs like this.

  • June — guidance. Finance issues the assumptions: inflation, headcount policy, salary increase, FX rates, and usually a growth or cost target.
  • September — submissions. Every cost centre submits, in the format finance specified, using those assumptions.
  • October — negotiation. The submissions total more than the target. This phase is where the budget is actually decided.
  • December — approval. The board votes. The plan is loaded into the system.

The dates shift by industry and by whether the year is a calendar one, but the shape holds, and so does the length: this is a five-month process with about three weeks of real decision-making in it.

The numbers harden earlier than anyone admits

Follow one line through the cycle and it goes through three states.

Open in June, when nobody has written anything down. Negotiable in September, when your submission is one input among many. Settled in practice by late October, once the executive has agreed the total and the allocation between units.

The board’s approval in December is real governance and it is almost never where a line changes. By then the number has been fixed for six weeks and is being presented, not decided.

The practical consequence: if you find out in November that your budget is wrong, you are not early — you are four weeks late.

Who asks, who decides, who is bound

Three roles, and they are usually three different people.

Finance asks. They own the calendar, the template, the assumptions, and the consolidation. They do not decide your number, which is why arguing with finance about the size of your budget is almost always the wrong conversation.

The executive decides the total and its split between units. This is the decision, and it happens in a room you are probably not in.

You are bound by the result. Your name goes against it, you explain the variances, and it is the basis on which your year is assessed.

That separation is the source of most budget cynicism, and it is not a conspiracy — a total has to be decided somewhere above the units that sum into it. What it does mean is that influence has to be applied before the decision, which is the last idea here.

The submission you make is not the budget you get

Submissions almost always exceed the target, because every unit is padded and because the target was set to be difficult. What comes back is a cut.

The important thing about that cut is how it is made. It is very rarely made line by line by somebody who has read your justification. It is a percentage, applied to the total, and pushed down.

Two consequences follow, and both are rational responses to the mechanism rather than to the merits. First, submitting an unpadded number means absorbing the full percentage cut from a figure that already had no slack. Second, the justification you wrote may never be read by the person applying the reduction.

This is the machinery that produces padding, and track 7 is about what it does to an organisation over time. For now it is enough to know it exists.

The calendar is the lever

Put the previous four ideas together and you get the only genuinely actionable one in this tutorial.

A request that arrives before the total is set helps to shape that total. It is in the assumptions, it is in the submission, and it is part of what the executive is deciding between.

A request that arrives after competes for whatever is left, against a process that has already balanced. It needs to be better than something already in the plan, and someone has to be willing to reopen a settled allocation.

So the discipline is to know when your organisation’s guidance phase is, and to be inside it. A mediocre case in August genuinely does beat a good one in November, and that is a fact about process rather than about quality.

Worked example: the €90,000 of tooling

August version November version
Where it lands Inside the submission A request against a settled plan
Competes with Other lines in your own submission Everything already approved
Who can say yes You, by prioritising within your ask The executive, by reopening the total
Approval needed None beyond the normal cycle A supplement, or a transfer from someone
Realistic odds Good Poor unless something has failed

The August route costs you something real: €90,000 of your submission that has to come from somewhere else in your own ask, and you make that trade yourself.

The November route costs somebody else something, and requires a person senior enough to take it from them. That is a much larger favour to ask for the same €90,000.

Note that this is not an argument for asking for everything in August. A submission stuffed with everything anyone might want is exactly what invites the percentage cut. It is an argument for knowing which few things genuinely need to be in, and getting those in.

Do it

  1. Find out your organisation’s actual budget calendar. Not the published one — ask finance when guidance goes out and when the executive review happens.
  2. Mark two dates in your own calendar: four weeks before guidance, and one week before submission.
  3. Between now and the first date, keep a running list of things that will need money next year. It does not need to be good; it needs to exist in August.
  4. Then find last year’s submission and last year’s final budget, and work out the percentage between them.

Step 4 tells you what your organisation’s standard cut is, which is the single most useful number for next year’s submission.
