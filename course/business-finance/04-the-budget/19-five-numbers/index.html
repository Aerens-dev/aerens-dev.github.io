Business Finance · The budget

Approved, control, committed, accrued, actual

Five different figures for one cost centre, the funnel that connects them, and the fact that one of the five names has no standard meaning at all.

Tutorial 03 of 06 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Name all five numbers for one cost centre and say how they differ
  • Use committed spend as an early warning rather than waiting for actuals
  • Establish which definition of “control budget” your organisation uses

The question

Someone says “we have €200,000 left in the budget”. Five people in the room hear five different numbers, and at least three of them are right.

This is the tutorial that separates them.

Five numbers, one funnel

For a single cost centre in a single year, all of these exist at once.

  • Approved — what was signed off. €520,000.
  • Control — what you may actually spend. €480,000.
  • Committed — orders raised, money not yet gone. €310,000.
  • Accrued — delivered, cost recognised, invoice may not have arrived. €240,000.
  • Actual — posted in the ledger. €205,000.

They descend, and they get more certain as they descend. Approved is a permission; actual is a fact. Everything in between is a claim of decreasing optimism about money that has not yet moved.

“How much is left” has five answers: €315,000, €275,000, €170,000, €240,000 short of nothing, or €315,000 against actuals — and the useful one depends entirely on what the question is for.

Approved is not released

The board approving €520,000 is an authorisation. It is not the same as finance loading €520,000 into your cost centre.

Very commonly a portion is held: released in tranches, held pending a performance condition, or simply kept back centrally as a buffer against the group’s own target. You are told your budget is €480,000 and the difference is not lost — it is somewhere above you, and it may arrive in Q3.

This is the single most common cause of a manager and a finance business partner disagreeing about the same cost centre, and it is resolved by asking one question: is this the approved figure or the released one?

Committed spend is the number that warns you first

Committed is money you have promised: purchase orders raised, contracts signed, work ordered. No invoice, no accrual, nothing in the P&L yet — and in many organisations, nothing in the report you are shown.

It is also the only leading indicator you have. Actuals tell you where you were two months ago. Commitments tell you where you are going, because they are obligations you can no longer easily reverse.

A cost centre whose actuals are at 40% of budget in June and whose commitments are at 95% is not comfortable. It is fully spent and does not know it yet. If your reporting does not show commitments, that is the single most valuable thing to ask for.

Accrued sits between committed and actual

Track 2 covered what an accrual is. Here is where it sits in this sequence.

A purchase moves through four states: the order is raised (committed), the work is delivered (accrued), the invoice is posted (actual), and the invoice is paid (cash). Only the last one is money moving.

The accrual matters here because it is what makes your budget report honest at month end. Without it, work delivered in June with an invoice arriving in August makes June look underspent and August look catastrophic. With it, June carries the cost and the variance is real.

Which is why the day-2 accrual request is not administrative box-ticking: it is what makes the difference between actual and committed mean something.

“Control budget” is not standardised — find out which one they mean

Now the term that sent us here, and the honest answer about it.

There is no single definition. The PMBOK Guide does not use “control budget”; it defines a cost baseline and a project budget. AACE’s terminology standard 10S-90 treats control baseline as a synonym for baseline and does not give “control budget” a separate entry. It is an organisational term, and it is used to mean at least three different things:

  • the baseline plus contingency — the figure a project manager may commit against, excluding management reserve;
  • what finance has released — the approved figure less anything held back, which is the sense most common in annual opex budgeting;
  • the whole approved figure, used loosely as a synonym for “the budget”.

None of these is wrong. They are different local conventions, and two people using different ones will disagree at length without ever discovering why.

So the first question in any new organisation is not what the control budget is. It is which of these your control budget means — and the reliable way to find out is to ask what number a purchase order is validated against.

Worked example: reading the same cost centre five ways

Cost centre 4200 at the end of June. Approved €520,000; control €480,000.

€ What it is
Approved 520,000 Board authorisation
Control 480,000 Released by finance
Committed 310,000 POs raised, mostly a 12-month contract
Accrued 240,000 Delivered, including €35,000 not yet invoiced
Actual 205,000 Posted in the ledger

Five different answers to “how much is left”:

Question being asked Calculation €
How much have we spent? Actual 205,000
What does the P&L show? Accrued 240,000
What can I still commit? Control − committed 170,000
What is unspent on paper? Control − actual 275,000
What did the board give us? Approved 520,000

The dangerous one is the fourth. €275,000 looks like plenty and it is the figure most standard reports show. But €105,000 of the committed spend has neither been delivered nor invoiced, so the genuinely uncommitted amount is €170,000 — and if the €310,000 commitment includes a contract running to December, most of that is already gone too.

The right number to manage against is control less committed, and it is frequently the one number not on the report.

Do it

  1. For your own cost centre, write down all five figures. Some will take a conversation to obtain, which is itself the finding.
  2. Establish which of the three meanings your organisation attaches to “control budget”. Ask what a purchase order is validated against.
  3. Compute control less committed. Compare it to the “remaining budget” figure on your monthly report and see whether they agree.
  4. Then find one commitment running beyond the year end and check whether it is in this year’s figure, next year’s, or both.

Step 4 finds double-counted or missing multi-year commitments, which is where budget surprises in Q4 usually come from.
