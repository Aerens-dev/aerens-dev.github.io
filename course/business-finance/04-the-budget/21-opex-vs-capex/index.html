Opex vs capex, and the behaviour it drives

One distinction, two governance paths, an eightfold difference in year-one profit, and the reason the cloud changed how software spending gets approved.

Tutorial 05 of 06 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Apply the future-benefit test to classify a piece of spend
  • Say what the same €400,000 does to year-one profit under each treatment
  • Name the two opposite pressures that distort the classification

The question

You have €140,000 left in a capital budget and a hiring freeze on operating spend. You need two engineers for six months. The money is right there and you cannot touch it.

That is not bureaucratic obstruction. It is the distinction this tutorial is about, and it drives more behaviour than any other line in a budget.

The distinction: consumed now, or an asset that lasts

Operating expenditure is consumed in the period it is incurred: salaries, rent, travel, licences, contractors.

Capital expenditure buys something future periods will still benefit from: machinery, buildings, vehicles, and — with conditions — software.

The test is future benefit. Will periods after this one get something out of it? If yes, the cost belongs to those periods too, which is the matching principle from track 2 applied to something long-lived, and depreciation is the mechanism.

Two engineers for six months produce work whose benefit may well outlast the year. That does not make their salaries capital: the test is applied to the asset created, under conditions IAS 38 sets tightly, and general staff cost is not one.

Different pots, different approvers, different scrutiny

The accounting distinction produces a governance one, and in day-to-day terms the governance is what you actually feel.

Opex is approved by the cost centre owner within the annual budget. It is reviewed monthly, it is granular, and nobody signs a paper for a €4,000 invoice.

Capex goes through a business case with a payback or an NPV, is approved by a capital committee or a board, and is tracked against the specific project it was authorised for.

And the pots do not swap. Capital budget cannot pay salaries, because the authorisation was for an asset and the accounting would be wrong even if the governance allowed it. This is why the €140,000 is untouchable — and it is worth knowing that it is a real constraint rather than an unhelpful policy.

What it does to the P&L in year one

€400,000 spent, one week, one bank transfer. Two treatments.

As opex, this year’s profit falls €400,000.

As capex on an eight-year life, this year’s profit falls by one year’s depreciation — €50,000 — and the other €350,000 sits on the balance sheet.

Eight times the difference in reported profit, from a classification decision, with identical cash. This is the mechanism behind everything in the last section of this tutorial, and it is also why the distinction is one of the more frequently tested areas in an audit.

The cloud moved a lot of capex into opex

Buying servers was capital: a board paper, a five-year life, a depreciation schedule. Renting the same capacity is operating: a monthly invoice, no asset, no committee.

The accounting change is small. The governance change is enormous.

Starting became easy — an engineer with a corporate card can commit spend that would once have needed a capital paper. Stopping became hard, because nothing forces a review the way a five-year asset does, and nobody is depreciating anything that prompts a question. This is a large part of why FinOps exists as a discipline at all, and it is track 8’s subject.

There is also a real financial effect worth naming: rented capacity keeps spending inside operating profit, so it depresses EBITDA where owned kit would have sat below it in depreciation. Two companies with identical infrastructure can report materially different EBITDA depending on which they chose.

The gaming, in both directions

Because the classification moves reported profit, there is pressure on it — and the pressure runs both ways depending on what is scarce.

Profit under pressure → capitalise more. Development costs, implementation work, anything with a defensible argument that it creates an asset. Legitimate in many cases, and the boundary is genuinely a judgement, which is exactly what makes it available.

Capital budget exhausted, opex available → treat it as a service. Buy the same capability as a subscription rather than a purchase, and it lands in the other pot.

Both are decided by a code chosen when the order is raised, months before anyone sees a report — the same leverage point as tutorial 05. This is why external auditors sample capitalised costs, and why a sudden increase in the capitalisation rate is a question worth asking rather than a trend to note.

Worked example: the same capability, three ways

A team needs a data platform. Three routes, each costing about €400,000 over five years.

Build in-house Buy a licence Rent a service
Cash profile 400k over 9 months 400k up front 80k a year
Classification Partly capex under IAS 38 Capex Opex
Year 1 P&L hit ~60k 80k 80k
Approval Capital paper Capital paper Cost centre
On the balance sheet Yes Yes No
Reversible in year 2 No No Yes

Three observations.

The year-1 P&L hits are close, which is the point people miss when they argue about capex versus opex on profit grounds alone. Spreading a purchase over five years lands in the same place as paying a fifth of it annually.

The cash profiles are not close at all. Build and buy need €400,000 now; rent needs €80,000. For a business that is short of cash, that is the whole decision, and it has nothing to do with the classification.

The reversibility differs completely. Rent can be stopped in year 2 with no write-off. Build and buy leave an asset that has to be impaired if abandoned, which is a cost and an admission.

The classification question is real, but it is usually the third most important thing in the decision, behind cash and behind reversibility.

Do it

  1. Find your organisation’s capitalisation threshold — the amount below which everything is expensed regardless. It is usually between €500 and €5,000.
  2. Find the accounting policy for internally developed software. Note which phases are capitalised and which are not.
  3. Take one project you know and split its cost into what would be capitalised and what would not, under that policy.
  4. Then ask what the year-one P&L difference is between your split and treating the whole thing as opex.

Step 4 is the number a finance business partner will already have, and knowing it before the meeting changes the meeting.
