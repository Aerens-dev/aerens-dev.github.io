Budget change control, and what a re-baseline destroys

Four ways a budget moves, in increasing order of who has to agree — and the one that makes a problem disappear from the report without solving it.

Tutorial 06 of 06 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Pick the right mechanism for a change and know whose signature it needs
  • Say what a re-baseline does to variance history and why that is the point
  • Recognise when a re-baseline is legitimate and when it is concealment

The question

You need €40,000 you do not have. There are four ways to get it, they need four different people to agree, and the one that is easiest to obtain is the one that does the most damage to everybody’s ability to see what is happening.

Four ways a budget changes

In increasing order of how much authority they need:

  • Reallocate — move money between lines inside one cost centre. Total unchanged.
  • Transfer — move it between two cost centres. Group total unchanged.
  • Supplement — new money from above. Group total rises.
  • Re-baseline — the plan itself is replaced.

The first three change where money sits. The fourth changes what the organisation considers the plan to have been, which is a different kind of act and deserves the section it gets at the end.

Reallocation within a centre is usually yours

Take €20,000 out of travel and put it into contractors. Same cost centre, same total, different account codes.

Because the total does not move, most organisations let the owner do this themselves up to a threshold — often something like 10% of a line or a fixed euro amount, above which finance is notified or has to agree.

Two limits worth knowing. It usually cannot cross the opex/capex boundary, for the reasons in the last tutorial. And it usually cannot touch payroll, because headcount is controlled separately from the money that pays for it — a distinction that surprises people, and exists because a permanent hire is a multi-year commitment made against a single-year budget.

A transfer needs the person losing it to agree

Between two cost centres, the group total is still unchanged — but one owner is now €40,000 worse off and has to sign.

That makes it a negotiation rather than an administrative act, and it has a predictable dynamic: whoever gives budget up is judged on a smaller number for the rest of the year, and they remember. Transfers are the currency of a lot of inter-team goodwill, and the giver rarely volunteers twice.

The version worth watching is a transfer agreed mid-year to cover an overspend somewhere else. It solves the overspend and it moves the variance onto somebody who did nothing wrong, which is a real cost even though no group number changed.

A supplement is new money, so the original approver decides

A supplement takes nothing from anyone. Nobody objects on their own behalf, and the group total goes up.

Which is exactly why it is the hardest to get. The total was approved by the board or the executive as a total, and raising it means going back to them. Supplements are usually reserved for genuine external change — an acquisition, a regulatory requirement, a contract nobody knew about — and they generally arrive with conditions attached.

The tell that you are being managed rather than funded: a supplement offered as a transfer from central contingency. That is not new money; it is somebody else’s reserve, and it will be remembered as such.

A re-baseline destroys the variance history

The fourth mechanism is different in kind. A re-baseline does not move money — it replaces the plan with a new one, usually the current forecast.

The immediate effect is that the accumulated variance disappears. A project €400,000 over its original plan, re-baselined to the current forecast, is on plan. Nothing has been repaid and nothing has got cheaper. The line that showed the overspend has been moved to where the spend is.

That is sometimes exactly right. If the scope genuinely changed, or the original plan was built on assumptions that no longer hold, measuring against it produces a variance that carries no information and distracts from the real question.

It is also the single most effective way to make a problem stop appearing in a report, and the two cases look identical from the outside. Three questions separate them:

  • Did the scope change, or only the cost? Scope change justifies it; cost overrun on unchanged scope does not.
  • Is the original baseline still visible anywhere? A legitimate re-baseline keeps the original as a reference. A concealing one does not.
  • How many times? Once is a plan meeting reality. Three times is a reporting strategy.

Worked example: finding €40,000

Cost centre 4200 needs €40,000 more for contractors in September.

Route Who agrees Group total What it costs you
Reallocate from travel You, under threshold Unchanged Something you wanted
Transfer from 4300 Both owners Unchanged Goodwill, once
Supplement Executive +40,000 A hard conversation
Re-baseline the plan Finance and sponsor Unchanged on paper The variance history

Take the first that works, in that order. The ordering is not about difficulty — it is that each step outward costs someone else more, and the last one costs everyone the ability to see what happened.

Now the arithmetic that makes the last one attractive. Suppose the centre is already €25,000 over at the end of August and needs €40,000 more.

Original plan After re-baseline
Budget to date 320,000 345,000
Actual to date 345,000 345,000
Variance (25,000) 0
Full-year budget 480,000 545,000

The re-baseline solves both problems at once — the historic overspend and the future shortfall — with one act and no negotiation with any peer. It is a genuinely tempting option and it costs €65,000 of visibility rather than €40,000 of anyone’s budget.

The test to apply to yourself: if the scope has not changed, you are not re-baselining a plan. You are editing the record of what you said you would spend.

Do it

  1. Find your organisation’s reallocation threshold. Ask finance what you can move without asking.
  2. Find out whether payroll and capex are ring-fenced from reallocation. They usually are, and people usually find out at the worst moment.
  3. Look at any project running more than a year and ask how many times it has been re-baselined, and whether the original baseline is still reported.
  4. Then, for the last budget change you were involved in, name which of the four it was and who bore the cost of it.

Step 3 takes one question and it is the most informative thing you can ask about a long-running project.
