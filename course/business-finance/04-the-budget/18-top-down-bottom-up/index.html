Top-down, bottom-up, and the negotiation in between

Two starting points that never agree, a gap closed by bargaining rather than analysis, and both sides adjusting in advance for the other's adjustment.

Tutorial 02 of 06 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Say what each direction starts from and what it is good at
  • Explain why both a submission and a target are pre-adjusted numbers
  • Choose the right direction for a given kind of budget line

The question

The submissions add up to €14.2m. The target is €12.5m. Nobody has done anything wrong and there is no analysis that resolves the difference, because the two numbers were not produced by the same kind of process.

Two directions, two starting points

Top-down starts from a number the business needs: a margin the market expects, a cash constraint, a cost reduction the board has committed to. It is divided downward.

Bottom-up starts from the work: headcount, contracts, planned projects, run rates. It is added upward.

The top-down number is a requirement. The bottom-up number is an estimate. Those are different kinds of claim, and the disagreement between them is not a mistake in either. It is information — it says the plan as currently conceived does not fit the constraint.

The gap is closed by negotiation, not analysis

There is no calculation that turns €14.2m into €12.5m. Something has to be given up, and choosing what is a judgement about priorities.

In principle that judgement is made jointly, informed by the submissions. In practice the settlement sits nearer the target than the midpoint, most years, for a straightforward reason: the target holder has more authority than the submitter and does not need agreement to impose it.

That is worth naming plainly rather than pretending the process is analytical. Knowing you are in a negotiation changes how you prepare for it — you go in knowing which of your lines you would give up, rather than defending all of them equally and having the choice made for you.

Padding and stretch: both sides adjust first

Because the cut is expected, submissions are padded. Because the padding is expected, targets are stretched. Both adjustments are made before either number is exchanged.

The result is that neither figure is anyone’s honest estimate of anything. The submission is an estimate plus a margin sized to survive an expected reduction. The target is a requirement minus an allowance for expected padding.

Everyone involved knows this, which is the strangest part. It is a stable equilibrium that nobody chose and nobody can unilaterally leave: the first person to submit an honest number gets cut anyway and misses their budget all year.

Jensen’s argument in track 7 is that this is not a cultural failing but a predictable output of tying pay to a negotiated target.

Iterative budgeting is both, several times

Most organisations do not pick a direction. They run both, two or three times, and call it iterative.

Pass one: the target comes down and the submissions go up. Pass two: the gap is named and both sides move part way. Pass three: whatever remains is imposed with less discussion, usually under time pressure because the board date is fixed.

Iteration genuinely improves the number — the second pass is better informed than the first. What it does not do is change the character of the process, and the third pass is where most of the resentment is generated, because by then there is no time to argue and the reductions are least considered.

Which direction suits which line

The choice is not ideological, and the right answer differs line by line.

Top-down is right when the constraint is real. If there is €12.5m of cash and no more, no amount of bottom-up estimating changes that, and a process that pretends otherwise wastes two months before imposing the answer anyway.

Bottom-up is right when nobody upstairs knows the cost. New work, unfamiliar technology, a first-of-a-kind project — a top-down number for these is a guess with authority attached, which is worse than a guess.

The usable rule: use top-down for the total when the constraint binds, and bottom-up for the composition. Argue about what you do within a fixed number rather than about the number, unless the number makes the work impossible — in which case say so once, plainly, in September.

Worked example: closing €1.7m

Submissions €14.2m against a €12.5m target.

Approach What it produces
Flat 12% cut across all units Fast, and takes most from whoever padded least
Cut the three lowest-priority programmes Slow, contested, and defensible afterwards
Freeze hiring for six months Concentrated on one input, easy to reverse
Push €1.7m of spend into next year Solves this budget and not the problem

The flat cut is what usually happens, and its defect is precise: it is proportional to what people asked for rather than to what they need, so it rewards padding and punishes accuracy. Do it three years running and every submission is padded.

The programme cut is the one that is actually a decision. It is slower and it generates a loser, which is why it is avoided.

Now the arithmetic that makes the flat cut seductive. A unit that padded its €2.0m submission by 15% is really asking for €1.74m; after a 12% cut it gets €1.76m and is fine. A unit that submitted an honest €2.0m gets €1.76m and is €240,000 short. Same cut, opposite outcomes, and the second unit learns something for next year.

Do it

  1. Find out whether your organisation starts from a target or from submissions. Ask what the guidance said about growth or cost — that is the top-down signal.
  2. Estimate your own padding. Be honest with yourself about the number; you do not have to tell anyone.
  3. Rank your submission lines by what you would give up first. Write the ranking down before the negotiation, not during it.
  4. Then decide the one line that would make the work genuinely undeliverable if cut, and prepare to say that about that line only.

Step 4 is the whole of budget negotiation strategy. Saying it about everything is the same as saying it about nothing.
