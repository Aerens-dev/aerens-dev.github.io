Cost per inference: a P&L for one AI feature

The unit is a request, output dominates, a flat price against usage-driven cost has a break-even user, and four levers in order of effect.

Tutorial 04 of 04 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Build the cost of one request including retrieval and retries
  • Find the break-even usage level for a flat-priced feature
  • Rank the four cost levers and say which is usually worth the rest

The question

A feature ships. It is popular. Six weeks later somebody notices the model bill has quadrupled and asks what the feature costs per user, and nobody has the number.

This is the last tutorial in the course, and it is the one where every earlier piece of machinery gets pointed at something Niels is likely to build: cost behaviour, contribution margin, break-even, and a P&L for a single thing.

The unit is a request, and it has three parts

Cost the smallest unit that scales. For a model feature that is one request, and it has three components rather than one.

Input tokens at the input price — the prompt, the system message, the retrieved context, the conversation history.

Output tokens at the output price, usually several times higher.

Everything around the call — the embedding for retrieval, the vector search, a reranking pass, and retries. This is the line missing from every first estimate, and in a retrieval-augmented feature it is frequently larger than the generation itself.

A first estimate that counts only the generation call is typically half the real number or less, which is why the bill surprises people six weeks in.

Output usually dominates, and you control it

Output tokens generally cost several times what input tokens cost. Combined with the fact that prompts are often long and answers short, that produces a non-obvious result: the length of the answer is frequently a bigger lever than the size of the prompt.

It is also the lever most under your control. Prompt size is largely determined by how much context the task needs. Output length is determined by what you ask for, and “answer in at most three sentences” is a one-line change that can move the cost of a feature by a third.

The general point, which is tutorial 12’s: know which component actually dominates before optimising. Teams routinely spend a sprint compressing prompts when the output was three quarters of the bill.

A flat price against a usage-driven cost

Charge a flat monthly fee for a feature whose cost rises with use, and you have created something software businesses have not usually had to think about: a break-even user.

Below some level of activity a user is profitable. Above it they cost more than they pay. And because usage is heavily skewed — a small share of users generates most of the requests — the heaviest users can be materially loss-making while the average looks fine.

That is a new problem. In classic software the heaviest user was the best customer: they were getting the most value at no marginal cost and were the most likely to renew and expand. With inference in the cost of revenue, the heaviest user may be the worst customer you have, and the metric that reveals it is gross profit per user rather than revenue per user.

Four levers, in rough order of effect

Route easy work to a smaller model. Usually the largest single saving, because the price gap between model tiers is far larger than any efficiency you can find within one tier. Most features have a distribution of difficulty and send all of it to the model sized for the hardest case.

Cache what is asked repeatedly. Both response caching for identical requests and prompt caching for a shared prefix. Effectiveness depends entirely on how repetitive the traffic is, which is measurable before building anything.

Shorten the output. Cheap to try, immediate, and bounded by what the feature actually needs to say.

Cut retries. Retries are silent and they multiply. A feature retrying 12% of calls is 12% more expensive than anybody’s estimate, and the fix is usually failing faster rather than retrying better.

The ordering is approximate and the first is usually worth more than the rest combined — which is worth knowing before a team spends two weeks on prompt compression.

A P&L for one feature

The way to make any of this arguable is to give the feature its own small P&L.

Revenue attributable to it. The hard line, and the one people skip. If the feature is bundled, attribution is a judgement — but “we believe it drives X% of retention, worth €Y” is a claim somebody can disagree with, which is more than zero is.

Less inference and infrastructure. Cost per request times requests, plus the vector store and the serving stack.

Less the people maintaining it. A feature with a permanent engineer is a feature with a €120,000 annual cost that never appears in the model bill.

What comes out is a number somebody can argue with. That is the whole objective — not precision, but a figure specific enough to be wrong, which is the standard this course has applied to a business case, a benefit estimate and a forecast alike.

Light 70% 60 1.80 94%

Heavy 5% 2,900 58.40 (101%)

The heavy 5% cost €58.40 against €29 of revenue. They lose €29.40 each, month after month, and they are almost certainly the users who like the feature most.

At 5% of users they cost 3.4% of gross margin overall — survivable. But the segment is growing, because heavy users are the ones who tell colleagues. Left alone, this becomes the whole business.

Three responses, and the course has an opinion on the order. Route the heavy segment’s simpler requests to a smaller model — it changes the cost curve without changing the offer. Cache, if their traffic is repetitive. And only then change the pricing, because a usage cap on your most enthusiastic users is the option that costs the most goodwill and it should not be the first one reached for.

Do it

  1. Cost one request in a feature you have built. Include retrieval, reranking and retries — especially retries.
  2. Multiply by requests per active user per month. Compare to what that user pays.
  3. Get the usage distribution rather than the mean. Find the level at which a user stops being profitable, and what share of users are past it.
  4. Then estimate what routing the easiest half of requests to a smaller model would do, before changing anything else.

That is the end of the course. Step 3 is the calculation that most reliably surprises people, and it is the one you can do this afternoon.
