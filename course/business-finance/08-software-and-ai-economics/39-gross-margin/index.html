Gross margin in a software business, and what belongs in COGS

What sits above the line, how much the line's position moves the number, why the figure is watched — and what inference does to it.

Tutorial 02 of 04 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • List what a software business puts in cost of revenue and say what is contested
  • Say how much gross margin moves when customer success crosses the line
  • Explain why an AI feature changes the shape of the cost curve

The question

Two SaaS companies, identical products, identical costs. One reports 81% gross margin and the other 71%. Neither is misreporting.

Tutorial 02 said where the cost-of-sales line falls is a policy choice. This is that tutorial applied to a business where the choice moves the single most scrutinised number on the page.

What belongs in cost of revenue

For a software business, cost of revenue is what it takes to serve a customer who has already bought.

  • Hosting and infrastructure — the cloud bill attributable to production.
  • Support and customer success — or part of them, which is the contested part.
  • Third-party fees passed through — payment processing, licensed data, usage-based APIs the product depends on.
  • Amortisation of capitalised development — where tutorial 31’s decision lands on the P&L.

The first and third are uncontroversial. The second and fourth are where companies differ, and neither is settled by any standard: IFRS requires cost of sales to be presented but does not prescribe what a software company puts in it.

Where the line moves, the margin moves

Take the customer success team. It exists to keep customers using the product and renewing.

In cost of revenue, because it is required to serve customers who have bought. In operating expense, because it is really a retention and upsell function — closer to sales than to service.

Both arguments are honest and the effect is large: on a typical mid-size SaaS P&L, moving customer success across the line changes gross margin by around ten points. Same people, same cost, same operating profit, and a number that investors treat as a fact about the business model moves a tenth.

Which is why the accounting policy note is worth reading before comparing two companies’ gross margins, and why any benchmark table of “typical SaaS gross margin” should be read as a range of conventions as much as a range of businesses.

Why the number is watched so closely

Gross margin is scrutinised because of what it is taken to prove.

A high gross margin says the next customer is nearly free to serve — that the cost of the product is mostly already sunk and revenue growth converts almost directly into gross profit. That claim is the entire investment case for software as an asset class.

A low one says every customer costs real money to serve, which makes the business look more like a services firm, and services firms are valued on different multiples entirely.

So the number carries a great deal of weight, it is not fully specified by any standard, and the people preparing it know both of those things. That is not an accusation; it is a reason to read the note.

Margin differs by revenue type, so mix matters

Blended gross margin hides a mix, and the components are far apart.

Subscription revenue carries the highest margin — typically 75% to 85% — because the marginal cost of another seat is hosting and a share of support.

Usage-based revenue is lower, because it scales with the thing being consumed.

Professional services — implementation, training, custom work — is often 15% to 30%, because it is people’s time sold at a modest markup.

So a blended margin that falls may mean the business got worse, or may mean it sold more services this year. Those are very different findings and the blended figure cannot distinguish them. Any company that reports the split has given you something useful; any that does not has made a choice.

What inference does to the shape

Here is the part that is new, and it matters for anything Niels builds.

Classic software cost of revenue is nearly flat against usage. Hosting rises, but sublinearly; support rises with customers rather than with requests. That near-flatness is where 80% gross margins come from.

Model inference is genuinely variable. Every request has a cost that scales with tokens in and tokens out. There is no economy of scale in the call itself — the millionth request costs what the first one did.

So an AI feature bends the cost curve upward, and it does so as the feature succeeds. A feature nobody uses is free; a feature everybody uses is a materially different gross margin. That is a genuinely new problem for software businesses, and it is why the next tutorial’s unit is a request rather than a customer.

Worked example: the same company, two policies

Revenue €40,000,000.

Policy A Policy B
Hosting 4,200,000 4,200,000
Support 2,600,000 2,600,000
Customer success 3,900,000 —
Amortised development 1,000,000 —
Cost of revenue 11,700,000 6,800,000
Gross profit 28,300,000 33,200,000
Gross margin 71% 83%
Operating expenses 24,000,000 28,900,000
Operating profit 4,300,000 4,300,000

Identical operating profit. Twelve points of gross margin.

Policy B is not wrong. It puts customer success in sales and marketing, where its function arguably belongs, and it expenses development rather than capitalising it — which tutorial 31 would call the conservative choice.

The lesson for reading somebody else’s numbers: gross margin is not comparable between companies without the policy note. The lesson for producing your own: whichever convention you adopt, keep it, because the year you change it your margin moves and everyone will assume the business did.

And note the direction of the pressure. Policy B looks better on the metric investors watch and is more conservative on capitalisation. Those two rarely point the same way, which makes this one of the few places where the flattering choice is also the prudent one.

Do it

  1. Take any listed software company’s accounts and find what it includes in cost of revenue. It is in the accounting policies, near the front of the notes.
  2. Find whether customer success is above or below the line. Compute what moving it would do.
  3. If the company splits revenue by type, compute margin for each. Compare the subscription margin to the blended one.
  4. Then, for something you have built: estimate the inference cost per active user per month, and what it does to a margin if usage triples.

Step 4 is the calculation almost nobody does before launching a feature, and it is the subject of the last tutorial.
