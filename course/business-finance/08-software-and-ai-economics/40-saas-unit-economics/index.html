SaaS unit economics

What a customer costs to win against what they are worth, how long the money is out, the measure that decides whether growth needs new customers, and the average that hides two businesses.

Tutorial 03 of 04 · 30 minutes

  • Compute CAC, LTV and the ratio, using gross profit rather than revenue
  • Say why CAC payback constrains growth more directly than the LTV ratio
  • Explain what net revenue retention above 100% means for a business

The question

A company reports LTV/CAC of 4.2 and is running out of cash. Both are true, and the ratio is not the number that would have warned anybody.

CAC and LTV, and the mistake in the middle

CAC — customer acquisition cost — is all sales and marketing spend in a period divided by the customers won in it. All of it: salaries, commission, tooling, events, not just advertising.

LTV — lifetime value — is the gross profit a customer produces before they leave. Monthly gross profit divided by monthly churn rate gives the standard approximation.

The mistake in the middle is using revenue instead of gross profit in LTV. At 80% gross margin that overstates LTV by 25%; at 60% it overstates it by 67%. It is the commonest error in the metric and it always points the same way.

The usual bar is LTV/CAC above 3. Below 1 the company loses money on every customer. Far above 5 usually means underinvestment in growth rather than excellence, which is a real finding and rarely reported as one.

CAC payback: how long the money is out

The ratio says whether a customer is worth winning. It does not say when you get paid, and that is what kills companies.

CAC payback is how many months of gross profit it takes to recover the acquisition cost. Under twelve months is comfortable. Over twenty-four means every new customer has to be funded for two years before any of the money comes back — so growth consumes cash in direct proportion to how well it is going.

This is tutorial 29’s point about payback, in the place where it binds hardest. LTV/CAC of 4.2 with a thirty-month payback describes a company that is creating value and running out of money, which is precisely the case in the question above.

Note also that LTV depends on a churn estimate over years, while payback depends on a few months of observed data. One is a projection; the other is nearly a fact.

Net revenue retention

Take last year’s customers only. Ignore everyone new. What are they worth this year?

Net revenue retention is that figure — the base, less churn, plus expansion from the customers who stayed. Above 100% and the existing base grows on its own before a single new customer is added.

That is a different kind of business. At 114% NRR a company grows 14% with no sales at all, so acquisition buys growth on top of growth rather than replacing losses. At 90% it must win 10% in new customers just to stand still, and every acquisition euro is running to stay in place.

Of all the metrics in this tutorial, this is the one worth watching most closely, because it is the hardest to flatter and it describes whether the product is actually working for the people who have it.

The rule of forty

A heuristic rather than a law: growth rate plus profit margin should be at least forty.

It formalises a trade everyone makes intuitively. Grow 60% and lose 15% — that scores 45, and the losses are buying growth. Grow 12% and make 30% — that scores 42, and the business is mature and profitable. Both are fine.

Grow 15% and lose 20% and it scores −5, which is the case the rule exists to catch: a company that is neither growing fast enough to justify the losses nor profitable enough to justify the slow growth.

Treat it as a screen rather than a target. It is arbitrary at the margin — nothing distinguishes 39 from 41 — and it is useful because it refuses to reward doing neither thing well.

The blended average that hides two businesses

The last idea is the one that makes the others usable, and it is tutorial 14’s argument again.

Most companies have at least two acquisition motions: a cheap self-serve one and an expensive enterprise one. Blended CAC averages them, and the average describes neither.

Self-serve might be €400 CAC with a four-month payback. Enterprise might be €38,000 with a twenty-two-month payback. A blended €6,200 is a number that matches no customer the company has ever won, and it hides the only question that matters: which motion should be funded harder?

So compute everything by segment. If you can only do one thing from this tutorial, do that — the segmented numbers usually change the answer, and the blended ones usually confirm whatever was already believed.

Worked example: the company with 4.2 and no cash

Blended figures first.
Sales and marketing spend, year 4,800,000
New customers won 780
CAC 6,154
Average revenue per customer, monthly 1,050
Gross margin 78%
Monthly gross profit per customer 819
Monthly churn 1.6%
LTV (819 / 0.016) 51,188
LTV / CAC 8.3
CAC payback (6,154 / 819) 7.5 months

That looks excellent. Now split it.

Self-serve Enterprise
Customers won 700 80
S&M spend 900,000 3,900,000
CAC 1,286 48,750
Monthly gross profit 180 6,410
Monthly churn 3.1% 0.4%
LTV 5,806 1,602,500
LTV / CAC 4.5 32.9
CAC payback 7.1 months 7.6 months

Both segments are healthy, and the blend was hiding that enterprise is extraordinary while self-serve is merely fine. The obvious action — fund enterprise harder — is invisible in the blended row.

Now change one number. Suppose enterprise churn is 0.4% measured over eleven months of data. LTV of €1.6m assumes a customer stays over twenty years. Cut the assumed life to a defensible five years and enterprise LTV falls to €384,600 and the ratio to 7.9 — still good, and a quarter of what was reported.

That is the standard fragility of LTV: it divides by a small churn number, so small errors in churn produce enormous errors in LTV. Payback does not have that problem, which is the argument for leading with it.

Do it

  1. Compute CAC for your business or one you can see. Include every sales and marketing cost, not just the advertising.
  2. Compute LTV using gross profit. Then compute it again using revenue, and note the difference — that gap is the error you will see in most decks.
  3. Compute CAC payback. Compare what it says to what the ratio says.
  4. Then split all three by segment, and see whether the recommendation changes.

Step 4 changes the answer more often than not, which is why the blended figure is the one usually presented.
