Cloud spend as opex: commitments, showback and chargeback

What a commitment really buys, the difference between telling people and charging them, the untagged share nobody owns, and why the line only goes one way.

Tutorial 01 of 04 · 30 minutes

  • Say what a commitment does to the fixed/variable character of cloud cost

The question

Cloud spend went from €40,000 a month to €95,000 over two years. No single decision caused it, nobody approved it, and asking who owns it produces a list of teams whose stated usage adds to about half the bill.

Every idea needed to understand that has already appeared in this course. This track is the same machinery pointed at the work Niels actually does.

What a commitment actually buys

Tutorial 21 said the cloud converted capex into opex and made spending frictionless. Commitments are the counter-move, and they are worth reading carefully.

A reserved instance or a spend commitment buys a discount — commonly 20% to 40% — in exchange for an obligation to spend a fixed amount over one or three years. You pay it whether you use it or not.

Which means: a commitment converts a variable cost back into a fixed one. That is a real trade and not obviously a good one. It is right when the baseline is genuinely stable and wrong when the whole reason for being on the cloud was that you did not know what you would need. Companies routinely commit to a baseline set from a peak, and then pay for a peak that does not recur.

Showback and chargeback

There are two ways to make a shared cost visible, and the choice is the same one tutorial 13 posed about allocation bases.

Showback reports each team’s usage and cost without moving any budget. The cost stays central; the team is simply informed.

Chargeback actually moves the money. The team’s cost centre carries the spend and its budget bears it.

Chargeback changes behaviour and showback mostly does not — a team that sees a number it does not pay will act on it only for as long as somebody keeps mentioning it. But chargeback also imports every argument from tutorial 13: the allocation base becomes contested, shared platform costs need a driver, and somebody will point out that they cannot reduce their share by using less.

The usual sequence is showback first, then chargeback once the numbers are trusted, which is sensible: charging people for a number they believe is wrong produces a fight about the number rather than about the usage.

The untagged share is nobody’s

Neither mechanism works on spend that carries no ownership tag, and in most organisations that is a substantial fraction.

Resources created before a tagging policy existed. Shared clusters. Things spun up during an incident. Anything created by a person who has since left.

Untagged spend cannot be shown back or charged out, so it accumulates centrally and belongs to nobody. Which means nobody is looking for savings in it, and it is frequently where the largest savings are — precisely because it has never been examined.

The first thing any cost programme should measure is not the bill. It is what share of the bill has an owner.

Why the line only goes one way

Tutorial 21’s point deserves its own treatment, because the asymmetry is the whole problem.

Going up is frictionless. An engineer provisions a resource. No purchase order, no approval, no capital paper. The cost appears next month as one line in an aggregate bill.

Coming down requires somebody to decide to. Nothing prompts it. An owned server is depreciated, reaches end of life, and forces a replacement decision. A running instance is asked about by nobody, ever, and simply continues.

That asymmetry is enough on its own to produce a ratchet. It needs no waste and no bad decisions — just a system where one direction is automatic and the other requires an act of will.

The FinOps cycle, and where the value is

The FinOps Foundation’s framework runs in three phases, repeatedly.

Inform. Give each team visibility of its own spend, tagged and attributed.

Optimise. Rightsize what is oversized, commit where the baseline is stable, delete what is unused.

Operate. Make it somebody’s standing job rather than a quarterly panic.

Worth knowing which phase does the work: most of the saving comes from the first one. Not because visibility is magic, but because nobody had ever seen the number. A team shown its own monthly spend for the first time typically finds obvious waste within a week — an environment nobody uses, a log retention setting, a cluster sized for a load test in 2023.

That is the same finding as tutorial 14’s whale curve, and for the same reason: the analysis is valuable the first time and the standing process is what stops the problem returning.

Worked example: the €95,000 month

Category € Tagged? Notes
Production compute 38,000 Yes Four teams, attributable
Production storage 14,000 Yes Growing 4% a month
Non-production 21,000 Partly Includes 6 idle environments
Data transfer 9,000 No Cross-region, unattributed
Untagged / legacy 13,000 No Created before the policy
Total 95,000

€22,000 — 23% — has no owner. Nothing can be shown back for it and nobody is reducing it.

Three findings fall out without any optimisation work.

Storage growing 4% a month compounds to a 60% annual increase. Nobody decided that; it is a retention setting.

Six idle non-production environments is the classic first-week finding of an inform phase. They exist because creating one is easy and deleting one is nobody’s task.

Cross-region transfer at €9,000 is usually an architecture decision made for a reason that no longer applies, and it is invisible until somebody puts it on its own line.

Now the commitment question. Of €95,000, the genuinely stable baseline is perhaps the €38,000 of production compute. Committing to €38,000 at 30% off saves €11,400 a month and is defensible. Committing to €95,000 — which a vendor will happily propose — locks in the idle environments and the untagged legacy for three years, and turns a fixable problem into a contractual one.

Fix first, commit second. It is the same order as the FinOps phases and the same reason.

Do it

  1. Get your cloud bill split by tag. Compute the untagged share as a percentage. That is the first number.
  2. List non-production environments and when each was last used. Idle ones are the cheapest saving available.
  3. Find one line growing faster than the business. Storage and log retention are the usual candidates.
  4. Then, before agreeing any commitment, establish which part of the spend is a stable baseline and which is a problem you have not fixed yet.

Step 4 is the one that costs money if skipped, and it is skipped often because the discount is quoted against the whole bill.
