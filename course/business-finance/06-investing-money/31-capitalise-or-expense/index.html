Capitalise or expense, including software (IAS 38)

Research is expensed and development may not be, six conditions that must all hold, where the line falls in a software project, and what capitalising commits you to.

Tutorial 04 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Apply IAS 38's research/development split to a real project
  • Name the six conditions and say which one usually decides
  • Say what happens to capitalised development if the product is abandoned

The question

Two years of engineering on a platform. Roughly €1.4m of salary. How much of it is a cost of those two years and how much is an asset the company now owns?

The answer moves reported profit by hundreds of thousands of euros, it is decided by six conditions in one standard, and in a Belgian group it can be two different answers in two sets of accounts for the same work.

Research is expensed; development may be capitalised

IAS 38 splits internally generated intangibles at one line.

Research — work aimed at finding out whether something is possible. Must be expensed as incurred, always, with no option.

Development — applying that knowledge to produce something specific. May be capitalised, if six conditions hold.

The distinction in one sentence: finding out whether it can be done is research; building it is development. A spike to see whether an approach works is research even if the code is kept. Building the thing after you know the approach works is development.

Note the asymmetry in the rules. Research is a prohibition; development is a permission with conditions attached. There is no discretion at all on the first half.

The six conditions, all of which must hold

IAS 38.57, in plain terms:

  1. It is technically feasible to complete it.
  2. You intend to complete it and use or sell it.
  3. You are able to use or sell it.
  4. It will probably generate future economic benefit.
  5. Adequate resources exist to finish it.
  6. The expenditure can be measured reliably.

All six. Failing any one means the spend is expensed.

Condition 1 is the one that usually decides, and it is what makes the research/development line real rather than semantic: until the approach is demonstrated, technical feasibility is precisely what is unknown, so the work cannot pass the first test. That is why the line sits where the design is committed rather than where the project is approved.

Condition 6 is the one that fails in practice for reasons nobody expects: if engineers do not book time to the project, the spend cannot be measured reliably, and a genuine asset gets expensed for want of timesheets.

Where the line falls in a software project

Map the conditions onto a real project and three phases appear.

Discovery and prototyping. Working out what to build and whether the approach holds. Research — expensed.

Build, after the design is committed. Feasibility demonstrated, intent clear, resources allocated. Development — capitalise, if the other conditions hold.

Support and enhancement after go-live. Maintaining what exists. Expensed again, with the exception of a substantial enhancement that meets the conditions in its own right, which starts the cycle over.

Two dates therefore have to be set by somebody, written down, and defended: when feasibility was established, and when the asset went live. Neither is a fact in a system; both are judgements, and both are the first thing an auditor asks for.

Once capitalised, it has to be lived with

Capitalising is not a way of making a cost disappear. It is a decision to carry it, and it has three consequences.

An asset appears on the balance sheet, which raises reported assets and equity.

It is amortised from go-live, over its useful life — commonly three to five years for software. So the €1.4m returns to the P&L at €280,000 to €470,000 a year, and next year’s profit is worse than it would have been.

It is impaired in full if abandoned. Cancel the product in year two and the unamortised balance is written off in one charge. That is the moment a capitalisation decision becomes visible, and it is why the write-offs cluster around strategy changes and new management.

The honest summary: capitalising moves profit from this year into later years, and it converts a cost that was already absorbed into a risk that can arrive all at once.

Belgian GAAP diverges here

Belgian statutory accounting under the CBN/CNC rules is more permissive on development costs than IFRS: capitalisation is available on looser terms, and the prudence principle shapes the treatment differently.

The practical consequence for anyone in a Belgian group: the statutory accounts of the Belgian entity and the IFRS consolidation can legitimately carry different figures for the same engineering work, and both are correct.

If you are handed two numbers for the same project and told they disagree, this is one of the places to look before assuming somebody has made a mistake. Ask which framework each set of accounts is prepared under.

Worked example: €1.4m over two years

Phase Months Cost Treatment
Discovery, options, spikes 4 220,000 Expense — research
Architecture agreed, build 14 890,000 Capitalise
Documentation, training 2 110,000 Capitalise, directly attributable
Post-launch support 4 180,000 Expense
Total 1,400,000

Capitalised: €1,000,000. Expensed: €400,000.

Now the effect on reported profit, against a five-year amortisation.

Expense everything Capitalise €1m
Year 1 charge 700,000 130,000
Year 2 charge 700,000 470,000
Year 3 charge — 200,000
Years 4–6 charge — 200,000 each
Total 1,400,000 1,400,000

The total is identical, as it must be. Year 1 differs by €570,000.

And the risk: if the product is cancelled at the end of year 2, the unamortised balance is roughly €800,000, written off in one charge. The €570,000 of year-1 profit that capitalising protected turns into an €800,000 charge in a year somebody is already having a bad time. That is the trade, stated plainly, and it is worth stating before the decision rather than after.

Do it

  1. Find your organisation’s accounting policy note on internally generated intangibles. It will name which phases are capitalised.
  2. Take one project and split its cost into research, development and support using the two dates from the third section.
  3. Check whether engineers actually book time to it. If not, condition 6 fails and none of the rest matters.
  4. Then compute the unamortised balance on anything capitalised more than a year ago, and ask what would happen to it if the product were retired.

Step 4 is the number that turns an accounting policy into a risk register entry.
