Cost of capital and hurdle rates

Where the discount rate comes from, why the rate projects are judged on sits above it, and what a single rate does to long projects.

Tutorial 03 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Compute a WACC from a debt and equity mix
  • Say why the hurdle rate exceeds the cost of capital and what that corrects for
  • Explain why a uniform hurdle rate systematically rejects long projects

The question

The last tutorial discounted everything at 10% without saying where 10% came from. It matters more than almost anything else in the calculation: on a fifteen-year benefit, moving the rate from 8% to 15% removes more than half the present value, and nobody in the meeting will mention it.

Where the discount rate comes from

A company funds itself with debt and equity, each has a price, and the discount rate is the weighted average of the two — the WACC.

With 40% debt at 4% after tax and 60% equity at 11%:

WACC = (0.4 × 4%) + (0.6 × 11%) = 1.6% + 6.6% = 8.2%

The logic is that a project has to earn at least what the money costs. Below WACC, the project destroys value even if it is profitable in accounting terms — it earns less than the capital funding it could have earned elsewhere.

The debt figure is stated after tax relief, because interest is deductible and the state therefore pays part of it. That is a real effect and part of why debt looks so cheap.

Debt is cheaper than equity, and why

4% against 11% is a large gap and it is not arbitrary.

Lenders are paid first, a contractual amount, on a contractual date, usually with security. If the company fails they rank ahead of shareholders. Low risk, so a low required return.

Shareholders are paid last, whatever is left, if anything. High risk, so a high required return.

Add the tax deductibility of interest and debt is cheaper twice over — which raises the obvious question of why any company uses equity. The answer is that debt has to be serviced whether or not the business is doing well, so beyond some point the cheapness is bought with fragility. Both prices rise as leverage increases, which is why WACC does not fall indefinitely as you borrow more.

The hurdle rate sits above WACC, deliberately

Most companies do not discount at WACC. They discount at a hurdle rate that is meaningfully higher — 8.2% becomes 15%.

The stated reason is risk. The real reason, usually, is a correction for optimism: benefit estimates in business cases are systematically overstated, and a higher hurdle is a blunt way of deflating them without arguing about each one.

That is a rational response to the incentive problem from tutorial 28. It is also an undisclosed one — the correction is applied silently rather than by challenging the estimates — so a project with genuinely conservative numbers is penalised for the sins of the ones around it, and the person who wrote it never finds out why.

One rate for every project is wrong, and universal

The textbook position is unambiguous: the discount rate should reflect the project’s risk, not the company’s average. A warehouse extension and a research programme should not be judged at the same rate.

Practically every company uses one rate anyway.

The consequence is systematic. Low-risk projects are over-discounted and get rejected; high-risk projects are under-discounted and get approved. Over years, a single hurdle tilts the portfolio towards risk while everyone believes the process is neutral.

Knowing this is useful even if you cannot change the rate. It tells you that a safe project failing the hurdle by a small margin may be worth arguing for on the grounds that the rate is wrong for it — which is a much better argument than inflating the benefits until it clears.

What the rate does to a long project

The last point is the one with the largest hidden effect.

Discounting is exponential, so a change in the rate barely touches near-term flows and dominates distant ones. Take the rate from 8% to 15%:

  • a benefit in year 3 loses about 15% of its present value;
  • a benefit in year 15 loses about 55%.

So a high hurdle is not neutral between projects — it systematically rejects long-horizon ones. Infrastructure, platform work, anything whose payoff is a decade out gets filtered out by a spreadsheet default that nobody discussed as a strategy.

This is worth naming when it happens, because it usually happens silently. “We don’t invest in long-term platforms” is a strategy somebody could argue with. “Our hurdle rate is 15%” is the same strategy, implemented without a conversation.

Worked example: the same project at three rates

A platform investment: €500,000 now, then €90,000 a year for fifteen years.

Discount rate NPV Verdict
8.2% (WACC) +268,000 Comfortably worth doing
12% +113,000 Worth doing
15% +26,000 Marginal
18% (37,000) Rejected

The project has not changed. Its cash flows are identical in all four rows. The verdict flips between “obviously yes” and “no” purely on the rate.

Now the same total benefit compressed into five years — €270,000 a year for five, so €1,350,000 of undiscounted benefit against €1,350,000 in the long version:

Discount rate Long, 15 years Short, 5 years
8.2% +268,000 +570,000
15% +26,000 +405,000
Loss from the rate rise (242,000) (165,000)

Same money, same total, and the long project loses half again as much from the rate rise. If both are on a list and the hurdle is 15%, the short one gets built and the long one does not — regardless of which is better for the business.

Do it

  1. Find your organisation’s hurdle rate. Then find its actual cost of capital, or estimate it from the debt/equity mix in the accounts.
  2. Compute the gap. That gap is the optimism correction, and nobody will describe it that way.
  3. Take one investment you know and recompute its NPV at both rates.
  4. Then take a fifteen-year benefit and a three-year one of equal total value, and compute what the hurdle does to each.

Step 4 is the argument to have if your organisation says it wants to invest in platforms and keeps not doing so.
