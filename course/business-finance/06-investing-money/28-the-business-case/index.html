The business case, and what it is really for

Read as a prediction, used as a promise — four sections, three grades of benefit, and the review step that would fix all of it.

Tutorial 01 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Grade a claimed benefit as cashable, efficiency or strategic
  • Cost the do-nothing option and say why the saving is a gap between futures
  • Say what a missing post-implementation review does to benefit estimates

The question

You write €600,000 of annual benefit into a business case in March. The project is approved. Two years later somebody asks where the €600,000 went, and the honest answer is that it was an estimate made to clear a hurdle and nobody ever intended to measure it.

That is not fraud, and it is not unusual. It is what happens to a number that is written as a prediction and used as a promise.

What a business case is really for

The document reads as a forecast: here is what we think will happen. It functions as a commitment: this is what you have signed up to deliver.

Which means the benefit number is not a neutral estimate. It is the figure you will be measured against for the life of the investment, and everyone writing one knows that — which is exactly why the estimating is systematically optimistic in one direction and the costs are estimated more honestly than the benefits.

Understanding this changes how you write one. A benefit you cannot demonstrate in two years’ time is a liability you are creating for yourself, not a persuasive detail.

Four sections, and only one is contested

Nearly every business case format reduces to four things.

The problem — what happens if nothing is done. The options — including doing nothing. The costs — usually well estimated, because they come from quotes and rates. The benefits — usually badly estimated, because nothing constrains them.

The asymmetry matters. The costs are checkable and get checked. The benefits decide the answer and are checked by nobody, which is why every technique in the rest of this tutorial is about the benefits.

Benefits come in three grades, and only one is real

Sort every claimed benefit into one of three:

Cashable. A budget line goes down and stays down. Three contractors no longer needed; a licence cancelled; a supplier contract reduced. Verifiable, because somebody’s budget can be cut by the amount.

Efficiency. Time saved that does not reduce any budget. “Saves each engineer two hours a week” is forty hours a week across a team of twenty — a full-time person, on paper, that nobody will ever remove. Real value, no cash.

Strategic. Optionality, resilience, positioning. Sometimes the most important thing in the case and unmeasurable by construction.

The discipline: count only the cashable ones in the financial case and put the other two in the narrative. A case that clears a hurdle only by counting efficiency savings does not clear the hurdle. This one rule removes most of the dishonesty from business cases, and it is unpopular for that reason.

The do-nothing option is a real option

Every case must include doing nothing, and most include it as a sentence rather than a costed option.

That matters because the benefit is the gap between two futures, not the gap against today. If costs would have risen anyway — volumes growing, a system going out of support, licences repricing — then the saving from acting is measured against that rising line, and it is larger than a comparison with today’s costs suggests.

The reverse is also true and more common: a case that claims a saving against today’s costs, when today’s costs were going to fall anyway, is claiming credit for something that would have happened.

Costing the do-nothing option properly is the single biggest improvement available to most business cases, and it usually strengthens them.

Post-implementation review, and why nobody does it

There is exactly one mechanism that would discipline benefit estimates: going back after two years and checking.

Almost no organisation does it. The project is closed, the team has dispersed, the sponsor has moved, and there is no appetite to establish that a completed investment did not pay. So the loop never closes, and benefit estimates are free.

Estimates that are free behave exactly as you would expect. This is not a moral failing on anyone’s part; it is what an unmeasured number does over time in any organisation.

If you want one thing from this tutorial that you can actually do: write the benefits so they could be checked. Name the budget line, the amount, and the date. Even if nobody checks, the act of making it checkable removes most of the optimism at the point of writing.

Worked example: grading a real case

A €480,000 platform replacement, claiming €600,000 a year.

Claimed benefit € Grade Counts?
Three contractors no longer needed 240,000 Cashable Yes
Legacy licence cancelled 85,000 Cashable Yes
Engineers save 2 hours a week each 190,000 Efficiency No
Faster onboarding for new customers 60,000 Strategic No
Reduced incident risk 25,000 Strategic No
Claimed total 600,000
Counting cashable only 325,000

The case still works — €325,000 a year against €480,000 is a payback under eighteen months — and it now rests on two claims that can be verified by looking at two budget lines next year.

The €190,000 of efficiency is the interesting one. It is probably real, and it becomes cashable only if somebody actually reduces headcount or absorbs new work without hiring. Either of those is a decision nobody has made, so writing it into the financial case commits you to a saving that depends on a decision outside the project.

Better: state it in the narrative, and add one line saying what would have to happen for it to become cashable. That is a stronger case, not a weaker one, because it is the version that survives the review.

Do it

  1. Take any business case you can find. Grade every benefit into the three categories.
  2. Recompute the financial case counting cashable only. See whether it still clears the hurdle.
  3. Find the do-nothing option and check whether it was costed or just mentioned.
  4. Then ask whether a post-implementation review was ever done on anything in your organisation, and what it found.

Step 4’s answer is usually “no” — which is the finding, and worth saying out loud once.
