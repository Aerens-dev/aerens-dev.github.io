Earned value: CPI, SPI, EAC, and what EVM cannot see

Three numbers, two ratios, one forecast, and a clear statement of the three questions a perfectly green dashboard does not answer.

Tutorial 05 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Compute CPI, SPI and EAC from planned value, earned value and actual cost
  • Say which EAC formula is the honest one and why
  • Name what earned value measures and what it structurally cannot

The question

A project is six months in, has spent 50% of its budget, and reports itself 50% complete. Whether that is on track depends entirely on how “50% complete” was arrived at, and in most projects it was arrived at by looking at the spend.

Three numbers

Everything in earned value comes from three figures at a point in time.

PV — planned value. The budgeted cost of the work the plan said would be finished by now.

EV — earned value. The budgeted cost of the work that is finished. Note carefully: measured at plan cost, not at what it actually cost. If a work package was budgeted at €40,000 and it is done, it has earned €40,000 — whatever was spent doing it.

AC — actual cost. What has been spent on the work that is finished.

EV is the one people find hardest to state, and it is the whole idea. It requires somebody to say how much of the work is genuinely complete, independently of what has been spent — which is precisely the thing the question above was dodging.

Two indices, both against earned value

CPI = EV / AC. How much planned work you get per euro spent. CPI 0.85 means 85 cents of work for every euro.

SPI = EV / PV. How much of the planned work is done. SPI 0.90 means 90% of what should be finished is finished.

Both read the same way: below 1.0 is behind, above is ahead. Both have earned value on top, which is why EV is the number that matters and the other two are denominators.

One caveat on SPI worth knowing: it is measured in cost units, not time, and it converges to 1.0 at the end of any project regardless of how late it is, because eventually EV equals PV. A late project’s SPI improves as it finishes, which makes it useless in the final stretch. Schedule variance in time units, or a critical-path view, is the corrective.

EAC and ETC: what the whole thing will cost

Estimate at completion projects the final cost, and there are two common formulas that say very different things.

The optimistic one: EAC = AC + (BAC − EV). Take what has been spent and add the remaining work at plan cost. This assumes the overspend so far was a one-off and everything from here goes to plan.

The honest one: EAC = BAC / CPI. Assume the rate of overspend continues.

The second is nearly always closer, and every project team prefers the first. PMI’s own guidance and the empirical work on large projects both point the same way: performance to date is the best available predictor of performance to come, and a CPI that has been 0.85 for six months is not about to become 1.0 because somebody has resolved to try harder.

ETC — estimate to complete — is simply EAC − AC: what is still to be spent.

What earned value does well

One thing, and it is genuinely valuable: it refuses to let spend stand in for progress.

Without it, “50% of the budget is gone” quietly becomes “we are halfway”, and nobody has to say anything they can be held to. Earned value makes somebody state, in writing, how much of the work is actually done — and once that statement exists, the gap between it and the spend is visible and arguable.

That single discipline catches the most expensive failure mode in project management, which is a project that is 30% done and 70% spent and does not know it yet.

What earned value cannot see

It measures conformance to a plan. So there are three things a perfectly green dashboard says nothing about.

Whether the plan was any good. A project executing beautifully against a badly scoped plan reports CPI 1.0 and SPI 1.0 all the way to a useless deliverable.

Whether the work is any good. EV counts a work package as complete when somebody says it is. Quality lives entirely outside the model, and rework appears only later, as an overspend on a package that was already earned.

Whether it is still worth building. The business case may have evaporated. EVM has no input for that and will keep reporting green.

None of these is a criticism of the technique — it does what it claims. It is a caution about what a set of green indices licenses you to stop asking. The question EVM answers is “are we delivering the plan”; the questions it cannot touch are “was the plan right” and “should we still be doing this”.

Worked example: six months in

Budget at completion €2,400,000, twelve months, spend planned evenly.

At month 6: PV = €1,200,000. AC = €1,200,000. The team says the work finished is worth €960,000 at plan cost, so EV = €960,000.

Measure Calculation Result
CPI 960 / 1,200 0.80
SPI 960 / 1,200 0.80
Cost variance 960 − 1,200 (240,000)
EAC, optimistic 1,200 + (2,400 − 960) 2,640,000
EAC, honest 2,400 / 0.80 3,000,000
ETC, honest 3,000 − 1,200 1,800,000

Spend and plan are exactly equal, so a spend-versus-budget report shows this project on budget. It is not. Only 40% of the work is done against 50% planned, at a cost of 50%.

The two EACs differ by €360,000, and the difference is entirely the assumption. The optimistic one says the last six months will run at CPI 1.0 after six months at 0.80. The honest one says they will run at 0.80.

The forecast to argue about is the second one. The conversation worth having is what would have to change to make the first one true, and whether anybody believes it — because “we will make it up in the second half” is a plan only if somebody can say what is being done differently.

Do it

  1. For a project you can see, get the three numbers. AC and PV are usually available; EV will require somebody to assess completion, which is the point.
  2. Compute CPI and SPI. Then compute both EACs and note the gap.
  3. Ask which EAC the project reports, and whether it has been revised as CPI moved.
  4. Then ask the three questions EVM cannot answer, out loud, about the same project.

Step 4 takes two minutes and is the part that is never on the dashboard.
