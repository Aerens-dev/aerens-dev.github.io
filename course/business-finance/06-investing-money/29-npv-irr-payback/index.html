NPV, IRR, payback - and IRR's failure modes

Three ways to score an investment, one that is right, one that is popular and misleading, and one that is crude and answers a different question.

Tutorial 02 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Compute an NPV and say what a positive answer actually means
  • Name IRR's three failure modes and say which is most dangerous
  • Say what payback measures and why it is worth computing anyway

The question

Project A returns 60% on €10,000. Project B returns 20% on €4,000,000. You can do both. A committee that ranks by IRR will discuss A first and at length.

Money later is worth less

A euro today can be invested; a euro in five years cannot be invested for those five years. So a future euro is worth less than a present one, and the discount rate states how much less.

At 10%, a euro in one year is worth 90.9 cents today, in three years 75.1 cents, in five years 62.1 cents. The arithmetic is one division: divide by (1 + r)ⁿ.

This is not a claim about inflation. Even with zero inflation, money now beats money later, because now can be put to work. Inflation is a separate reason on top of it, and so is the risk that the money never arrives.

NPV: one number, in today’s euros

Net present value discounts every cash flow — in and out — back to today and adds them up.

Spend €400,000 now and receive €120,000 a year for five years at a 10% discount rate: the inflows are worth €454,900 in today’s money, so the NPV is +€54,900.

The interpretation is precise and often misstated. A positive NPV does not mean “the project makes money”. It means the project earns more than the discount rate. At a 10% rate, a €54,900 NPV says: after paying for capital at 10%, there is €54,900 of value left over.

Which is why NPV is the right default. It answers the question you asked, in euros, and it adds up across projects in a way percentages do not.

IRR: the rate that makes NPV zero

Plot NPV against the discount rate and it slopes down. The rate where it crosses zero is the internal rate of return.

Its appeal is obvious: one percentage, comparable to a hurdle rate, no need to agree a discount rate first. “This project returns 18%” is a sentence anybody can use in a meeting, which is why IRR is more widely quoted than NPV despite being the worse measure.

IRR’s three failure modes

Scale. IRR is a rate, so it ignores size entirely. 60% on €10,000 creates €6,000 of value; 20% on €4,000,000 creates €800,000. Ranking by IRR puts them in the wrong order, and this is by far the most common way it misleads in practice.

Multiple sign changes. If the cash flows switch between negative and positive more than once — a project with a decommissioning cost at the end, say — the polynomial has more than one root and there is more than one IRR. Both are mathematically valid and neither means anything.

The reinvestment assumption. IRR implicitly assumes interim cash flows are reinvested at the IRR itself. For a project returning 60%, that assumes you have somewhere else returning 60% to put the money, which you generally do not.

NPV has none of these. Use IRR as a secondary sanity check, and rank by NPV.

Payback: crude, popular, not useless

Payback is how long until the outlay is recovered. €400,000 at €120,000 a year pays back in 3.3 years.

It ignores the time value of money — a discounted payback fixes that — and it ignores everything after the payback point entirely, which is a serious defect: a project paying back in three years and then stopping ranks equal with one paying back in three years and running for fifteen.

But it is worth computing alongside NPV, because it measures something the others do not: how long the money is exposed. For a company short of cash, or in a market that may not exist in four years, that is a real constraint and not a crude proxy for value.

The rule: rank by NPV, sanity-check with IRR, and use payback as a constraint rather than a score.

Worked example: the two projects

Discount rate 10%.

Project A Project B
Outlay 10,000 4,000,000
Annual inflow 6,000 1,100,000
Life 3 years 5 years
IRR 36% 11.6%
Payback 1.7 years 3.6 years
NPV at 10% 4,921 169,865

A wins on IRR by a wide margin and on payback. B creates thirty-five times as much value.

If you can only do one and capital is not constrained, do B. If capital is constrained, the right measure is neither — it is the profitability index, NPV per euro invested: A returns €0.49 of NPV per euro, B returns €0.04. Under a hard capital limit, A wins.

Which is the honest summary of this whole tutorial: the right measure depends on what is scarce. Unconstrained, maximise NPV. Capital-constrained, maximise NPV per euro. Cash-constrained, payback becomes a binding constraint. IRR is the answer to none of those questions and is quoted more often than all three.

Do it

  1. Take a real investment you know the numbers for. Build the cash flows by year, including the outlay as year 0.
  2. Discount them at your organisation’s hurdle rate and compute NPV. A spreadsheet does this in one function.
  3. Compute IRR and payback as well. Note whether they would rank two projects differently from NPV.
  4. Then compute NPV per euro invested, and ask whether your organisation is actually capital-constrained.

Step 4 matters: most organisations rank as though capital were unlimited and then behave as though it were not.
