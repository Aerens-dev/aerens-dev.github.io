Variance analysis: flexible budgets, price, volume and mix

One adverse number that answers nothing, split into a volume effect, a rate effect, a usage effect and a mix effect — and the rule for which ones to chase.

Tutorial 04 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Flex a budget to actual volume and split the variance in two
  • Split a spending variance into rate and usage
  • Say which variances are worth investigating and which are already explained

The question

Budget €100,000, actual €118,000. An €18,000 adverse variance, and somebody is going to be asked about it.

You made 1,250 units against a plan of 1,000. So you did 25% more work for 18% more money, which is a good month reported as a bad one.

A fixed-budget variance mixes two questions

The comparison as printed asks a single question — did you spend what you said — against a plan built for a different amount of work.

That conflates two entirely separate things: did volume change and did you spend differently per unit. Both are real questions and the combined number answers neither, which is why a variance against a fixed budget is close to useless in any business whose volumes move.

The flexible budget separates them

The fix is one step. Restate the budget at the volume actually achieved.

If the budget was €100,000 for 1,000 units and the variable cost is €100 a unit, the flexed budget for 1,250 units is €125,000. Now:

  • Volume variance = flexed − original = €25,000 adverse. Cost rose because output rose, which is not a problem and may be excellent news.
  • Spending variance = actual − flexed = €118,000 − €125,000 = €7,000 favourable. At the volume achieved, you spent less than the model said.

An €18,000 adverse total containing a €7,000 favourable performance. The flexible budget is the single most useful piece of variance technique there is, and it takes one multiplication.

One caution from track 3: flexing needs a correct fixed/variable split. If the €100 per unit is really €60 variable plus fixed cost divided by budgeted units, flexing on €100 overstates the flexed budget and manufactures a favourable spending variance out of nothing.

Price and quantity, on the same cost

The spending variance splits again, into the two things that make a cost what it is: the rate paid and the quantity used.

Expected 500 hours at €90 = €45,000. Actual 520 hours at €86 = €44,720. A €280 favourable variance that looks like nothing, containing:

  • Rate variance = (90 − 86) × 520 = €2,080 favourable. You paid less per hour.
  • Usage variance = (500 − 520) × 90 = €1,800 adverse. You used more hours.

Two effects, each seven times the net figure, pointing in opposite directions. And they usually have different owners — procurement negotiated the rate, operations consumed the hours — which is why splitting them is the difference between a useful conversation and a shrug.

They also often have a causal link worth naming: a cheaper rate frequently buys a less experienced person who takes longer. A favourable rate variance sitting next to an adverse usage variance is a pattern, not a coincidence.

Mix, when there is more than one thing

With more than one product, a fourth effect appears and it is the one nobody looks for.

Sell exactly the planned number of units, at the planned prices, at the planned costs — but a different blend of them, weighted towards the low-margin product. Volume is on plan. Prices are on plan. Profit is down.

That is the mix variance, and it is invisible in any report that tracks total units or total revenue. It appears as a margin percentage falling for no reason anybody can name, and the reason is that the question was never asked about the composition.

The same effect applies to costs: the same total hours at the planned rates, but more of them from the expensive grade.

Which variances are worth investigating

Investigating a variance costs somebody’s afternoon, so the test matters.

Chase it if it is large, controllable and unexplained. All three: large alone is usually phasing, controllable alone is usually small, and explained already needs no work.

Also chase it if it is small but repeats every month. A €900 adverse variance appearing eleven months running is not eleven events. It is a structural error — a budget set wrong, a rate that changed, an allocation misdirected — and it is €9,900 that a size threshold will never surface.

Do not chase it if it is understood. A line 20% over since January because the phasing is flat and the spend is annual does not need explaining again in July.

The second of these is the one that gets missed, because reports sort by size and structural errors hide under the threshold by design.

Worked example: the whole split

A support team. Budget €100,000 for 1,000 tickets. Actual €118,000 for 1,250 tickets. Variable cost budgeted at €100 a ticket.

Step 1 — flex.

€
Original budget, 1,000 tickets 100,000
Flexed budget, 1,250 tickets 125,000
Actual 118,000
Volume variance (25,000)
Spending variance 7,000

Step 2 — split the spending variance. Budget was 2.0 hours a ticket at €50; actual was 2.1 hours at €45.

Calculation €
Rate (50 − 45) × 2,625 hours 13,125 F
Usage (2,500 − 2,625) × 50 6,250 A
Net spending 6,875 F

The €125 difference from the €7,000 above is rounding on the flexed rate; leave it, and do not spend an hour on it.

Step 3 — read it. The team handled 25% more tickets. They did it with cheaper staff — €45 an hour against €50 — who each took 5% longer per ticket. Net, that saved €6,875 against what the model predicted at that volume.

Now the question worth asking, which no variance produces: is a 5% slower resolution at 10% lower cost a good trade? That depends on what the extra time does to the customer, and the numbers cannot say. What they can do is put the trade in front of somebody, which the original €18,000 adverse never would have.

Do it

  1. Take one cost line with a volume driver. Flex the budget to the actual volume and compute the two variances.
  2. Check your variable rate first — if it includes allocated fixed cost, the flex is wrong. Track 3’s high-low tutorial is how to get it right.
  3. If you can, split the spending variance into rate and usage. Note whether they point the same way or opposite ways.
  4. Then look back six months for a small variance that appears every single month, and find out what it is.

Step 4 usually finds a budget line that was set wrong at the start of the year and has been quietly wrong ever since.
