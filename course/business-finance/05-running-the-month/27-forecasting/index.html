Forecast, reforecast, and landing the year

Three objects that get confused, a cycle that gets less useful as it gets more accurate, the Q4 behaviours, and a measurement nobody takes.

Tutorial 05 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Distinguish budget, forecast and actual, and say which of them should move
  • Explain the Q4 spending pattern from the incentive that produces it
  • Score a forecast against its outturn and detect a bias

The question

In September you are asked for a full-year forecast. You submit the budget number, because submitting anything else invites a conversation about why you are off plan.

Everyone does this, everyone knows everyone does this, and the resulting forecast is worthless. This tutorial is about why the incentive is built that way and what the alternatives look like.

Budget, forecast and actual are three different objects

They get used interchangeably and they are not the same kind of thing at all.

Budget is fixed. It is what you committed to in December and it does not change during the year — that is what makes it a benchmark.

Forecast moves. It is the current best estimate of where the year ends, revised as facts arrive. A forecast that never moves off the budget is not a forecast; it is the budget with a different column heading.

Actual is fixed and backward-looking. What the ledger recorded.

The confusion has a cost. When a forecast is treated as a commitment, moving it becomes an admission, so it stops moving, and the organisation loses the one number that was supposed to tell it what is coming.

The reforecast cycle

A reforecast is actuals to date plus a fresh estimate of the rest. Most companies run one quarterly; some monthly.

Each round the closed portion grows and the open portion shrinks. Which produces a property worth noticing: the forecast becomes steadily more accurate and steadily less useful, because accuracy comes from having less left to guess and usefulness comes from having something left to change.

The November forecast is nearly exact and nearly pointless. The March one is rough and is the one that could still alter the year. Most organisations pay most attention to the November one.

Landing the year: the Q4 behaviours

Two patterns appear in the last quarter, in opposite directions, and both come from the same fact: the budget resets on 1 January.

Underspent, so spend it. Unspent budget does not carry over, and next year’s budget is set from this year’s spend, so an underspend is punished twice — once by losing the money and once by having a lower base. The rational response is to find something to buy in November.

Overspent, so defer it. Push the invoice into January, delay the start, renegotiate the milestone. It solves this year and starts next year in a hole.

Neither behaviour is about what the business needs in November. Both are responses to an annual clock, and they are the clearest everyday evidence for track 7’s argument that a budget is an incentive scheme before it is a plan.

Rolling forecasts as an alternative

The obvious fix is to stop having a hard edge at 31 December.

A rolling forecast always covers the same horizon — typically five or six quarters — and is refreshed each quarter. The window moves. There is no month where the remaining horizon is two weeks, so November stops being structurally different from June, and the use-it-or-lose-it pressure loses its deadline.

This is a central Beyond Budgeting recommendation, and it works. The honest objections are worth stating too: it is more forecasting work, not less; it does not by itself remove the annual budget, since most companies still need one for external commitments and remuneration; and if targets stay annual while forecasts roll, you have added a process without removing the incentive.

The version that changes behaviour is rolling forecasts plus relative targets. The version most companies implement is rolling forecasts alone, and it disappoints them.

Forecast accuracy is measurable, and nobody measures it

The last idea is the cheapest improvement in this entire track.

Every forecast makes a checkable claim. When the period closes, the outturn is known. Subtract. That is the forecast error, and after three or four rounds a pattern is visible.

Almost nobody does this. Forecasts are produced, discussed, and never scored, so a team that is consistently 8% low stays consistently 8% low for years.

And the finding is usually a bias rather than noise — five rounds all on the same side of the outturn. A bias is the easy case: it can be subtracted. Nobody can fix random error, and anyone can add 8%.

Keeping your own record takes a spreadsheet with two columns, and it is worth more than any improvement to the forecasting template.

Worked example: two years of forecast error

A cost centre’s September forecast against the eventual outturn.

Year Sep forecast Outturn Error
2021 480,000 511,000 (31,000)
2022 505,000 543,000 (38,000)
2023 520,000 549,000 (29,000)
2024 540,000 578,000 (38,000)

Four years, four under-forecasts, averaging 6.4% low. That is not noise; noise changes sign.

Two readings, and they call for different things.

If the cause is optimism about the run rate, the fix is arithmetic: add 6.4% to the September number and the forecast becomes usable immediately.

If the cause is that forecasting an overspend invites a difficult meeting, adding 6.4% will not survive contact with the same meeting. The fix is governance — separating the forecast from the target so that revising one is not an admission about the other.

The data cannot distinguish them, and the person producing the forecast usually can. Which is why this is worth doing privately first.

Note also which figure the pattern makes reliable. The September forecast plus 6.4% predicted the outturn within about 1% in three of the four years, which makes a systematically biased forecast more useful than an unbiased noisy one — provided somebody is measuring.

Do it

  1. Find your last four forecasts and the outturns they were predicting. Compute the error each time, with a sign.
  2. If the signs are the same, you have a bias. Compute the average and apply it to your current forecast.
  3. Ask whether your organisation’s Q4 shows either of the two behaviours. Look at December spend as a share of the year.
  4. Then ask one question of whoever owns the process: is the forecast used as a target?

If the answer to step 4 is yes, steps 1 to 3 explain most of what you found.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 5 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

  1. 1Which of the three is supposed to change during the year?

    Not quite. Prior-period actuals are closed. Reversals land in the following month rather than restating the earlier one.

    Not quite. Changing it destroys the benchmark. Re-baselining exists for that and is a deliberate act with consequences.

    Right. The budget is a fixed benchmark and actuals are a record. A forecast that never moves is the budget relabelled.

    Not quite. Only the forward-looking one is revised. That is what distinguishes it from the other two.

  2. 2Why does a reforecast get less useful as it gets more accurate?

    Not quite. Later rounds usually get more attention, not less. The loss of usefulness is structural rather than about effort.

    Not quite. Accuracy is measured against outturn. The trade-off is between certainty and the remaining room to act.

    Not quite. Closed months are not restated. What changes each round is how much of the year is still an estimate.

    Right. The closed portion grows each round, so precision rises while the scope for acting on the answer falls away.

  3. 3Why does an underspent cost centre look for things to buy in November?

    Not quite. Sometimes true and incidental. The behaviour appears regardless of what suppliers are doing.

    Not quite. Finance would generally prefer the underspend. The pressure comes from next year's base, not from a requirement.

    Right. The underspend is punished twice, so spending it is the rational response to how the annual clock works.

    Not quite. That is a real constraint on capital specifically. The use-it-or-lose-it pattern applies to operating budgets too.

  4. 4What is the honest limitation of adopting rolling forecasts on their own?

    Not quite. Reconciliation is routine — the statutory year is simply a slice of the rolling view.

    Not quite. They increase the effort rather than reducing it, since the exercise runs every quarter over a longer horizon.

    Right. Moving the horizon removes the forecasting cliff. If the target still resets each December, the behaviour survives it.

    Not quite. Most adopters keep the annual budget alongside. The gap is the target, not the budget.

  5. 5Four forecasts have all come in below outturn. What does that make them?

    Not quite. Four same-sign errors would be unusual from noise alone. The consistency is the signal.

    Not quite. A biased forecast is more useful than an unbiased noisy one, provided somebody applies the correction.

    Not quite. Under-forecasting cost is the opposite of prudent — it understates what the year will consume.

    Right. Noise changes sign. Four rounds on one side is a systematic offset, and a systematic offset is correctable arithmetic.
