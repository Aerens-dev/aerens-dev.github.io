Commitments, purchase orders and the three-way match

What raising an order actually does, the three documents that have to agree before anyone is paid, and the one click that holds up both the supplier and your month.

Tutorial 03 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Say what a purchase order does beyond authorising a purchase
  • Diagnose a blocked invoice from which of the three documents is missing
  • Say what happens to an order still open at the year end

The question

A supplier calls to say they have not been paid in eleven weeks. Finance says the invoice is blocked. You approved the work, the work was done, and nothing about the situation is anybody’s fault.

Somebody did not click a button, and this tutorial is about which button.

What raising an order actually does

A purchase order is not paperwork attached to a purchase. It does three specific things, and only the first is obvious.

It reserves the budget as committed — tutorial 19’s leading indicator. The moment the order exists, that money is spoken for.

It tells the ledger to expect an invoice of roughly that size, from that supplier, against that account and cost centre. Which is what lets the system check the invoice when it arrives instead of trusting it.

It records who approved it, before the money was spent. This is the control that matters. An invoice arriving with no order behind it means somebody committed the company without an approval, and the approval is now being sought after the fact, when refusing costs more than agreeing.

The three-way match

Before an invoice is paid, three documents have to agree:

  • the purchase order — what was asked for, at what price;
  • the goods receipt — what actually arrived;
  • the invoice — what is being billed.

Quantity and price must reconcile across all three. That is the three-way match, and it is the core control in accounts payable: it stops payment for things nobody ordered, things nobody received, and amounts nobody agreed.

Note which of the three you own. Finance never sees the goods arrive, and purchasing does not either. The receipt is yours, and so, usually, is the order.

Why an invoice gets blocked

The match fails in three ways, and they have very different fixes.

Price or quantity mismatch. The invoice is larger than the order. Somebody has to decide whether to accept it, which usually means the budget holder amending the order or the supplier issuing a credit.

No goods receipt. The order exists, the invoice exists, and nobody has confirmed delivery. The system cannot tell whether the work was done.

No order at all. The invoice arrives out of nowhere. Someone bought something without raising an order, and it now needs a retrospective one.

The second is by far the most common and the only one you can clear on your own in ten seconds. It is also the one nobody is told about, because the block sits in a payables queue rather than on your report.

Goods receipting is yours, and it holds up two things

Confirming receipt is a small action with two consequences, and both are invisible from where you sit.

The supplier does not get paid. Their invoice sits blocked, they chase, and eventually they chase you. Eleven weeks is not unusual, and for a small supplier it is genuinely damaging.

The cost does not hit your month. No receipt means no accrual trigger, so the P&L is missing a cost you have incurred. Your month looks better than it is and next month looks worse.

That second one is the reason the close asks about it. Receipting is not an administrative favour to the procurement team; it is one of the three inputs the ledger cannot generate for itself.

Open orders at the year end

An order raised in November for work delivered in February is still open on 31 December, and what happens to it is a policy decision that varies by company.

Carried forward. The commitment moves into the new year and the budget goes with it. Sensible, and it means your new-year budget is partly pre-spent.

Cancelled. The order is closed and the budget lapses. You re-raise it in January against next year’s budget — which you now need to have planned for.

Both are defensible and they have opposite consequences for your January. Finding out which applies in November costs one email; finding out in January costs a budget line.

Watch also for the reverse failure: an order left open for work that was never delivered and never will be. It holds committed budget you could otherwise use, and nothing prompts anyone to close it.

Worked example: the eleven-week invoice

Date Event State
4 March Order raised, €18,000 Committed €18,000
22 April Work completed Nothing changes
28 April Invoice received, €18,000 Blocked, no receipt
30 April Month-end close No accrual posted either
15 July Supplier calls Still blocked

Two failures, both from the same missing click.

The supplier has been unpaid since April because the three-way match has two of three documents. Nothing else was wrong: right order, right amount, right supplier.

The accounts are wrong in two months. April is understated by €18,000 because no receipt meant no cost. Whichever month it is finally cleared in is overstated by €18,000. The full year is right, which is cold comfort to anyone reading the April pack.

And note what would have caught it. An accrual would have — the work was delivered in April and tutorial 07 says to accrue delivered work. The commitments report would have shown €18,000 committed and €0 actual against a completed piece of work. Both signals existed and neither was looked at.

Do it

  1. Ask for a list of your open purchase orders with the amount and the raise date. Anything older than three months is a question.
  2. For each, ask whether the work was delivered. Receipt the ones that were, close the ones that will never be.
  3. Ask payables whether any invoice against your cost centre is currently blocked, and why.
  4. Then find out your organisation’s year-end policy on open orders, and put it in your November calendar.

Step 1 usually finds committed budget you did not know you had.
