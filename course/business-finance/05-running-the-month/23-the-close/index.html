The close: what happens between day 1 and day 6

Six days, a cut-off rule that causes almost every error, two kinds of close, and the three things only you can supply.

Tutorial 01 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Say what happens on each day of the close and where your window is
  • Apply the cut-off rule to a delivery and invoice that straddle a month end
  • Say why a management pack is deliberately approximate

The question

Every month there is a short period where the ledger for the month just ended is still open, and then it is not. Almost everything anyone finds annoying about finance — the chasing, the deadlines, the “sorry, it’s closed” — comes from that window being about two days long.

Six working days, and your window is the first two

A typical monthly close:

  • Days 1–2. Cost centre owners post accruals and receipt outstanding deliveries. This is the only part that needs people outside finance.
  • Day 3. Finance posts journals, runs depreciation, and applies standing allocations.
  • Day 4. Recharges between entities, intercompany eliminations, consolidation.
  • Days 5–6. The ledger locks, the pack is produced and issued.

Longer in a group with many entities, shorter in a small company on one system, but the shape is stable. The important part is where you sit in it: your contribution is over by the end of day 2, and after that a change is a journal request that somebody has to justify.

Cut-off is the whole difficulty

Cut-off is the rule deciding which period a transaction belongs to, and almost every closing error is a transaction landing on the wrong side of it.

The rule is not the invoice date. It is when the good or service was delivered. Work done on 29 June belongs to June, whatever the invoice says and whenever it arrives.

That sounds obvious and is routinely got wrong, because the invoice is the document that shows up in the system and the delivery is an event only the recipient witnessed. Which is precisely why the close asks you and not the ledger.

Hard close, soft close

Not every close is the same depth.

A soft close is fast and partly estimated: standard allocations, accruals on judgement, some reconciliations deferred. It is for management, monthly, and it is fit for that purpose.

A hard close reconciles everything: every balance sheet account agreed, every intercompany balance matched, provisions reassessed. It supports statutory reporting and audit, and it takes considerably longer — which is why quarter and year ends feel different and why the year-end accrual request is more insistent than the March one.

Knowing which one you are in tells you how much precision your accrual actually needs.

What the close needs from you

Three things, and the reason it needs you is the same for all three: none of them exist anywhere in the system yet.

Accruals for work received and not invoiced. Covered in tutorial 07; the close is where it lands.

Goods receipts against open purchase orders — confirming that what was ordered actually arrived. This is tutorial 25’s subject and it is the one most often skipped.

Corrections to anything coded to the wrong account or centre, while the period is still open.

Everything else — payroll, depreciation, standing recharges, invoices already posted — finance can do without asking anyone. The two-day window exists for exactly these three.

Why the pack is a bit wrong, and why that is fine

A management pack is approximate on purpose, and it is worth understanding the trade rather than being irritated by it.

Accuracy improves with time — more invoices arrive, more reconciliations complete, more estimates get replaced by facts. But the value of the information falls with time much faster, because a month-end figure is only useful while there is still something to do about it.

So the close is deliberately cut where the curve crosses: about one per cent wrong on day six beats exact on day twenty. The residual error washes out next month when the accruals reverse.

This is also the answer to “why can’t you just wait for the invoice”. Waiting for all of them means a pack in the third week of the following month, by which point half the year’s remaining decisions have already been made without it.

Worked example: four items on 30 June

Item Delivered Invoice Which month, and why
Contractor, 3 weeks June 12 July June — accrue it
Software renewal, 1 Jul–30 Jun 1 July 20 June July onwards — prepay it
Hardware, arrived 2 July July 28 June July — the invoice is early
Consultancy, invoiced monthly in arrears June 5 July June — accrue it

Three of the four have an invoice date in the wrong month, in both directions. The one people get wrong most often is the third: an invoice dated 28 June for something that arrived on 2 July belongs to July, and if it is posted on receipt it overstates June and understates July.

The consultancy row is worth noting too. Monthly-in-arrears billing means there is always an accrual — every single month, for the same supplier, at roughly the same amount. That is the easiest accrual in the world to get right and one of the most commonly forgotten, because nothing prompts it.

Do it

  1. Find out your organisation’s actual close calendar and when the ledger locks. Ask for the working-day number, not a date.
  2. List every supplier who bills you monthly in arrears. Each one needs an accrual every month; set a recurring reminder for day 1.
  3. For last month, find one cost you believe landed in the wrong period and work out which side of the cut-off it should have been.
  4. Then ask finance whether your quarter ends are hard closes, and what additional they need from you at those.

Step 2 takes ten minutes and removes most of the accrual work for the rest of your career at that company.
