Reading the monthly pack

Read it backwards, treat the phasing as an assertion, and understand that a variance has two possible causes and the report cannot say which.

Tutorial 02 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Read a monthly pack in the order that answers the useful question first
  • Say why a monthly variance often measures phasing rather than performance
  • Pick the two lines in a forty-line pack that are worth discussing

The question

Forty lines, four columns, one hour of meeting. Most of the hour goes on the largest number on the page, which is usually a recharge nobody controls, and the one line that has genuinely changed direction goes unmentioned.

Read it in the opposite order to how it is printed

The pack is printed in the order the ledger produces it: line detail, then month, then year to date, then — if you are lucky — a full-year view.

Read it the other way.

Start at the full-year forecast, because it is the only figure anyone can still act on. If it lands where it should, most of what happened this month is noise. If it does not, that is the question, and everything above it exists to explain why.

Then work down: which part of the year-to-date position produced that forecast, which month drove the year-to-date, which lines drove the month. Four steps, and you stop as soon as you have the answer — which is often at step one.

Phasing: a budget spread twelve ways is a claim

An annual budget becomes a monthly one by phasing, and the default phasing is one twelfth per month.

That default is an assertion that the spend is even, and most spend is not. Annual licences land in one month. Recruitment is seasonal. A project ramps. Holiday months are quiet.

So a monthly variance against a flat phasing is measuring the phasing at least as much as the performance. The April overspend on a licence renewal is not an overspend; it is a phasing error, and it will reverse across the remaining months.

Two consequences. Spend a little time phasing the lines you know are lumpy — it makes eleven monthly conversations shorter. And when you see a variance, check the phasing before you explain the spend.

Month, year to date, forecast: three different questions

The columns are not three views of the same thing.

Month asks what just happened. It is the noisiest and it is where phasing errors are largest.

Year to date asks where the year stands. Phasing errors partly wash out, which makes it a better read on whether there is a real problem.

Forecast asks where the year will end. It is the only one still actionable, and it is the only one that contains a judgement rather than a record.

Most monthly meetings spend most of their time in the first column, which is the least informative and the least actionable of the three.

A variance is a difference from a plan, not from reality

This is the idea that changes how the pack reads.

A variance is the gap between what happened and what was planned. So an unusual variance means one of two things: the spend was wrong, or the plan was wrong. The report contains no information about which.

That reframes the first question in any variance discussion. Not “why did you spend that” but “what did the plan assume”. Very often the answer is that the plan assumed a headcount that was never hired, a project that started three months late, or a price that was set eighteen months ago.

None of those are spending problems, and all of them show up as spending variances.

The two lines worth your time

In a forty-line pack, two categories deserve attention.

The largest controllable variance. Controllable, from tutorial 16 — filter out recharges and allocations first, or you will spend the meeting on a headcount driver.

Anything that has changed direction. A line that was on plan for five months and is now moving is more interesting than a line that has been 20% over since January, because the second is a known phasing or budgeting error and the first is news.

That second one is underrated. Size attracts attention and direction carries information, and packs are almost always sorted by size.

Worked example: a month that looks bad and is not

Cost centre 4200, April. Budget €40,000 a month, flat.

Line Month budget Month actual Variance What it is
Salaries 24,000 23,100 900 A leaver, not yet replaced
Contractors 4,000 5,200 (1,200) Covering the leaver
Licences 1,500 14,800 (13,300) Annual renewal, paid in April
Travel 1,000 400 600 Quiet month
IT recharge 6,500 7,900 (1,400) Headcount driver, rose in March
Facilities 3,000 3,000 — Flat allocation
Total 40,000 54,400 (14,400)

A 36% overspend, and almost none of it is a problem.

€13,300 is the licence, phased flat but paid annually. Over the year the line is on budget; if it were phased properly, April would show €14,800 against €14,800 and every other month €0 against €0.

€1,400 is a recharge on a headcount driver that moved in a different department. Not controllable, per tutorial 16.

€1,200 of contractors against €900 of salary saving is the only genuinely controllable movement in the month, and it nets to €300 adverse — for covering a vacancy, which is a decision somebody made deliberately.

So the honest summary is: one line €300 over, everything else phasing and allocation. That is a thirty-second update, and the pack as printed invites a forty-minute one.

Do it

  1. Take your last pack and phase one lumpy line properly — an annual renewal, a seasonal cost. Recompute the monthly variances.
  2. Split the pack into controllable and not, per tutorial 16, and recompute the total variance on the controllable half only.
  3. Find one line that has changed direction in the last two months, regardless of size.
  4. Then write the three-sentence version of the month and compare it to what was actually discussed.

If step 4’s two versions differ a lot, the pack is running the meeting.
