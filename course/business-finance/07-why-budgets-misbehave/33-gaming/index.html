Business Finance · Why budgets misbehave

Gaming: sandbagging, use-it-or-lose-it, the hockey stick

A catalogue of behaviours everyone recognises, and the single observation that explains all of them at once.

Tutorial 01 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Recognise the four common gaming patterns from their signature in the data
  • Say what each one borrows from and what it costs later
  • Explain why replacing the people does not change the behaviour

The question

Every organisation has the same four behaviours, and every organisation explains them as a problem with the people involved.

They are not. They are what an optimiser does when you tell it what to optimise, and this track is about the reward function rather than the people.

Sandbagging: the submission is an opening bid

Covered in tutorial 18 from the process side; here it is as behaviour.

A submission is padded because a percentage cut is expected. The pad is sized to survive it. The person doing it is not being dishonest in any way they would recognise — they are producing a number that, after the standard reduction, will fund the work.

The signature in the data is a submission-to-award ratio that is stable year after year at the same percentage. If your organisation cuts 12% every year and has done for six years, everyone submitting knows it.

Use it or lose it, and the December spend

Unspent budget lapses. Next year’s budget is set from this year’s spend. So an underspend costs the money and costs the base, and the rational response is to find something to buy before the year closes.

The signature is unmistakable: a final month at two or three times the run rate, every year, on lines that are otherwise flat. Software licences bought in November for a January start. Training booked and not attended. Equipment ordered against a specification written that week.

Note that everybody involved knows exactly what is happening. Finance sees the December spike; the budget holder knows why they are spending; the executive knows the base is set from spend. It persists because no single participant can stop doing it unilaterally without being punished for it.

The hockey stick

The same mechanism on the revenue side. Commission and quota are measured to a quarter boundary, so deals are pulled into the last two weeks of the quarter — by discounting, by pressure, by whatever it takes to get the signature before the date.

The signature is revenue by week: eleven quiet weeks and two enormous ones, repeating every quarter for years. Customers do not buy in that pattern. Commission schemes do.

The cost is real and delayed. Deals pulled forward are discounted, which permanently lowers the price. And they are pulled from the following quarter, so the following quarter starts short and needs a bigger pull, which is where the pattern gets worse over time rather than staying stable.

Pull revenue forward, push costs back

The two are the same manoeuvre in opposite directions, and once you see that, the catalogue collapses into one item.

Pull revenue forward. Discount to close early, ship before it is ready, recognise on a technicality.

Push costs back. Delay a start, defer an invoice, ask a supplier to bill in January, postpone maintenance.

Both borrow from the next period. Both therefore have to be repeated, and repeated larger, because the next period begins with a hole the previous one made. That escalation is the diagnostic: a one-off is a judgement call, and a pattern that grows is a system.

None of it is cheating — it is the reward function

The observation that ties the tutorial together.

Every behaviour here maximises the stated measure. Sandbagging maximises funded budget under a percentage cut. December spend maximises next year’s base. The hockey stick maximises commission under a quarterly boundary. Each is what a rational participant does given the rules.

Which has a testable consequence: change the people and the behaviour comes back. Replace a sales team and the hockey stick returns within two quarters, because the scheme did not change. Replace a budget holder and December still spikes.

That is why this track is about incentives rather than ethics, and why the next tutorial is Jensen’s argument that the problem is arithmetic rather than character.

Worked example: reading the signatures

Four charts and what each one is telling you.

Pattern in the data Almost certainly
Submission cut by 11–13% every year for five years Everyone pads to survive a known cut
December spend at 3× the monthly run rate Budget lapses and sets next year’s base
60% of quarterly revenue in the last two weeks Commission measured to a quarter boundary
Q1 always weakest, and getting weaker Deals pulled from it every Q4, escalating

The fourth row is the one worth dwelling on, because it is the escalation made visible. If Q4 pulls from Q1, then Q1 starts short, and the same team has to pull even harder from Q2 to make Q1’s number. Run that for three years and the distortion grows every cycle rather than staying flat.

That is also the test for whether a behaviour is a one-off or a system. A single strong quarter-end is a team closing deals. A pattern that gets more extreme each year is a reward function nobody has adjusted.

And the fix for all four is the same shape and it is not a memo about integrity. It is: change the boundary, or change what is measured at it.

Do it

  1. Get monthly spend for one cost centre over two years. Plot it. Look at the final month of each year.
  2. Get the submission and the final budget for three years. Compute the reduction each time and see whether it is stable.
  3. If you can see revenue by week, plot one year. Look for the quarter-end shape.
  4. Then pick the one pattern that is clearest and write down, in one sentence, what measure would have to change for it to stop.

Step 4 is the whole exercise. If you cannot name the measure, you have found a behaviour rather than an incentive, and it is worth looking again.
