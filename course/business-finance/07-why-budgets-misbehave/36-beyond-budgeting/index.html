Beyond Budgeting, and the honest objections to it

One number doing three incompatible jobs, the proposal to separate them, what the adopters actually changed, and the three objections worth taking seriously.

Tutorial 04 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • State the three jobs an annual budget is asked to do at once
  • Say what separating them changes about the forecast
  • Give the three honest objections and name the one that usually stops it

The question

Everything in this track has been a criticism. This tutorial is the constructive one — and it is worth reading with some scepticism, because the literature on Beyond Budgeting is written by advocates and the objections are usually left to the reader.

The diagnosis: one number, three jobs

The central insight is a good one and it does not require agreeing with anything that follows from it.

An annual budget number is asked to be three things at once:

  • A target. It should be ambitious, or it motivates nothing.
  • A forecast. It should be accurate, or planning on it is worthless.
  • A resource allocation. It should be sufficient, or the work cannot be done.

These pull in different directions. An ambitious target is by construction not the most likely outcome, so it is a bad forecast. An accurate forecast is not motivating. A sufficient allocation invites padding, which corrupts both others.

No single number can be all three, so the annual budget compromises on all three — and everybody then works around the compromise, which is track 7 so far.

Separate the target, the forecast and the allocation

The proposal follows from the diagnosis: stop asking one number to do three jobs. Run three processes.

The target can be ambitious, and relative rather than absolute.

The forecast can be unbiased, because nothing is riding on it. This is the change with the largest practical effect: once the forecast is not also a commitment, revising it stops being an admission and it starts being useful.

Resource allocation can happen when resources are needed, rather than being fixed twelve months ahead — which also removes the use-it-or-lose-it pressure, because there is nothing to lose at 31 December.

Whether or not an organisation goes further, this separation is the part worth stealing. A forecast that nobody is judged on is a different instrument from one that doubles as a promise.

Relative targets instead of fixed ones

A fixed target is set months before the year and is then either trivially met or unreachable, depending on conditions nobody controls. In a boom, “grow 8%” is free money. In a downturn it is unreachable by March, and tutorial 34’s threshold argument takes over from there.

A relative target compares against something that moves with the same conditions: peers, the market, the same period last year adjusted for volumes, or other units in the same group.

“Beat the market by two points” is hard in every year and impossible to make free. It also removes the negotiation Jensen objected to, because there is nothing to negotiate — the target is set by what happens.

What the adopters actually did

Worth being precise here, because the popular version is “they abolished the budget” and that is not what happened.

The organisations most often cited moved to quarterly rolling forecasts, set relative targets — frequently determined after the period rather than before — and released resources on demand against a case rather than allocating twelve months ahead.

They kept a financial plan. Every organisation of any size needs an aggregate view of what it expects to spend and earn, for external commitments if nothing else.

So the accurate description is separation, not abolition. That is a smaller claim and a much more defensible one.

The honest objections

Three, and the advocacy literature tends to be light on all of them.

It is more work, not less. Quarterly rolling forecasts over a five-quarter horizon is more forecasting than one annual budget, not less. The pitch is better decisions, not lower effort, and organisations that adopt it expecting relief are disappointed.

External commitments still need an annual number. Listed companies guide the market. Lenders have covenants. Groups consolidate. The annual figure does not disappear; what changes is whether it is also the internal target.

Relative targets need a comparator you trust. This is the one that stops most attempts. Who are the peers? Is the market data timely and comparable? What happens when the whole sector has a bad year — do bonuses pay out for losing less than everyone else? Each has an answer and none is clean, and a badly chosen comparator is worse than a fixed target because it is arbitrary and unpredictable.

Worked example: what a mid-size company could actually change

Not a transformation. Four changes, in increasing order of difficulty.

Change Difficulty What it fixes
Stop calling the forecast a commitment Low The forecast starts moving again
Let unspent budget carry across the year end Low Most of the December spike
Move to quarterly rolling forecasts Medium The Q4 cliff, the stale horizon
Make bonus targets relative High The negotiation Jensen objected to

The first two are nearly free and address a large share of what track 7 has described. The first in particular costs nothing at all: it is a decision about how a number is talked about in a meeting, and it directly attacks the “submit the budget as the forecast” behaviour from tutorial 27.

The fourth is where the difficulty concentrates, and it is worth attempting only if the comparator question has a good answer. A company with genuinely comparable public peers has one. A company in a niche with three competitors and no timely data does not, and should stop at the third row.

That laddering is the honest recommendation: take the cheap changes, and treat relative targets as a separate decision with its own prerequisite rather than as part of a package.

Do it

  1. Ask whether your organisation’s forecast is treated as a commitment. The test is whether revising it upward requires an explanation.
  2. Find out what happens to unspent budget at the year end. If it lapses, that is one mechanism producing one behaviour.
  3. Look at the current target and ask what it would be as a relative target. Then ask what the comparator would be, and whether you would trust it.
  4. Then rank the four changes above by what your organisation could actually do in a year.

Step 3’s second half is the real test. If no comparator survives thirty seconds of scrutiny, relative targets are not available to you yet, and saying so is more useful than admiring the idea.
