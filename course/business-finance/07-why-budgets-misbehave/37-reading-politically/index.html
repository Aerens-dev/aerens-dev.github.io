Reading a budget politically

Whose number it is, what it was built to defend, which line is deliberately vague, where the slack sits — and what this reading is actually for.

Tutorial 05 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Identify the author and purpose behind a budget line
  • Say where slack usually hides and why the first place is the biggest
  • Use the reading to ask for something that can actually move

The question

You need €80,000 that does not exist. Somewhere in a €12m budget there is €80,000 that could move, and finding it is not an accounting exercise.

This is the last tutorial in the track and it is the practical one. Everything before it explained why budgets contain what they contain; this is how to read one with that in mind.

Whose number is it

Every line was proposed by a person, at a time, for a reason. Three questions tell you more about a line than the number does.

Who proposed it, and when? A line carried forward unchanged for four years has a different status from one added in this cycle. The first has probably stopped being examined.

What did they need it to protect? Sometimes a capability, sometimes a team, sometimes a claim on future scope.

What happens to them if it is cut? This is the one that predicts how hard it will be to move.

None of this is cynicism. It is the reading you would give any other document produced by people with interests — a proposal, a design review, a job description. A budget is not different in kind.

What it was built to defend

A budget line can be doing one of several jobs, and only one of them is the job its label describes.

Buying the thing it names. The straightforward case.

Holding headcount through a lean year. A contractor line that is really a placeholder for a permanent hire that could not be approved.

Keeping a claim on scope. A small line for work another team also wants, so that when the work happens it is yours.

All three are legitimate — the second and third are how sensible people manage capability across an annual cycle that is shorter than their planning horizon. The point is not that they are illegitimate; it is that a line doing the second job will be defended with arguments about the first, and you will get nowhere arguing about the first.

The line that is deliberately vague

Precision cuts both ways, and everybody who has written a budget knows it.

A specific line — “Oracle licence renewal, €47,300, due March” — is easy to verify, easy to challenge, and impossible to spend on anything else.

A vague line — “professional fees”, “programme costs”, “external support” — survives review because there is nothing specific to attack, and can be spent on whatever turns out to be needed.

So vagueness is not laziness. It is where the flexibility is deliberately kept, and any experienced budget holder has at least one such line. When you are looking for money that can move, this is the first place to look — and when you are defending your own budget, this is the line you can least afford to have made specific.

Where the slack is hidden

Four places, in rough order of how much is usually in them.

Headcount assumed and not hired. By far the biggest, in most organisations and most years. A budget built on twelve heads with two vacancies running six months carries a large amount of unspendable-looking money that is entirely spendable.

Run rates set from a busy month. A line extrapolated from a peak carries the difference between the peak and the average, every month.

A contingency line under another name. Rarely called contingency after tutorial 20’s executive cut it; usually called something reasonable.

Multi-year phasing set early. A three-year programme phased in year one, with year two now looking different and the phasing never revisited.

The first one is worth knowing precisely, because it is both the largest and the one finance is already tracking — vacancy savings are usually a formal line in a group forecast, which means the money has often already been claimed centrally before you go looking for it.

What this reading is for

Not for accusing anybody. Every behaviour in this track is a sensible response to a system, and treating a colleague’s protected line as a moral failing is both unfair and tactically useless.

It is for three things.

Knowing which arguments are real. If a line is defending headcount, an argument about whether the tool is needed will not move it. An argument about the headcount might.

Knowing which lines can actually move. Most cannot, at any given moment. A reallocation request aimed at a line that is load-bearing for somebody’s team fails and costs goodwill.

Asking for the one that can. Which is the whole point. €80,000 out of a vague line with three months of vacancy behind it is a conversation. The same €80,000 out of a specific committed contract is not.

Worked example: finding €80,000

A €12m departmental budget, October, and €80,000 needed.

Line € Reading Can it move?
Salaries, 42 posts 4,200,000 Three vacancies since June Yes — €135,000 already saved
Cloud commitment 1,800,000 Signed three-year contract No
Oracle renewal, March 47,300 Specific, dated, committed No
Professional fees 260,000 Vague, no detail behind it Probably, partly
Programme X, phase 2 900,000 Phased in year one, slipped Yes, but it is somebody’s scope
Training 90,000 18% spent by October Yes, and it will be spent in December

Three real candidates, and they cost very different things.

Vacancy savings are the largest and the least contested — €135,000 already exists. The catch from the previous section: finance may already have counted it in the group forecast, so ask before assuming.

Professional fees is the classic. €80,000 out of €260,000 with no detail behind it is defensible on paper, and it is somebody’s flexibility, so taking it costs goodwill you may need later.

Programme X’s slippage is the largest opportunity and the most expensive politically. Phase 2 has slipped and the money is genuinely uncommitted, and it is also a claim on scope. Taking it reads as taking the programme.

The order to try them is the order of what each costs somebody else: vacancy savings, then training, then professional fees, and Programme X only if the first three fail. Which is the same ordering principle as tutorial 22’s four mechanisms, applied to people rather than to process.

Do it

  1. Take a budget you can see. For three lines, answer: who proposed it, and what does it protect?
  2. Find the vaguest line in it. Estimate what fraction of it is genuinely specified.
  3. Compute vacancy savings to date on the salary line. Then ask finance whether that money is already claimed.
  4. Then, for the next thing you need money for, write down which line you would ask for and what it costs the person who holds it.

Step 4 is the difference between a request that works and one that makes an enemy. Both take the same amount of time to write.
