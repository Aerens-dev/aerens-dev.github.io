Jensen's argument: paying people to lie

A budget tied to a bonus is a machine for producing dishonest numbers — the kinked reward curve, what happens at each bend, and the fix nobody adopts.

Tutorial 02 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • State Jensen's argument in one sentence and say why it is arithmetic
  • Say what a rational person does just below the threshold and above the cap
  • Give the three reasons a linear schedule is resisted

The question

The previous tutorial catalogued four behaviours and said they came from the reward function. Michael Jensen’s 2003 paper makes the sharper claim: the standard budget-linked bonus does not merely permit the behaviour, it pays for it, and it does so by construction.

The claim

Three sentences.

Pay depends on hitting a target. The target is set by negotiation between the person paid and the person paying. Therefore both parties are paid to misrepresent what is achievable — the subordinate to understate it, the superior to overstate it — and the number that emerges is a negotiated fiction that both sides then have to defend all year.

Jensen’s point is that this is arithmetic, not culture. Given those three conditions, the misrepresentation follows. It does not require anyone to be dishonest by disposition, and it will not be fixed by hiring people who are not.

The kinked reward curve

Look at how the bonus is actually shaped against performance.

Below a threshold — typically 80% or 90% of target — the bonus is nil. From there it rises steeply through a band. At a cap — often 120% — it stops.

So the curve is flat, then steep, then flat. Two bends. And a bend in a reward curve is an enormously strong local incentive, because a small change in performance near it produces a large change in pay.

What the kinks do at each end

Just below the threshold, with the target clearly unreachable, the bonus is gone whatever you do. So the rational move is to make this year as bad as possible and next year as good as possible: take every cost now, defer every sale, clear the decks. “Take a bath” is the standard term and it is exactly what the curve pays for.

Just above the cap, additional performance earns nothing. So the rational move is identical in direction: push the surplus into next year, where it counts again. Delay a signature, hold a shipment, let an invoice slip.

Both ends give the same instruction — move it to next year — and only the middle band rewards doing the actual work in the actual period. An organisation whose managers are mostly outside that band is paying for timing games rather than for performance.

The fix: a linear schedule

Jensen’s proposal follows directly. Remove the bends.

Pay a straight-line share of the result, with no threshold and no cap. One more unit of performance is then worth the same amount whatever the current level, so there is no point on the curve where moving a result between years pays.

He pairs it with a second recommendation that matters as much: stop using the negotiated budget as the basis for the payout at all. Pay on results measured against something the person being paid does not negotiate — an external benchmark, a peer group, a market rate.

That second half is the same instinct as Beyond Budgeting’s relative targets, which is tutorial 36.

Why almost nobody adopts it

The argument is well known, widely accepted in principle, and rarely implemented. Three reasons, and only two get said out loud.

No cap means an unbounded payout. Boards dislike a scheme with no ceiling, even when the payout is proportional to a result they wanted. The optics of a very large bonus are a real constraint.

It needs a measure nobody negotiates. Which is genuinely hard. External benchmarks are imperfect, peer groups are arguable, and a poor unnegotiated measure can be worse than a good negotiated one.

The present system suits whoever sets the targets. A negotiated target gives the setter discretion — over the number, over the adjustments, over what counts as an exceptional item. Linearity removes that discretion, and the person who would have to approve the change is the person who would lose it.

The third reason is usually the binding one and it is essentially never the one given.

Worked example: a manager at three positions

Bonus is 20% of salary at 100% of target, nil below 90%, capped at 120%. Salary €120,000, so €24,000 at target and €28,800 at the cap.

Position A — tracking at 78% in October, target unreachable.

Action This year’s bonus Next year’s
Push on regardless, land at 85% 0 Normal start
Take every cost now, land at 74% 0 Starts clean, target likely lowered

Identical this year, better next year. The curve pays for the second one.

Position B — tracking at 131% in October, cap already cleared.

Action This year’s bonus Next year’s
Book everything, land at 131% 28,800 Starts empty, target raised
Hold 11 points back, land at 120% 28,800 Starts 11 points ahead

Identical this year again, and much better next year. Same instruction.

Position C — tracking at 97%. Here every point is worth roughly €960 and moving anything to next year costs real money. This is the only position where the scheme buys what it is meant to buy.

The uncomfortable arithmetic: in any given year a meaningful share of managers are in position A or B, and the scheme is paying all of them to move results across the boundary. It is not paying them to perform, because at those positions performance and payout have come apart.

Do it

  1. Find the actual shape of a bonus scheme you can see. Note the threshold, the slope and the cap as percentages of target.
  2. Work out where the current year sits against it, and what the curve is paying for at that position.
  3. Ask what fraction of the population is likely inside the responsive band at any time. It is usually smaller than people expect.
  4. Then ask what an unnegotiated measure would look like for the same role, and whether one exists that anybody would accept.

Step 4 is where the argument usually stops, and being able to say why is more useful than repeating the criticism.
