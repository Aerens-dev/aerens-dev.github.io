Diagnostic vs interactive control

Simons' distinction between the systems you set and monitor by exception and the few you argue about in person — and which kind a budget is.

Tutorial 03 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Classify a control as diagnostic or interactive from how attention is spent on it
  • Say when using a budget diagnostically becomes dangerous
  • Choose which two or three measures should be interactive

The question

Two companies run the same budgeting process. In one it is a monthly exception report nobody reads unless something breaches. In the other it is the meeting where the executive spends two hours a month arguing about assumptions.

Same system, entirely different function. Robert Simons’ distinction names the difference and makes it a choice rather than an accident.

Diagnostic control: set it and watch the exceptions

A diagnostic control is set once, delegated, and monitored by exception. A budget, a service level, an inventory target. Somebody defines the limit, somebody else operates within it, and management attention is spent only when something breaches.

This is what most controls are, and it is a good thing. Attention is the scarcest resource an executive has, and a system that consumes none of it until something goes wrong is exactly what you want for the ninety per cent of the business that is running fine.

Its weakness is precise: it catches deviations from what you already expected. A risk nobody wrote a measure for produces no exception, and the report stays green while it grows.

Interactive control: the thing management argues about

An interactive control is the opposite. Senior management discusses it face to face, regularly, with the people who produce it. Assumptions are challenged. Data is questioned. The conversation is about what the numbers mean, not whether they breached.

That expense is the point. The purpose of interactive control is not to detect variance — it is to force the organisation to keep looking at the thing most likely to invalidate its strategy, and to surface information that no pre-defined measure would have captured.

Because it costs senior attention, there can only be a few. Simons’ observation is that companies which use more than about three interactively are not using any of them interactively; they are holding long meetings.

A budget is usually diagnostic, and sometimes should not be

In most organisations the budget is a diagnostic system: targets set in December, variance reviewed monthly, attention on the exceptions.

That is right when the environment is stable and the plan’s assumptions are holding. It is wrong when the thing that will hurt you is not on the report — a market shifting, a competitor changing model, a technology assumption going stale — because exception reporting only catches exceptions to what you expected.

The diagnostic question is “are we hitting the number”. The interactive question is “is the number still the right one to be hitting”. A budget can be run either way, and almost every organisation runs it the first way without ever having decided to.

The four levers, briefly

Simons’ full framework has four.

Beliefs — what the organisation is for. Mission, values, the reason to refuse a profitable thing.

Boundaries — what it will not do. Codes of conduct, risk limits, the things that are simply off.

Diagnostic systems — targets monitored by exception. The budget lives here.

Interactive systems — the few measures senior management works through directly.

The first two set the space; the second two operate inside it. What makes the framework useful rather than a taxonomy is that the fourth is a choice: any diagnostic system can be made interactive by deciding to spend attention on it, and most organisations have never made that decision explicitly.

Choosing which few are interactive

The practical question, and the one worth taking away.

You have forty measures and attention for three. Which three?

The useful test: which measure, if it moved, would mean the strategy was wrong? Not which is largest, not which is most volatile. Which one is a signal about the assumptions rather than about the execution.

For a subscription business that might be net revenue retention rather than new bookings. For a manufacturer it might be a lead-time or a quality measure rather than cost per unit. For most software organisations it is almost never the budget variance, which is what makes running the budget interactively an expensive default.

And the corollary: making a measure interactive means taking attention away from something else. If nothing came off the list, nothing went on it.

Worked example: the same measure, two modes

A monthly cost-centre report, run two ways.

Diagnostic Interactive
Who reads it The owner, and finance The executive, with the owner
How often discussed When something breaches Every month, in person
Question asked Why is this line over? What is this telling us?
Cost Minutes Hours, at the most expensive rate
Catches Deviations from the plan Problems with the plan
Fails on Anything not measured Volume — it does not scale

Neither column is better. They cost different amounts and catch different things, and the mistake is not choosing — which produces the common failure mode: a two-hour monthly meeting that asks diagnostic questions.

The tell is the question being asked. If the meeting is working through “why is this line over”, the organisation is paying interactive prices for diagnostic value, and the same result would come from an email.

If it is working through “what does this tell us about whether the plan is right”, it is genuinely interactive, and the budget is the right thing to spend that attention on only if the budget is where the strategic uncertainty is.

For most software organisations it is not.

Do it

  1. List the recurring management meetings you attend. For each, note whether the questions asked are about the plan or against it.
  2. Count how many measures get genuine face-to-face senior attention each month. If it is more than three or four, none of them is really interactive.
  3. Answer the test: which single measure, if it moved, would mean your strategy was wrong?
  4. Then ask whether that measure is currently diagnostic — and what would come off the list to make room for it.

Step 4 is the hard half. Adding an interactive measure without removing one is how a company ends up with eleven and none.
