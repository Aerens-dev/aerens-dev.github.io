Chart of accounts, cost centres and the org chart

The ledger has a shape, it mirrors the company, and the code chosen when the order is raised decides which argument you end up having.

Tutorial 05 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Read a cost-centre report and say which part of it the owner actually chose
  • Say what changes when the same invoice is coded three different ways
  • Explain why a miscoding is cheap to prevent and expensive to correct

The question

You are handed a cost-centre report showing €61,000 against a €48,000 monthly budget. Before arguing about it, three questions are worth more than any explanation: how much of that €61,000 did anyone in your team order, what would the same spend have looked like under a different code, and when was the code chosen.

Usually one of the three answers the whole thing.

The ledger has a shape, and it mirrors the company

Every posting in a general ledger carries at least two coordinates.

  • The account — a number from the chart of accounts, saying what the money was: 6120 consultancy, 6130 software licences, 6400 salaries.
  • The cost centre — saying whose it was: 4200, the platform team.

Larger systems add a third and sometimes a fourth — project, legal entity, and in project-driven businesses a WBS element. But account × cost centre is the grid, and everything you will ever be shown is a slice through it. A P&L is the grid summed down the cost centres; a departmental report is the grid summed across the accounts.

The chart of accounts is a taxonomy of spend. The cost-centre list is a copy of the organisation chart. When a reorganisation happens and the ledger does not follow, comparatives stop meaning anything, which is why finance departments are conservative about both.

A cost centre is an accountability boundary

A cost centre is not a bucket. It is a line drawn around spend with one person’s name against it, and that name is the entire point — it is what makes a number into a budget rather than a forecast.

This is Anthony and Govindarajan’s responsibility accounting, and it has one uncomfortable implication: an owner should only be judged on what they can influence. Almost no cost-centre report respects that, which is the next idea.

The account code decides what your spend looks like

Take one €10,000 invoice from one supplier for one piece of work, and consider three defensible codings.

  • 6120, consultancy. Hits this year’s opex. Shows up in the “external spend” line every cost-review meeting starts with.
  • 6130, software licences. Also opex, also this year, but sits in a different line that is usually treated as committed rather than discretionary.
  • 1750, capitalised development. Not an expense at all this year. It becomes an asset and reaches the P&L as amortisation across five years, so this year’s cost is €2,000.

Same supplier, same work, same money out of the bank. Three different budget pots, three different pressures, and three different conversations. Whether the third is legitimate is a real question with a real answer — IAS 38 sets conditions — and it is track 6’s subject. The point here is that the code is a choice, and it is consequential.

Part of your cost centre arrives from somewhere else

A significant share of what appears under your name was never ordered by anyone in your team: shared IT, facilities, group overhead, internal service charges. These arrive as allocations or recharges, usually monthly, usually on a driver like headcount or floor area, and usually without warning.

Two practical consequences. First, when a report is over, check the recharge lines before checking anything you control — the movement is often there. Second, the driver is the thing worth arguing about, not the amount. An allocation on headcount cannot be reduced by spending less; it can only be reduced by employing fewer people, which is not the lever anyone intended.

Coding happens once, early, by whoever raises the order

The account and cost centre are usually set when a purchase order is raised — frequently by someone who is neither the budget owner nor an accountant, working from a dropdown, months before anyone reads a report.

By the time a miscoding is visible, the invoice has been posted, the month has closed, and correcting it means a journal entry, an approval, and a conversation. It is genuinely cheap to get right at the order and genuinely tedious afterwards.

So the leverage is at the front. Knowing which code a piece of work should carry before raising the order is worth more than any amount of variance analysis after it.

Worked example: the €61,000 month

Line € Ordered by the team?
6400 Salaries 31,000 No — set by hiring, not by the month
6120 Consultancy 9,500 Yes
6130 Licences 4,500 Partly — annual renewal, decided last year
6210 Travel 1,200 Yes
9100 IT recharge 8,900 No — headcount driver
9200 Facilities 5,900 No — floor area driver
Total 61,000

Against a €48,000 budget, that is €13,000 over. Now split it: €10,700 of the spend is recharges the team did not order, and the budget was set at €7,000 for them — so €7,800 of the overspend is allocation, driven by a headcount number that rose when two people joined in a different department’s reorganisation.

The genuinely discretionary spend is €10,700, against a €12,000 budget. The team is €1,300 under on everything it controls.

That is a completely different meeting, and getting to it took one column.

Do it

  1. Pull the most recent cost-centre report you have access to. Add a column: did anyone here choose this, this month?
  2. Total the “no” rows. That is the share of your budget that is not a management question.
  3. Find one recharge line and ask what its driver is. Headcount, floor area, revenue share, or a flat split — each fails differently.
  4. Then look at one PO your team raised last month and check the code against what the work actually was.

If you cannot find out what a recharge’s driver is by asking twice, that is itself the finding.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 5 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

  1. 1What is the relationship between the cost-centre list and the organisation chart?

    Right. Cost centres mirror reporting lines. Move the lines without moving the ledger and this year stops comparing to last year.

    Not quite. Auditors test the figures rather than design the ledger. The structure is the company's own, and it tracks the org chart.

    Not quite. Those are the two separate coordinates. Accounts classify what was spent; cost centres say which part of the business spent it.

    Not quite. The budget is loaded onto cost centres, not the other way round. The structure is deliberately slower-moving than that.

  2. 2What turns a number on a cost centre into a budget rather than a forecast?

    Right. Responsibility accounting rests on the name. Without somebody accountable, the figure is a prediction with no consequence.

    Not quite. Phasing decides when it is measured, not who answers. A monthly split with no owner is still a forecast.

    Not quite. Approval makes it authorised money, which matters. It still needs an owner before anyone can be held to it.

    Not quite. Loading it makes it reportable. What makes it a budget is that a specific person is answerable for the variance.

  3. 3The same €10,000 invoice is coded 6120 consultancy in one company and 1750 capitalised development in another. What differs?

    Not quite. The bank pays €10,000 either way, on the same day. What the code changes is when the cost reaches the P&L.

    Not quite. The supplier issues the same invoice regardless. The classification happens inside the buyer's ledger, after the fact.

    Right. Capitalising moves the spend off this year's P&L and releases it over the asset's life, so the current-year charge is a fraction.

    Not quite. Amortisation is how the €10,000 reaches the P&L, not an extra charge. The total cost is the same in both companies.

  4. 4A cost centre is over budget and the movement is in an IT recharge driven by headcount. What is worth arguing about?

    Right. A headcount-driven allocation only falls if you employ fewer people, which is not the lever anyone meant to pull.

    Not quite. Monthly allocation is normal and spreads the charge sensibly. The problem is which measure the share is computed from.

    Not quite. It may have, but your share moved because the driver moved. Attacking the total misses what actually changed here.

    Not quite. Recharges within one entity are an internal allocation, not an entity question. The driver is what determines your share.

  5. 5Why is a miscoded purchase expensive to fix but cheap to prevent?

    Not quite. Only the actual posting is wrong; the budget was set separately. The cost is in the delay, not in the number of places.

    Right. By the time it appears on a report the period has closed, so correcting it needs a journal, an approval and a conversation.

    Not quite. The total is unaffected — the money moves between lines. The expense is the correction process after a closed period.

    Not quite. The supplier is not involved — the code is internal. What makes it costly is that the correction comes after the close.
