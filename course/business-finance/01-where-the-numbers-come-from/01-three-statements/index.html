The three statements and how they lock together

Why there are three and not one, and the two hard joints that stop them being three independent opinions.

Tutorial 01 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Say which question each statement answers, and which it cannot
  • Point at the two numbers that tie the three statements to each other
  • Explain a report showing an underspend, an overspend and a rising bank balance at once

The question

Your project report shows €40,000 underspent. Finance says you are over. The bank balance went up. Nobody is lying and nobody has made an arithmetic error — the three claims are measured against three different things, over three different windows, and only one of them is about cash at all.

Everything else in this course depends on being able to say which of the three you are looking at. That is what this tutorial is for.

Three questions, three statements

Each statement answers exactly one question, and refuses the other two.

  • The profit and loss account — did we make money over a period?
  • The balance sheet — what do we own and owe at one instant?
  • The cash flow statement — where did the cash actually move over that same period?

The shape difference matters more than the content difference. Two of them cover a span. One is a photograph.

The balance sheet is an identity, not a finding

Assets = liabilities + equity. This is not a discovery about the company; it is the definition of equity. Equity is the residual — whatever is left of the assets once everyone else’s claim is subtracted.

So a balance sheet that balances tells you the bookkeeping closed. It is not evidence that the assets are worth what they say, that the provisions are big enough, or that anyone has counted the inventory this year.

Profit lands in equity

The joint between the P&L and the balance sheet is one number in one place: net profit for the period is added to retained earnings.

That is the whole connection. If the closing equity did not move by the profit (less any dividend, plus any share issue), something else happened and it is worth knowing what.

Cash flow reconciles the other two

The cash flow statement exists because profit is not cash. It starts at net profit and applies three corrections:

  1. Add back non-cash charges — depreciation above all. A cost on the P&L that moved no money this year.
  2. Adjust for working capital — a sale on credit is revenue now and cash later. The gap sits in receivables and has to be taken out.
  3. Split off investing and financing — buying a machine is cash out and no cost; the cost arrives later, spread, as depreciation.

The closing figure is the change in the bank balance, and it must equal the movement in the cash line on the balance sheet. If it does not, the statement is wrong. That check is why the third statement is worth having.

A period only means something between two instants

A P&L is the difference between two balance sheets. On its own it tells you what changed and hides what it changed from — €96,000 of profit is a different fact for a company holding €80,000 of equity than for one holding €8 million.

This is the habit worth building: never read a period statement without the instant at each end of it.

Worked example: one year at Vermeersch BV

A distributor, one year, in euro.

Vermeersch BV €
Revenue 1,200,000
Net profit 96,000
Cash at 1 January 120,000
Cash at 31 December 79,000

Profit of €96,000 and €41,000 less cash. The bridge:

The bridge €
Net profit 96,000
Add back depreciation 60,000
Increase in receivables (130,000)
Increase in payables 18,000
Cash from operations 44,000
Purchase of equipment (85,000)
Change in cash (41,000)

Read it back. The company was profitable and still drained the bank, and it did so in two entirely ordinary ways: it sold more on credit than it collected (€130,000 stuck in receivables), and it bought €85,000 of equipment, of which only €60,000 of depreciation — mostly from older assets — hit the P&L.

Now the joints. Opening equity was €310,000; closing equity is €406,000, which is €310,000 + €96,000 with no dividend. The cash line on the balance sheet went from €120,000 to €79,000, which is the €(41,000) the bridge ended on. Both ties hold, so the three statements are describing one company.

Do it

Fifteen minutes, on real numbers rather than these.

  1. Take the most recent full-year accounts you can reach — your employer’s filed accounts, or any listed company’s annual report.
  2. Find net profit on the P&L. Find retained earnings on the balance sheet for this year and last. Check the movement. If it does not equal profit, find the dividend or the reserve transfer that explains the rest.
  3. Find the closing cash on the cash flow statement and the cash line on the balance sheet. They must be the same number.
  4. Then the interesting one: is cash from operations bigger or smaller than net profit, and which line in the bridge explains most of the gap?

Step 4 is the whole skill. The answer is usually working capital or depreciation, and knowing which tells you more about the business than the profit figure does.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 5 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

  1. 1A colleague reads the balance sheet to see how the quarter went. What is wrong with that?

    Right. It is a photograph taken on one date. Performance over a span is what the P&L and the cash flow statement are for.

    Not quite. Filing frequency is not the problem — plenty of companies produce a quarterly one. The problem is that it describes an instant rather than a period.

    Not quite. True that revenue is not on it, but that is a symptom. It carries no period figures at all, because it is not about a period.

    Not quite. It is prepared on exactly the same accrual basis as the P&L. What differs is that it reports a position, not a movement.

  2. 2Why is a balance sheet that balances not reassuring on its own?

    Not quite. It is the other way round, and weakly at that: balancing says the postings closed. It tests no valuation, no provision and no stock count.

    Not quite. An auditor does no such thing — they test the figures, they do not plug them. It balances because equity is defined as the residual.

    Not quite. Pre-tax profit lives on the P&L, so nothing is being hidden here. The reason balancing means little is that equity is a residual.

    Right. Assets = liabilities + equity is the definition of equity, not a discovery about the company. Balancing shows the bookkeeping closed and nothing more.

  3. 3Closing equity rose by less than the year's net profit. What should you look for first?

    Not quite. Receivables are an asset, and a rise in them does not touch equity at all. It costs cash, which is a different statement.

    Right. Profit is added to retained earnings, and a distribution is what usually takes some back out again. Find it before assuming an error.

    Not quite. Tax is deducted before net profit, not after retained earnings. The profit that reaches equity is already the post-tax figure.

    Not quite. Depreciation is a cost on the P&L, so it is already inside the profit figure. It never touches equity a second time.

  4. 4What must the closing figure of the cash flow statement equal?

    Not quite. Retained earnings is where profit lands, not where cash lands. The cash flow statement ties to the cash line instead.

    Right. That tie is the point of the statement. If the bridge does not land on the balance sheet's cash movement, the statement is wrong.

    Not quite. The working capital movement is one correction inside the bridge. The closing figure is the change in the bank balance.

    Not quite. Net profit is where the bridge starts, not where it ends. If the two were equal the statement would have nothing to say.

  5. 5Why is €96,000 of net profit not yet a fact you can judge?

    Not quite. The P&L covers one period and starts from zero each time. Retained earnings is the cumulative figure, and it sits elsewhere.

    Right. The same profit means one thing against €80,000 of equity and another against €8 million. A span needs the points that bound it.

    Not quite. Net profit is the post-tax figure by definition. What is missing is the position at each end of the year, not a further deduction.

    Not quite. Audited or not, it would still be unreadable alone. The gap is the opening and closing balance sheet, not the precision.
