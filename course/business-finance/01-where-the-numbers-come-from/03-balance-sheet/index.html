The balance sheet as a snapshot of claims

Read it as an ordered list of claims rather than a valuation, and the twelve-month line does most of the work.

Tutorial 03 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Read a balance sheet in the order it is written, and say why that order exists
  • Compute working capital and say what a rise in it costs
  • Explain a fall in equity that involved no sale and no payment

The question

A company reports €4.2m of assets, €1.1m of profit, and cannot pay a €90,000 supplier invoice on Friday. Nothing about that is contradictory, and the balance sheet says so plainly — if you read it in the order it is written rather than looking for the biggest number.

The order on the page is liquidity

A balance sheet is not a list of things. It is a list of claims, sorted, and the sort is the useful part: assets run from what is slowest to turn into money down to cash, and liabilities run from what is due furthest away down to what is due first.

That ordering is why the bottom of both columns is where the trouble lives. The €4.2m of assets is mostly a building; the €90,000 is due Friday.

Current and non-current is a twelve-month line

The formal version of that ordering is a single cut at twelve months. Anything that will be settled or converted within a year is current; anything beyond is non-current, on both sides.

That one line is what makes a balance sheet capable of saying anything about solvency at all. Current assets against current liabilities is the comparison, and it is the first thing a bank looks at.

Working capital is money you own and cannot spend

Working capital = inventory + receivables − payables. It is the cash tied up in the gap between paying for something and being paid for it.

The uncomfortable part: it grows with the business. Sell 40% more and you hold roughly 40% more stock and are owed roughly 40% more — so a good year takes cash out of the bank rather than putting it in. Profitable companies fail this way more often than unprofitable ones do, because the unprofitable ones are not growing.

Book value is cost, not worth

An asset is carried at what was paid for it, less depreciation charged on a schedule chosen when it was bought. Nothing in that process consults the market.

So a building bought in 1998 sits at a fraction of what it would sell for, and a custom platform capitalised last year sits at close to full cost whether or not anyone would pay a euro for it. Under IFRS some classes may be revalued and financial instruments are marked to fair value — but for the ordinary run of property and equipment, book value is a formula, not a price.

Equity moves when either side moves

Because equity is the residual, it changes whenever anything else does — and not only through profit.

Write €300,000 off an asset that turned out to be worth less than carried, and equity falls €300,000. No sale, no payment, no cash. The same is true in reverse for a revaluation upwards. This is why “equity fell” is not by itself a statement about trading.

Worked example: why the €90,000 cannot be paid

Assets €
Building 3,100,000
Equipment 480,000
Inventory 390,000
Receivables 215,000
Cash 15,000
Total assets 4,200,000
Claims on them €
Bank loan, 7 years 2,400,000
Trade payables 340,000
Tax and payroll due 95,000
Total liabilities 2,835,000
Equity 1,365,000

Current assets are €620,000 — inventory, receivables and cash. Current liabilities are €435,000. The current ratio is 1.43, which reads as comfortable.

It is not. Cash is €15,000, and the €90,000 is due on Friday. €390,000 of the current assets is stock that has not been sold and €215,000 is money owed on 30-day terms by customers who are, on average, twelve days from paying. The ratio was measured against a year; the invoice is due in three days.

Working capital is €390,000 + €215,000 − €340,000 = €265,000. That is the sum this business has permanently lent to its own operations, and it is €250,000 more than it holds in the bank.

Do it

  1. Take a balance sheet and compute working capital. Then compute it for the prior year from the comparatives.
  2. The movement between the two is a cash flow. Which direction, and how much?
  3. Find that same number in the cash flow statement’s operating section. It is there, usually split across three lines.
  4. Last: divide receivables by revenue and multiply by 365. That is roughly how many days the company waits to be paid. Compare it to the payment terms it claims to offer.

Step 4 is the fastest read on whether a business is collecting what it sells.

  5. 5Equity fell €300,000 in a year the company traded profitably. What could have done that?
