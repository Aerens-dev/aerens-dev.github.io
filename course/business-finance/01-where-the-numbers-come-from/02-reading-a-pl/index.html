Reading a P&L

Revenue is a recognition claim, gross margin is partly a policy choice, and only one of the lines is yours.

Tutorial 02 of 05 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Say when revenue is recognised, and why that is rarely when the money arrives
  • Explain how two identical businesses can report different gross margins
  • Point at the line a cost-centre report stops on, and say why it stops there

The question

Two competitors, the same product, the same prices, the same headcount. One reports a 38% gross margin and the other 26%. Neither is lying, neither is more efficient, and the difference is entirely in where each of them drew a line that nobody outside the company can see.

Revenue is a recognition claim, not a receipt

Revenue goes on the P&L when it is earned — under IFRS 15, when control of the goods or service passes to the customer. Not when the order is signed, not when the invoice is raised, and not when the money lands.

For a box shipped from a warehouse those dates are days apart and nobody cares. For a three-year support contract billed annually in advance they are years apart, and the difference is most of what makes software accounting hard.

Gross margin, and where the line falls

Gross profit is revenue less cost of sales — the costs of delivering what was sold. Everything else is operating cost. The split is real, but the boundary is a judgement, and it is made inside the company.

An engineer who runs the production platform can sit in cost of sales or in operating costs. Move them and gross margin moves several points while the business does not change at all. This is why a gross margin is only comparable between two companies once you know how each defines the line — and why software companies argue about whether support engineers belong in it.

Operating profit is the line you are accountable for

Operating profit — EBIT — is what is left after everything the business does to run itself, and before the two things nobody in operations controls: how the company is financed, and what it owes in tax.

That is why almost every internal report stops at operating profit or above. Being charged for a decision made in the treasury department is not accountability, it is noise.

Below the line, and what “adjusted” hides

Under operating profit sit interest, tax, and the awkward items: restructuring charges, impairments, share-based payment, disposals.

Adjusted EBITDA is what you get by adding several of these back. It is genuinely useful — a one-off factory closure really does obscure the run rate — and it is also the most abused number in corporate reporting, because every add-back is a decision that a real cost should not count. Restructuring charges that appear every year for six years are not one-off.

The rule worth carrying: read what was added back, not the adjusted figure.

The P&L has no memory of cash

Nothing on the P&L is an entry about money moving. Depreciation is a cost with no payment behind it this year. Buying a €400,000 machine is a payment with no cost behind it at all — the cost arrives later, spread over the machine’s life.

So a P&L can be entirely correct and tell you nothing about whether the company can pay its suppliers next week. That is a different statement, and it is next.

Worked example: two identical resellers

Both sell €1,000,000 of hardware with €620,000 of purchase cost, and both employ four engineers costing €280,000 in total.

Line Alpha NV Beta NV
Revenue 1,000,000 1,000,000
Cost of sales 620,000 900,000
Gross profit 380,000 100,000
Gross margin 38% 10%
Operating costs 280,000 —
Operating profit 100,000 100,000

Alpha treats the engineers as an operating cost; Beta treats them as a cost of delivering the sale. Same money, same people, same profit — and a gross margin of 38% against 10%.

The operating profit line is identical, which is the point. The further down the P&L you read, the less room there is for presentation.

Do it

  1. Find the accounting policy note in any annual report — it is near the front of the notes, and it says what the company includes in cost of sales.
  2. Find one cost you would have put on the other side of that line.
  3. Now the harder one: find the reconciliation from operating profit to adjusted EBITDA. List the add-backs, and mark any that also appeared last year.

A recurring add-back is the single most useful thing on that page.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 5 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

  1. 1A customer signs a three-year contract in January and pays the first year up front. When is that first year's revenue recognised?

    Not quite. Waiting until the contract closes would understate every year but the last. Revenue follows delivery, month by month.

    Right. Control passes as the service is provided, so the revenue is spread over the period it covers rather than taken when billed.

    Not quite. Signing commits both parties but delivers nothing. IFRS 15 recognises revenue as control passes, not when a promise is made.

    Not quite. Cash received before delivery is a liability — deferred income — not revenue. Receipt and recognition are separate events.

  2. 2Two resellers with identical costs report gross margins of 38% and 10%. What is the most likely explanation?

    Not quite. Depreciation policy shifts costs between years, not between the two halves of the P&L. Gross margin turns on the classification line.

    Not quite. That would be a real cost difference, but the question says the costs are identical. What differs is where they are reported.

    Not quite. Timing changes which year a sale lands in. It does not change the ratio of cost of sales to revenue within a year.

    Right. The boundary between cost of sales and operating costs is a judgement made inside the company, and moving staff across it moves gross margin.

  3. 3Why does an internal cost-centre report normally stop at operating profit?

    Not quite. Interest and tax are published in the statutory accounts. The reason to exclude them is responsibility, not secrecy.

    Not quite. Interest and tax are estimated during the year like everything else. They are excluded because they are not the centre's to control.

    Right. Charging a manager for a treasury decision is noise, not accountability. Operating profit is the last line their choices reach.

    Not quite. Consolidation happens above and below the line alike. The cut is made where a manager’s influence stops, not where the data moves.

  4. 4A company has reported a restructuring add-back in each of the last six years. What does that tell you?

    Not quite. Auditors check the statutory figures. Adjusted EBITDA sits outside them and carries no assurance of that kind.

    Not quite. Restructuring is usually very much cash — redundancy payments and exit costs. Being added back says nothing about that.

    Not quite. It may or may not be. What the pattern shows is that the charge is habitual, which is what makes the add-back doubtful.

    Right. A one-off that recurs annually is a running cost of the business. Adding it back every year flatters the earnings figure.

  5. 5A company buys a €400,000 machine in March. How does that appear on the P&L for the year?

    Not quite. A provision is for an obligation, not for an asset you now own. The mechanism for spreading the cost is depreciation.

    Not quite. That would be the cash view. Capital spend is capitalised and released to the P&L over the life of the asset instead.

    Right. The payment is a balance sheet event. The P&L sees the cost spread across the machine's useful life, starting this year.

    Not quite. It skips the P&L on the day it is paid, but not for ever. Depreciation brings the cost back in over the following years.
