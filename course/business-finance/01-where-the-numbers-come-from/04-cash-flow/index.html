Cash flow, and why profit and cash disagree

Three sections, one bridge that starts at the wrong number on purpose, and the figure that is hardest to flatter.

Tutorial 04 of 05 · 30 minutes

  • Compute free cash flow and say what it excludes

The question

Two companies report €2m of operating profit. One generated €2.4m of cash from operations; the other generated €180,000 and borrowed the difference. The P&L does not distinguish them. This statement does, in about four lines.

Three sections, and what each one tells you

  • Operating — cash from trading. Does the business pay for itself?
  • Investing — buying and selling long-lived assets. What is being built, and what is being sold off to cover something?
  • Financing — loans drawn and repaid, share issues, dividends. Who is funding the gap?

The first section is the business. The other two are how it is being kept supplied with money, which is a different and usually shorter-lived question.

A company whose operating line is negative and whose financing line is large and positive is being funded rather than trading. That can be entirely correct — it describes most of the first five years of anything — but it should be a decision rather than a surprise.

The indirect method starts at the wrong number on purpose

Almost every published cash flow statement uses the indirect method: it opens with net profit, which is not a cash figure at all, and then removes every reason profit and cash differ.

That looks perverse and is not. Starting at profit makes the statement a reconciliation — it shows you which specific thing caused the gap, which is the interesting part. The direct method, which simply adds up receipts and payments, gives a cleaner number and explains nothing. Standard-setters have preferred it for decades and almost nobody publishes it.

Every balance sheet movement lands in one of the three sections

Here is the structural fact that makes the statement easy: the cash flow statement is last year’s balance sheet subtracted from this year’s, then sorted.

Stock up, debtors up, payables up — operating. Machine bought, subsidiary sold — investing. Loan drawn, dividend paid — financing. Every movement goes somewhere, and what is left over after all of them is the change in cash.

This is also why you can rebuild an operating cash flow yourself from two balance sheets and a P&L, which is the exercise at the end.

Capex is invisible on the P&L and brutal in the bank

Buy €400,000 of equipment on an eight-year life and the P&L sees €50,000 a year. The bank sees €400,000 in one week.

Two consequences worth carrying. First, a company can look profitable for years while its cash is consumed by replacing its own assets — the depreciation charge is a rough estimate of that replacement cost, and if it is set too low, profit is overstated. Second, comparing depreciation against capex over several years tells you whether a business is investing or quietly harvesting itself.

Free cash flow, and why it is harder to flatter

Free cash flow = cash from operations − capital expenditure. What is left after the business has paid for itself and paid to stay in business.

It is not defined by any accounting standard, so the exact formula varies — some subtract only maintenance capex, some subtract lease payments. But every version ends at a bank balance, and a bank balance is the one figure in the accounts that somebody outside the company can, in principle, count. Revenue recognition policy, depreciation lives and provisioning judgement all move profit. None of them move the bank.

Worked example: rebuilding the operating line

From the P&L: operating profit €2,000,000, depreciation €310,000.

From the two balance sheets:

Balance Last year This year Movement
Inventory 640,000 890,000 +250,000
Receivables 1,120,000 1,460,000 +340,000
Payables 700,000 640,000 (60,000)

Build the operating line:

Operating line €
Operating profit 2,000,000
Add back depreciation 310,000
Inventory increase (250,000)
Receivables increase (340,000)
Payables decrease (60,000)
Cash from operations 1,660,000

Signs are the thing people get wrong, and the rule is short: an asset going up uses cash; a liability going up provides it. Inventory rose, so cash left. Payables fell — the company paid suppliers faster — so cash left again.

Capex that year was €1,480,000, so free cash flow is €180,000 against €2m of operating profit. The business is running hard to stand still, and only this statement says so.

Do it

Twenty minutes, and it is the exercise that makes the statement stop being mysterious.

  1. Take a set of accounts with comparatives. Copy out inventory, receivables and payables for both years, and the depreciation charge.
  2. Rebuild the operating line yourself, as above. Signs first.
  3. Compare your figure to the published one. It will not match exactly — provisions, disposals and foreign exchange sit in there too — but it should land within a few percent.
  4. Then compute free cash flow, and compare capex to depreciation for three consecutive years.

If capex has run below depreciation for three years, the company is consuming assets it is not replacing, and profit is flattering it.

    Not quite. Valuation at cost is how the figure is measured. The movement in that figure is still a cash flow, and it is outward.

  4. 4A company's capex has run below its depreciation charge for three years. What does that suggest?

    Not quite. That is the flattering reading and occasionally true. Sustained across three years, under-investment is the likelier one.

    Not quite. It excludes depreciation and then subtracts real capital spend instead — the substitution matters more than the exclusion.

    Right. Revenue policy, depreciation lives and provisions all move profit. None of them move the money actually in the account.
