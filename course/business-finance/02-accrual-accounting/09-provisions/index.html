Provisions vs accruals, and IAS 37's three tests

Where judgement formally enters the accounts: three tests, the shelf for near-misses, the extra hurdle for restructuring, and why regulators watch this line.

Tutorial 04 of 06 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Place an item as an accrual, a provision, a contingent liability, or nothing
  • Apply IAS 37's three tests to a case you have not seen before
  • Say what a large release of provisions in a weak year should prompt you to ask

The question

A customer is suing you for €400,000. Your lawyers think you will probably lose but cannot say how much. A second customer is threatening to sue and your lawyers think it will go nowhere. One of these is a cost this year, one is a sentence in the notes, and the difference is worth about €400,000 of reported profit.

The line between an accrual and a provision

Both recognise a cost for something that has already happened. The difference is how much you know.

An accrual is near-certain in both timing and amount. The contractor worked fourteen days at a known rate; you are waiting for paper.

A provision is a liability of uncertain timing or amount. You know you have an obligation. You do not know when it will be settled, or for how much, or sometimes either.

That is the whole distinction and it is a spectrum in practice. The reason it is drawn at all is that provisions get a standard of their own, three tests, and a lot of scrutiny — because uncertainty is where the room to move lives.

IAS 37’s three tests

A provision is recognised only when all three hold.

  1. A present obligation from a past event. Something has already happened that leaves you obliged — legally, or constructively, because your own conduct created a valid expectation in someone else.
  2. An outflow is probable, which IAS 37 defines as more likely than not — above 50%, not the everyday sense of “quite likely”.
  3. The amount can be reliably estimated. A range is fine; you take the best estimate within it.

Fail any one and there is no provision. The first test is the one that does most of the work, because it excludes future operating losses, planned future spending, and anything you could still avoid by changing your mind.

Fail one test and it is disclosed, not posted

Something that fails a test does not vanish. If an outflow is possible but not probable, or the amount cannot be estimated, it becomes a contingent liability: described in the notes, quantified where it can be, and carried at zero on the face of the accounts.

If the chance is remote, it is not mentioned at all.

So the second lawsuit — the one going nowhere — is either a note or nothing, depending on how nowhere. The first one is a provision, at your lawyers’ best estimate. Same event type, opposite treatment, and the driver is a probability judgement made by people with an interest in the answer.

This is why the notes to the accounts are worth reading. A large contingent liability disclosure is a real number that is not in any total.

Restructuring has an extra hurdle

Restructuring provisions get their own rule because the temptation is obvious: announce a reorganisation, provide generously for it, and next year’s costs are already taken.

IAS 37 requires a detailed formal plan — the business affected, the locations, the roles, the timing, the spend — and that it has been announced to those affected, or implementation has begun, in a way that creates a valid expectation.

A board decision alone is not enough, because a board can unmake it. The obligation exists at the point someone outside the boardroom has been told.

Why regulators watch this line

Provisions are the most convenient smoothing mechanism in the accounts.

Over-provide in a strong year and profit is understated, which nobody complains about. Release it in a weak year and profit is supported by a credit that represents no trading at all. The technique has a name — cookie jar reserving — and enforcement cases against it are a recurring feature of both SEC and European regulatory action.

What to do with that as a reader: when profit holds up in a bad year, look at the provisions note. It shows the opening balance, amounts charged, amounts used, and amounts released unused. That last line is the one to read. A large release is not automatically wrong — provisions genuinely do turn out too big — but it is profit that did not come from customers.

Worked example: five items at year end

Item Provision, disclosure, or nothing?
Lawsuit, likely loss, lawyers estimate €300–500k Provision at €400,000
Lawsuit threatened, lawyers say it will fail Disclose, or nothing if remote
Next year’s expected operating losses Nothing — no past event
Reorganisation decided by the board in December, unannounced Nothing yet
Warranty claims on this year’s sales, 2% historically Provision, at 2%

Three of these are worth a second look.

Future operating losses fail test 1. You have no obligation to trade next year. You can stop. Companies used to provide for them and IAS 37 exists partly to stop that.

The unannounced reorganisation is the previous section: real decision, no obligation, nothing to post until it is communicated.

Warranties pass all three easily, and are the cleanest example of a provision. The past event is the sale. The outflow is probable in aggregate even though no individual claim is certain. And the amount is reliably estimable from history — which is what makes 2% a defensible number and not a guess.

Belgian GAAP

Belgian statutory accounts (the CBN/CNC rules) are more permissive here than IFRS: they allow provisions for certain future charges that IFRS would refuse for want of a present obligation, and the prudence principle carries more weight. If you are comparing a Belgian statutory filing with an IFRS group consolidation, provisions is one of the lines where they will legitimately differ.

Do it

  1. Find the provisions note in any annual report. It is a table: opening balance, charged, used, released, closing.
  2. Compare used with charged. If used is consistently far below charged, the provisioning runs generous.
  3. Find released unused and express it as a percentage of profit before tax.
  4. Then read the contingent liabilities note and ask what would happen to equity if the largest one crystallised.

Step 3 on a bad year is the single most informative number in a set of accounts that most people never look at.

  3. 3A lawsuit's outcome is possible but not probable. How does it appear?

    Right. It becomes a contingent liability: disclosed and quantified where it can be, but carried at zero in every total.

    Not quite. That is the treatment once an outflow is probable. Below that threshold it is disclosed rather than recognised.

  4. 4The board decides on a reorganisation in December but tells nobody. Can a provision be made?

    Not quite. They can be provided for once the extra hurdle is met. It is the lack of announcement that blocks it here.

    Not quite. A reliable estimate is the third test, not the missing one. What is missing is an obligation the company cannot reverse.

    Not quite. A decision is necessary but not sufficient. IAS 37 adds the requirement that it be announced or started.

  5. 5Profit holds up in a weak year and the provisions note shows a large release. What should you ask?

    Not quite. They will have looked at both ends. The useful question is how much of this year's profit the release accounts for.

    Not quite. A release moves no cash at all — that is part of why it is easy. Cash is the cross-check, not the thing released.
