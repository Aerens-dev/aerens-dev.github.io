Cash basis vs accrual basis, and the problem matching solves

Two ways to answer the same question, why the second one needs estimates, and evidence that the gap between them is worth reading.

Tutorial 01 of 06 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Say what the cash basis can and cannot tell you about a quarter
  • Apply matching to a cost paid in one period for revenue earned in another
  • Say what a large gap between accrual profit and cash generated implies

The question

A consultancy pays €7,000 of subcontractor invoices in March for a project it bills €10,000 for in April. On a cash basis March lost €7,000 and April made €10,000. Both statements are true and neither describes what happened, which was one project that made €3,000.

Everything in this track exists to fix that, and everything it costs — the estimates, the judgement, the room to mislead — is the price.

The cash basis tells you what happened to the bank

A cash-basis profit figure is the bank statement with the credits added up, the debits added up, and one subtracted from the other.

This is not a criticism. It has two real virtues: nobody can argue with it, and it cannot be manipulated without moving actual money. It is what very small businesses use, and it is what the cash flow statement reconstructs for a company that reports on the other basis.

Its limit is that it answers when did money move, and almost never the question anyone asked, which is did this quarter’s trading work.

Matching puts the cost in the period that earned the revenue

The accrual basis has one governing idea: recognise revenue when it is earned, and recognise the costs that produced it in the same period.

So the €7,000 does not belong to March. It belongs to April, where the €10,000 it produced is. The cash left the bank in March and that is the cash flow statement’s business; the P&L reports a €3,000 profit in April and nothing at all in March.

Every remaining tutorial in this track is a mechanism for doing that — accruals for costs incurred before payment, prepayments for the reverse, depreciation for costs spread over years, revenue recognition for the same problem on the income side.

Accrual accounting is estimates inside a hard total

Matching cannot be done without guessing. Nobody knows on 31 December what the December electricity bill will be, how many of the outstanding invoices will go bad, or how long a machine will last.

So an accrual-basis profit is exact to the cent as a total and partly estimated in its composition. Both halves of that are true and people usually hold only one of them. The number is not made up; it is also not measured.

This is the single most useful thing to carry out of this course: when you are handed a figure, the question is not is it right but which parts of it were counted and which were judged.

Over a long enough window the two bases agree

Here is the constraint that keeps accrual accounting honest: every accrual eventually settles in cash. The estimated electricity bill arrives and is paid. The doubtful debt is either collected or written off. The machine reaches the end of its life and is scrapped.

So across the whole life of a business, total accrual profit equals total cash generated. They can differ enormously in any one year, and the difference must reverse in some later one.

That is why persistent divergence is interesting. A single bad year proves nothing; five years of accrual profit running well ahead of cash means either the business is growing hard, or the estimates lean one way.

The size of the gap carries information

This is not just intuition. Richard Sloan showed in 1996 that of two companies reporting the same profit, the one whose profit rests more on accruals than on cash tends to report lower earnings the following year — and that the market did not price it in, which is why the finding is known as the accrual anomaly.

The mechanism is unglamorous: accruals are the estimated part, estimates mean-revert, and a year that leaned on optimistic ones borrows from the next. You do not need the trading strategy to use the result. It says that reading profit alongside cash from operations tells you something that reading profit alone does not.

Worked example: the same quarter, both ways

A consultancy, Q1 and Q2.

Event When Amount
Subcontractors invoiced and paid March 7,000
Project delivered to client April —
Client invoiced April 10,000
Client pays, 30-day terms May 10,000
Basis Q1 (Jan–Mar) Q2 (Apr–Jun)
Cash (7,000) 10,000
Accrual — 3,000

Three things worth noticing.

The cash total across both quarters is €3,000, and so is the accrual total. They always agree in the end; here the end came in one quarter.

The accrual view is the only one that is a fact about the project. €3,000 is what the work made. Neither cash figure is a fact about anything except the bank.

And the cash view is the only one that tells you the business needed €7,000 of working capital for two months. That is also a fact, and an important one. The answer is not that one basis is right — it is that they answer different questions and you need both, which is why the accounts contain both.

Do it

  1. Take last month’s spend for something you own. Write down what was paid in the month and what was consumed in the month. They are different lists.
  2. Find one item on each list that is not on the other, and say which period the cost belongs to under matching.
  3. Then, on a real set of accounts: find operating profit and cash from operations for three consecutive years. Compute profit minus cash each year.
  4. If the difference is large and the same sign every year, name the line causing it. It is almost always receivables, inventory, or a provision.

Step 4 is the Sloan test, done by hand and without the statistics.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 5 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

  1. 1What is the cash basis genuinely good for?

    Not quite. Working capital is a balance sheet comparison. The cash basis shows movements, not what is parked in stock and debtors.

    Right. Every figure traces to a bank movement. That makes it hard to manipulate without moving real money, which is a real virtue.

    Not quite. Matching is exactly what it does not do — that is the accrual basis. The cash basis reports payments in date order.

    Not quite. That is the one thing it cannot do. It reports when money moved, which is often a different quarter from the trading.

  2. 2€7,000 of subcontractor cost is paid in March for a project billed €10,000 in April. Where does the cost belong?

    Not quite. Collection is later still and changes nothing. Revenue was earned on delivery in April, and the cost goes with it.

    Right. Matching puts cost and revenue in the same period. The March payment is a cash flow, reported on a different statement.

    Not quite. Time apportionment applies to something consumed over both periods, like rent. Here one project earned in one period.

    Not quite. That is the cash view, and it makes March look like a €7,000 loss on a project that made €3,000. The cash flow statement carries it.

  3. 3What is the most useful question to ask about an accrual-basis profit figure?

    Not quite. A worthwhile comparison, and it comes later. First separate what was counted from what was estimated inside the figure.

    Not quite. The arithmetic is rarely the problem in a modern ledger. What varies between companies is the judgement inside the figure.

    Right. The total is exact to the cent and its composition is a mix. Separating the two is more useful than asking whether it is right.

    Not quite. An audit tests the judgements rather than removing them. The same question about composition applies either way.

  4. 4Why must total accrual profit equal total cash generated over the life of a business?

    Not quite. Depreciation is not reversed; it simply stops. The convergence comes from settlement across every accrual, not one of them.

    Not quite. The cash flow statement reconciles them annually, but that is reporting. The convergence is forced by settlement, not by audit.

    Not quite. No standard sets such a cap. What closes the gap is that every accrual is a claim about cash that eventually moves.

    Right. The bill arrives, the debt is collected or written off, the machine is scrapped. Nothing estimated stays estimated for ever.

  5. 5Two companies report the same profit; one leans far more on accruals. What did Sloan find?

    Not quite. Funding structure is a different question entirely. The result concerns what next year's earnings do, not the balance sheet.

    Not quite. Restatement is a separate and much rarer event. The finding is about ordinary earnings reverting, not about error.

    Right. Accruals are the estimated part and estimates mean-revert, so a year leaning on them borrows from the following one.

    Not quite. Part of the finding was that the market did not price it in, which is why it is called the accrual anomaly.
