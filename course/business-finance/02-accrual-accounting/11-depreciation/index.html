Depreciation, amortisation, and the useful-life choice

Spreading a cost already paid, the two common shapes, and the estimate that quietly moves reported profit every year.

Tutorial 06 of 06 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Compute a depreciation charge under both common methods
  • Say what a change in useful life does to profit and why it is not cash
  • Distinguish depreciation from impairment, and say what triggers each

The question

Two identical companies buy the same €400,000 machine on the same day. One reports €30,000 more profit than the other, every year, for eight years. Neither is wrong and neither has done anything unusual.

They picked different useful lives.

Spreading a cost you have already paid

Depreciation is not a valuation, a fund, or a cash movement. It is the mechanism that takes a cost paid once and charges it to the years that use the asset — matching, applied to something that lasts.

Two properties worth holding on to. The charges add up to what was paid, no more; depreciation cannot invent or destroy cost, only place it. And it moves no money, which is why the cash flow statement adds it straight back.

Amortisation is the identical mechanism for intangibles — software, capitalised development, acquired customer lists. Different word, same arithmetic.

Straight line and reducing balance

Straight line charges the same amount every year: depreciable amount divided by useful life. It is the default for most things and it is what you should assume unless told otherwise.

Reducing balance charges a fixed percentage of the carrying amount, so the charge is large early and shrinks. It suits assets that lose most of their value in the first years — vehicles above all — and it is the standard method in several tax regimes, which is a common reason to see it.

Both write off the same total over the same life. They differ only in the shape, and the difference is concentrated in the early years, which is precisely where a company comparing itself to a competitor will notice it.

The useful life is a choice, and it moves profit

Here is the €30,000. A €400,000 machine on a five-year life costs €80,000 a year. On an eight-year life it costs €50,000. Same machine, same cash, €30,000 of profit difference every year until the shorter schedule runs out.

The life is an estimate made by management, disclosed in the accounting policy note as a range, and revisited annually. It is genuinely a judgement — nobody knows how long a machine will be useful — and it is also a lever. Lengthening useful lives is a well-known way to nurse a profit figure, and it is visible if you compare the policy note across years.

Note the direction of the trade. A longer life raises profit now and leaves a larger carrying amount to be written off later, which is where impairments come from.

Residual value, and where the schedule stops

You do not depreciate the whole cost. You depreciate cost less residual value — what you expect to recover when you dispose of it.

And the period is your useful life, not the asset’s physical one. A company that replaces its laptops every three years depreciates them over three years, even though the machines will run for eight. The question is how long this company will get benefit from it.

Residual value is usually zero for equipment and rarely zero for vehicles or property, and like the life it is reviewed annually.

Impairment is what happens when the schedule was wrong

Depreciation is a plan made at purchase. Sometimes the world disagrees — the product it was bought for is cancelled, the market collapses, the software is replaced before it was amortised.

Impairment cuts the carrying amount in one step down to its recoverable amount, which under IAS 36 is the higher of what it would sell for net of costs and what it will generate in use. Depreciation then continues from the new, lower figure on a new schedule.

Three things about impairments. They are a charge to the P&L, so they hurt profit in the year taken. They move no cash whatever — the money was spent years ago. And they are usually the moment a bad capital decision becomes visible, which is why they arrive in clusters, late, and often with a new management team.

Worked example: the same machine, three ways

A €400,000 machine, residual value €40,000, so a depreciable amount of €360,000.

Method Year 1 Year 2 Year 3
Straight line, 8 years 45,000 45,000 45,000
Straight line, 5 years 72,000 72,000 72,000
Reducing balance, 25% 100,000 75,000 56,250

The reducing-balance figures are 25% of the carrying amount each year: €400,000 → €100,000, leaving €300,000; then €75,000, leaving €225,000.

Now the year-3 comparison. Cumulative charges are €135,000, €216,000 and €231,250. Three companies, one machine, one purchase date, and a €96,000 spread in accumulated depreciation — which is also a €96,000 spread in both reported profit and net book value.

None of it is cash. All three paid €400,000 in one week.

Do it

  1. Find the accounting policy note in any annual report and write down the useful-life ranges for each asset class.
  2. Compare them to the same note in the report from three years earlier. A lengthened range is worth a moment’s thought.
  3. Take the depreciation charge and divide it into the gross cost of property, plant and equipment. The reciprocal is roughly the average life being applied.
  4. Then find any impairment in the last five years and read what it was on. That sentence is usually the most candid one in the report.

Step 3 gives you a life you can sanity-check against the policy note, and a mismatch between them is a question worth asking.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 5 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

  1. 1What does a depreciation charge represent?

    Not quite. Loan or lease payments are their own thing and sit elsewhere. Depreciation runs whether the asset was financed or not.

    Right. It is matching applied to something long-lived. The charges add up to what was paid and no money moves when one is posted.

    Not quite. Nothing is set aside — no fund exists and no cash moves. The idea of a sinking fund is a persistent misreading.

    Not quite. Carrying amount follows a schedule set at purchase, not the market. That gap was the subject of an earlier tutorial.

  2. 2How do straight line and reducing balance differ over an asset's life?

    Right. Both write off the same depreciable amount over the same life. Reducing balance front-loads it, which shows in the early years.

    Not quite. Both are costs on the P&L. Nothing about the method changes where the charge lands, only how it is spread.

    Not quite. The life is an input to both methods and is chosen separately. The method decides the shape within that life.

    Not quite. The total is the same under both. Reducing balance simply gets there faster in the first years and slower later.

  3. 3A company lengthens the useful lives in its policy note. What is the effect?

    Right. A longer life means a smaller annual charge and a larger carrying amount, which is where later impairments come from.

    Not quite. Cash is untouched — the asset was paid for years ago. Only the reported charge and the carrying amount move.

    Not quite. The total is indeed the same, but it is spread differently. Each individual year's profit moves, which is the point.

    Not quite. The direction is the other way. Spreading the same cost over more years makes each year's charge smaller.

  4. 4A company replaces laptops every three years though they would run for eight. Over what period are they depreciated?

    Not quite. Residual affects the amount depreciated, not the period. The period is the three years the company will use them.

    Right. Useful life is the period this entity expects benefit, not the asset's physical endurance. Replacement policy sets it.

    Not quite. Disposing on a known cycle is planned, not an impairment. The schedule should have matched the policy from the start.

    Not quite. Physical life is the wrong measure. If the company disposes of them at three, the benefit to it stops at three.

  5. 5What does an impairment charge tell you about the company's cash?

    Not quite. Often the underlying reason, but the charge measures recoverable amount rather than reporting a cash shortfall.

    Not quite. No payment accompanies an impairment. That is why the cash flow statement adds it back alongside depreciation.

    Right. An impairment moves no cash. It corrects a carrying amount, and the cash consequence happened when the asset was bought.

    Not quite. It may or may not replace it. The charge itself says the carrying amount was too high, not what happens next.
