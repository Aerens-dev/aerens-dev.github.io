Revenue recognition: IFRS 15's five steps

How one signature becomes several promises, how the price is split between them, and why software contracts were the reason the standard was rewritten.

Tutorial 05 of 06 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Split a bundled contract into performance obligations and defend the split
  • Allocate a discounted price across obligations by standalone selling price
  • Say what deferred revenue is and why it is a liability

The question

You sign a customer for €120,000: a perpetual licence, four months of implementation, and three years of support. They pay it all in January.

How much of that is this year’s revenue? The answer is not €120,000, it is not €40,000, and getting to it is what the whole standard is for.

The five steps

IFRS 15 replaced a pile of industry-specific rules with one model that applies to everybody, in five steps.

  1. Identify the contract. An agreement with commercial substance, where payment is probable.
  2. Identify the performance obligations. The distinct promises inside it.
  3. Determine the transaction price. Including discounts, variable consideration, and any significant financing component.
  4. Allocate the price across the obligations.
  5. Recognise revenue as each one is satisfied.

Steps 2 and 5 do nearly all the work, and step 2 is where most of the arguing happens.

A performance obligation is a promise you could sell separately

The test for splitting a contract: is the promise distinct? Which means the customer could benefit from it on its own or with resources already available to them, and it is separately identifiable from the other promises in the contract.

So the licence, the implementation and the support are three obligations if the customer could sensibly buy them apart — and for most enterprise software they can, because the licence works without your implementation team and support is sold as a renewable line.

They are not distinct if the implementation is so entangled with the licence that it produces a combined output — heavy customisation the licence would not function without. Then it is one obligation and the whole thing is recognised together, over the implementation period.

This is exactly the judgement that makes software revenue interesting, and it is decided in the contract’s drafting more often than in the accounting.

The price is allocated by standalone selling price

The €120,000 is not assigned by picking figures. It is allocated across the obligations in proportion to their standalone selling prices — what each would sell for on its own.

List prices are €50,000, €40,000 and €60,000, summing to €150,000. The customer pays €120,000, which is 80% of that. So each obligation takes 80% of its list: €40,000, €32,000 and €48,000.

The consequence to remember: a discount is spread across everything, in proportion. You cannot decide the discount came out of support and leave the licence at full price — which was the pre-IFRS-15 game, because the licence is recognised immediately and support is not.

Over time or at a point in time

Each obligation is recognised either at a point in time — when control passes — or over time, if any one of three conditions holds:

  • the customer receives and consumes the benefit as you perform (support, a subscription, cleaning);
  • your work creates or enhances an asset the customer already controls (building on their site, upgrading their system);
  • the work has no alternative use to you and you have an enforceable right to payment for work done to date (a bespoke build).

If none of the three hold, it is a point in time, and the point is when control passes to the customer.

The licence is a point: control passes at delivery. Implementation is over time, across four months. Support is over time, across thirty-six.

Deferred revenue is what is left over

Bill €36,000 for three years of support and earn €12,000 of it this year, and the other €24,000 does not disappear. It sits on the balance sheet as deferred revenue — sometimes contract liability — and it is a liability because you owe the customer two more years of service they have already paid for.

This is where cash and revenue come apart most visibly, and why a subscription business can be flush with cash and show modest revenue. It also gives you a genuinely useful read: deferred revenue is contracted future revenue, which is why it is watched as closely as the revenue line itself.

Worked example: the €120,000 contract

Signed 1 January, paid in full 15 January. Implementation runs January to April. Support runs for three years from delivery.

Obligation List Allocated at 80% Pattern Year 1 revenue
Licence 50,000 40,000 Point, on delivery 40,000
Implementation 40,000 32,000 Over 4 months 32,000
Support 60,000 48,000 Over 36 months 16,000
Total 150,000 120,000 88,000

Support: €48,000 over thirty-six months is €1,333 a month, and all twelve fall in year 1, so €16,000.

Year 1 revenue is €88,000 against €120,000 of cash collected. The €32,000 difference is deferred revenue at 31 December, and it unwinds as €16,000 in each of years 2 and 3.

Two things follow. Cash from operations in year 1 is far ahead of revenue, which is the normal shape for a business billing annually in advance and not a sign of anything. And if the salesperson’s commission was paid on €120,000 of bookings, the commission is a cost that IFRS 15 also wants spread — incremental costs of obtaining a contract are capitalised and amortised over the same period. That detail catches a lot of people.

Do it

  1. Take a contract your employer sells — or any SaaS pricing page with tiers and onboarding fees.
  2. List the distinct promises. For each, argue in one sentence whether the customer could benefit from it alone.
  3. Put standalone prices against them, total, and work out the ratio to what is actually charged. Allocate.
  4. Then decide point-in-time or over-time for each, and compute first-year revenue against first-year cash.

The gap you get in step 4 is the deferred revenue balance, and it is the number that explains most of what looks strange about software company accounts.
