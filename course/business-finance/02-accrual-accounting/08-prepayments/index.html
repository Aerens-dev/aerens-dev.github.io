Prepayments, the mirror image

Cash out first, cost later — an asset until it is consumed, released in twelfths, and the reason your budget never sees the payment.

Tutorial 03 of 06 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Say why a prepayment is an asset rather than a cost
  • Compute the release schedule and the year-end balance for an annual bill
  • Explain why the bank and your cost centre disagree about January

The question

You renew a €12,000 annual licence in January. In February your cost centre report shows €1,000 against it. You did not pay €1,000. You paid €12,000, once, five weeks ago, and the money is gone.

Nothing has been hidden from you. What you are looking at is a prepayment doing its job, and once you can see it the whole class of “why does my report not match the bank” questions goes away.

Cash out first, cost later

A prepayment is an accrual with the arrows reversed. An accrual is cost before cash — the work arrived, the invoice has not. A prepayment is cash before cost — the money went, the service has not yet been delivered.

Matching does the same job in both directions. If the benefit belongs to the months ahead, the cost belongs to the months ahead, whatever the bank did in January.

It is an asset until it is consumed

The other half of the posting is not a liability this time. It is an asset: prepayments, sitting among current assets near receivables.

That is not a bookkeeping convenience, it is literally true. You have paid for something you have not yet had, so you hold a claim on the supplier. If they went under in March you would be a creditor for the nine months of licence you paid for and did not receive, and you would queue with everyone else.

It releases on a schedule

Each month, a twelfth of the prepaid balance is released: the asset falls by €1,000 and the P&L takes €1,000 of cost. Twelve steps, and at the end the balance is nil and the full €12,000 has been charged.

Straight-line is the default and it is right when the benefit really is even — a licence, an insurance policy, rent. It is wrong when it is not. Prepaid advertising for a single campaign in April is not one twelfth a month; it is one hundred percent in April. The schedule is supposed to follow the benefit, and the default is only a default.

Where they come from: annual billing

Prepayments are not exotic. They are the ordinary consequence of anything billed a year ahead — software licences, insurance, rent in advance, maintenance contracts, domain registrations, conference sponsorship.

Which means at any year end a company carries a prepayments balance roughly equal to the unexpired portion of everything it pays annually. It is usually one of the duller lines on a balance sheet and it is almost entirely mechanical.

Your budget sees the release, not the payment

Here is the part that matters day to day. Your cost centre is charged the release, not the payment. The €12,000 leaving the bank in January never appears in your variance at all; €1,000 appears every month for twelve months.

Two consequences.

A big renewal is not a January overspend, and if it shows as one, somebody coded it straight to expense instead of to prepayments — worth flagging, since it will also leave eleven months short.

And your annual budget for that line should be €12,000 regardless of when the invoice falls. The month the money moves is a treasury question, not yours.

Worked example: three renewals, one year end

The company’s year ends 31 December. All three were paid in full up front.

Item Paid Period covered Months left at 31 Dec
Licence, €12,000 1 January Jan–Dec 0
Insurance, €9,600 1 April Apr–Mar 3
Maintenance, €18,000 1 October Oct–Sep 9

The prepayment balance at 31 December:

Item Monthly Months left Prepaid
Licence 1,000 0 0
Insurance 800 3 2,400
Maintenance 1,500 9 13,500
Total 15,900

€39,600 was paid during the year and €23,700 of it was charged to the P&L. The remaining €15,900 sits on the balance sheet as an asset and will be charged next year.

Now the point of the example. Cash out was €39,600 and the cost of the year was €23,700 — a €15,900 difference, on three utterly routine renewals. That gap is one of the reasons profit and cash disagree, and it is the same gap the cash flow statement’s working capital line picks up.

Do it

  1. List everything your team pays for annually in advance. Licences, insurance, support contracts, anything with a renewal date.
  2. For each, write the monthly release and how many months of it are unexpired at your next year end.
  3. Add the unexpired amounts. That total is your share of the prepayments line.
  4. Then check one of them against your last cost-centre report: is the monthly figure there, or did the whole invoice land in one month?

Step 4 catches a real and common coding error, and it is easier to fix in month two than in month eleven.
