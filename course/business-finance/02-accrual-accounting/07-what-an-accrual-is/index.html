What an accrual actually is

One posting with two halves, why it reverses on the first of next month, and why finance needs an answer from you on day 2.

Tutorial 02 of 06 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Post an accrual in both halves and say what each one does
  • Say what remains in next month's figures once the invoice arrives
  • Answer a day-2 accrual request without guessing at the number

The question

On the second working day of January, finance emails asking what you received in December that has not been invoiced. You have no invoice, no exact figure, and a day to answer. The instinct is to say you do not know yet.

That answer is worse than a rough number, and this tutorial is about why.

An accrual is a posting for work already received

An accrual recognises a cost for something delivered to you in the period but not yet invoiced. The contractor worked in December; the invoice comes on 15 January; the year ended on 31 December.

Without an accrual the cost lands in January, in the wrong year, against revenue it had nothing to do with. The accrual pulls it back where the work was.

Note what it is not. It is not an estimate of future work, and it is not a guess about something that might happen. The work is done. What is missing is paperwork.

It has two halves, and the second one is a debt

Every accrual posts twice: a cost on the P&L, and an equal liability on the balance sheet, usually called accruals or accrued expenses and sitting next to trade payables.

The second half is the part people forget, and it is the honest one. Recognising the cost means admitting you owe for it. That is why the balance sheet you saw in track 1 has a line for money owed on invoices that do not yet exist.

It reverses next month, and that is the whole trick

Here is the mechanism that makes accruals safe to get approximately right.

An accrual is posted at the end of the month and reversed on the first day of the next one. When the real invoice arrives, it is coded normally, in full. The reversal cancels the estimate; the invoice replaces it.

So if you accrued €12,000 and the invoice comes at €11,400, December carries €12,000 and January carries −€12,000 + €11,400 = −€600. The only thing that survives your estimate is the amount you were wrong by.

A wrong accrual moves profit, it does not lose it

Follow that through and the reassuring consequence appears: an accrual error is a timing error. Over-accrue in November and November is understated, December overstated by the same amount, and the year is exactly right.

That is the argument for answering on day 2 with a number you are not sure of. The cost of a rough accrual is that two months are each slightly wrong in opposite directions. The cost of no accrual is that the whole cost lands in the wrong month at full size, which is a larger error in the same direction.

The exception is a year end, where “next month” is next year and the two months sit in different sets of statutory accounts. That is when the estimate deserves real work.

Why finance asks you, and asks on day 2

A month-end close runs about five working days. Accruals have to be posted before the ledger is locked, which means they are collected almost immediately.

And they ask you because the ledger has no way to know. Nothing in the finance system records that a contractor worked three weeks in December — there is no invoice, no purchase order line receipted, sometimes no purchase order. The only record is in the head of whoever commissioned the work.

So the useful answer on day 2 is not a precise figure. It is: what was delivered, roughly what it is worth, and how confident you are. Days worked × day rate is a perfectly good accrual.

Worked example: three accruals on 31 December

Item Delivered Invoice expected Accrue
Contractor, 14 days at €650 December 15 January 9,100
Electricity, quarter to 31 Dec Oct–Dec Late January 2,400
Recruiter fee, candidate started 2 Dec December Disputed 0

The contractor is arithmetic: days times rate. No invoice needed to know it.

The electricity is a genuine estimate — meter read in late January, billed in arrears. Last quarter was €2,250 and it is winter, so €2,400. If it comes in at €2,600, January carries €200 and nobody minds.

The recruiter is the interesting one. The candidate started, so the fee is owed under the contract — the fact that you are disputing the amount does not make it a non-event. Accrue your best estimate of what will actually be paid, not zero. Accruing nothing because a number is contested is the most common way a real cost lands in the wrong year.

Total accrued: €11,500, of which €9,100 is effectively certain, €2,400 is a reasoned estimate, and the recruiter fee is a judgement that should be written down somewhere so the person unwinding it in February knows what you meant.

Do it

  1. List what your team received in the last completed month that you have not seen an invoice for. Contractors, cloud overage, anything on a quarterly billing cycle.
  2. Put a number against each. Days × rate, or last period × a factor. Do not reach for precision.
  3. For one of them, write the two halves out: which cost account, and the accruals line it credits.
  4. Then look up last month’s actual invoice for something you accrued previously, and compute the difference. That difference is the whole quality of your estimate, and it is the only feedback the process gives you.

Step 4 is the one almost nobody does, and it is how you find out whether you habitually accrue high or low.
