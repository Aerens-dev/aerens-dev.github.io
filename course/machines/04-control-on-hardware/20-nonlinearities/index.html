Deadband, backlash and stiction

The three nonlinearities that eat a loop, how to tell a friction limit cycle from a real instability, and which of them control cannot fix.

Tutorial 02 of 06 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Distinguish a stiction limit cycle from an instability by shape and by direction
  • Say what backlash does to an integral term during a reversal
  • Choose between a firmware compensation and a mechanical fix for each

The question

Your position loop oscillates around the target. Small, slow oscillation — about 2 Hz, a degree either side, never settling. Textbook says too much gain, so you lower P. It gets worse: the oscillation is larger and slower.

That is not an instability. It is a friction limit cycle, and the fix is the opposite of the one the theory suggests, because the theory assumed a linear plant and your machine has a bearing in it.

Everything in this tutorial is a place where the linear model stops describing the machine. None of it appears in a transfer function, all of it appears in the plot, and recognising each one on sight saves days.

Stiction

Static friction is higher than sliding friction. Breaking a joint free takes more force than keeping it moving, and the gap can be a factor of two.

Watch what a loop does with that. The error grows, the controller builds torque, nothing moves. Torque keeps building. At the breakaway point the joint releases — and now friction has dropped, so the torque that was just enough to break free is far more than enough to move, and the load lurches past the setpoint. Now the error is negative, the same thing happens in reverse, and it repeats.

The result is a sustained oscillation, and the giveaway is the shape: flat-topped, squarish, with visible dwell at the extremes rather than the smooth sinusoid of a gain instability. And the direction of the cure is inverted — lowering P makes it slower and larger, because it takes longer to build enough torque to break free.

Backlash

Free play in a gearbox or a coupling means that on a reversal, the motor turns and the load does not, until the teeth make contact on the other face.

During that interval the loop is open. The sensor, if it is on the load, reports no change; the controller, seeing no response, pushes harder; the integral accumulates against a plant that is not connected. Then the teeth meet and all of that accumulated command arrives at once, as an impact.

Three consequences worth knowing. It limits your achievable bandwidth — you cannot control faster than the play, and pushing gain through it just makes the impacts harder. It wears the gears, since every reversal is now a hammer blow rather than a transfer. And it makes the machine noisy in a way that is often the first thing anybody notices.

Where the encoder sits changes the character of this. On the motor, the loop looks beautiful and the load does whatever it likes. On the load, the loop sees the truth and fights it. Neither is a fix.

Deadband

Below some command threshold, nothing happens at all. The causes stack up: the bridge’s dead time distorts small duty cycles, PWM resolution quantises them, and stiction eats whatever torque survives.

The visible symptom is a loop that creeps towards its target and then hunts around it. Proportional action alone cannot close the last bit of error, because the command it produces is inside the dead zone. Integral does eventually climb out, but slowly, and then overshoots because by the time anything moves the accumulated command is well past what was needed.

This one is genuinely fixable in firmware, and cheaply: measure the threshold once, then add it to the command with the sign of the command. Suddenly the smallest useful command is the smallest command that does something. The fix is about ten lines and it transforms low-speed behaviour.

Telling a limit cycle from an instability

Because the treatments are opposite, this diagnosis comes before any tuning.

Shape. A gain instability is smooth and close to sinusoidal, at a frequency set by where the loop’s phase reaches 180°. A friction limit cycle has flat tops and visible dwell — the load is stuck for part of each cycle, and stuck looks flat.

Direction. Raise P a little. A gain instability gets worse. A stiction cycle gets faster and smaller, because the loop reaches breakaway torque sooner.

Amplitude against setpoint. A gain instability has an amplitude roughly proportional to whatever excites it. A friction cycle has an amplitude set by the friction itself, so it is much the same size at any setpoint — which is a strong tell.

Where it happens. Friction cycles appear at low speed and around standstill, which is exactly where a position loop lives. Gain instabilities appear wherever the loop gain is highest.

What to do about each

Four problems, four remedies, and the important observation is that the best remedy is often not a control remedy.

Stiction — dither, a small high-frequency signal added to the command that keeps the joint microscopically moving so it never fully sticks. Or a small bias torque. Both are workarounds; better bearings are the actual fix.

Backlash — preload the gears, use an anti-backlash pair, or go direct drive. No amount of tuning removes free play, and every hour spent trying is an hour not spent on the mechanics. This is the one to take out of the control domain entirely.

Deadband — compensate in firmware as above. This one genuinely belongs to control and the fix is good.

Saturation — anti-windup, which is tutorial 22 in its entirety.

The general lesson for someone arriving from software: a control loop can compensate for a plant it can observe and influence. Free play is an interval where it can do neither, and the answer is a mechanical one.

Worked example: diagnosing the 2 Hz oscillation

The opening symptom. A geared position axis, encoder on the motor, oscillating ±1° at about 2 Hz.

Test 1 — the shape. Log position at 1 kHz and look at one cycle.
Time at each extreme 90 ms of a 500 ms cycle
Transit between extremes 160 ms
Waveform flat tops, fast transitions

Dwell of 90 ms at each end is not a sinusoid. First evidence for friction.

Test 2 — the direction. Change Kp and re-measure.

Kp Frequency Amplitude
0.8 1.2 Hz ±1.8°
1.6 (original) 2.0 Hz ±1.0°
3.2 3.1 Hz ±0.7°

Raising gain made it faster and smaller. A gain instability does the opposite. That is conclusive.

Test 3 — the amplitude against setpoint. Command 10°, 90°, 180°. Amplitude stays at ±1.0° in all three. Set by friction, not by excitation. Confirmed.

Test 4 — where is the friction? Disconnect the load and run the motor alone. The cycle disappears. So it is in the gearbox or the load bearing rather than the motor.

Now the arithmetic that decides the fix. Breakaway torque measured with a gauge: 0.34 Nm at the output. Sliding: 0.19 Nm. The ratio is 1.8, and the difference, 0.15 Nm, is what gets dumped into the load at every release.

With a gear ratio of 20:1 and Kt = 0.021 Nm/A at the motor, breakaway is 0.34/20/0.021 = 0.81 A of motor current, and the excess after release is 0.36 A. The loop’s proportional gain converts 1° of error into 0.9 A — which is just over breakaway, and that is why the cycle sustains at exactly ±1°.

Three candidate fixes, costed.

Effect Cost
Dither, 200 Hz at 0.1 A cycle gone, small audible whine 6 lines
Deadband compensation no effect — this is stiction, not deadband wasted day
Replace the output bearing breakaway to 0.08 Nm, cycle gone €14, one hour

The middle row is there because it is the plausible wrong answer: deadband compensation and stiction dither look similar and address different things. Compensation adds a fixed offset and the joint still sticks.

Take the bearing. It is the cheapest of the three in the long run, it removes the problem instead of masking it, and it makes every subsequent tuning easier.

Do it

Twenty minutes with a machine that misbehaves.

  1. Find something that oscillates and classify it by the two tests: is the waveform flat-topped, and does raising P make it better or worse?
  2. Measure your actuator’s deadband. Ramp the command up from zero and note where motion starts. Then add that offset in firmware and see how much the low-speed behaviour improves.
  3. Measure backlash directly: hold the load, rock the input, read the encoder. The number in degrees is an upper bound on your positioning accuracy and no gain changes it.
  4. Then measure breakaway and sliding torque with a spring gauge. The ratio tells you how bad the stiction problem is, and the absolute number tells you whether a better bearing is worth buying.
