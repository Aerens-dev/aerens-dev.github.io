Anti-windup, derivative filtering, feedforward

What an integrator does while the actuator is saturated, why pure derivative is unusable, and the one improvement that costs no phase margin.

Tutorial 04 of 06 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Implement conditional integration and back-calculation, and say which recovers better
  • Bound derivative gain with a filter, and choose the ceiling deliberately
  • Add feedforward for the predictable part of a load, without affecting stability

The question

You command the test stand from rest to 3,000 RPM. It accelerates, reaches speed — and then sails past to 3,400 and takes two seconds to come back.

Nothing is unstable. During the acceleration the current limit was holding the output at 10 A, so the loop had no authority left, and the error stayed positive for 400 ms. The integral term was dutifully accumulating throughout, and by the time the motor reached speed the integrator held a command far larger than anything the situation needed. It then had to count its way back down.

This is windup, it happens to every loop with an integrator and a limit, and it is the commonest defect in otherwise well-tuned control code.

What the integral does while saturated

An integrator’s job is to accumulate error until the error goes away. That is correct behaviour, and it depends on an assumption: that adding more command eventually reduces the error.

Saturation breaks that assumption. Once the actuator is at its limit, extra command changes nothing about the plant. The error persists — not because the integral is too small but because the machine is doing all it can — and the integral responds to persistence by growing.

The damage comes afterwards. On arriving at the setpoint, the integrator holds a large value that has to be unwound before the output comes off its limit, and during that unwinding the error is negative and the loop is still commanding maximum. The longer the saturation lasted, the longer and uglier the recovery.

The tell: overshoot proportional to how long the loop spent saturated, rather than to the size of the step. If a bigger step gives a much longer overshoot, this is what you are looking at.

Two ways to stop it

Conditional integration is the simple one: if the output is saturated and the error would push it further into saturation, do not accumulate. Three lines, no tuning constant, and it works.

float out = kp * e + integ + kd * de;
float sat = clamp(out, -LIM, LIM);
if (out == sat || (e * out) < 0) integ += ki * e * dt;

The second condition matters: keep integrating when the error would bring you out of saturation, or the loop cannot recover.

Back-calculation is better and barely harder. Feed the amount you clipped back into the integrator, scaled:

float out = kp * e + integ + kd * de;
float sat = clamp(out, -LIM, LIM);
integ += (ki * e + kt * (sat - out)) * dt;

Now the integrator is continuously pulled towards whatever value would put the output exactly at the limit, instead of being frozen. Recovery is smooth rather than abrupt, and kt is usually set around 1/√(Ti·Td) or simply tuned — it is not sensitive.

Whichever you pick, pick one. An integrator with a limit and no anti-windup is a bug waiting for a large enough step.

Derivative has to be filtered

A pure derivative multiplies each frequency component by its own frequency, so its gain rises without bound. Real signals have noise at every frequency, and quantised signals have noise concentrated at the sample rate — which is the worst possible place.

Concretely: an encoder with one count of jitter, differentiated at 1 kHz, produces a rate estimate that swings by 1,000 counts per second between consecutive samples. Multiply by a derivative gain chosen for the physics and you get a full-scale command from a single count of noise. The motor buzzes and gets hot doing nothing.

So derivative is always implemented as filtered derivative, with the filter setting a ceiling on the gain — typically at 10 to 20 times the proportional term. That ceiling is a design choice, and it is worth choosing rather than inheriting: too low and D does nothing, too high and the noise returns.

And note the trap from tutorial 21: the filter costs phase, and phase is part of what D was buying. Beyond a certain noise level the filtered D term gives back everything it contributes, which is why so many good loops run without it.

Feedforward costs no margin

Everything above is about feedback, which can only respond after an error exists. That is its defining limitation: the error is the input, so there is no correction without one.

Feedforward sidesteps it. Where part of the required command is predictable, compute it directly and add it to the output. The feedback loop is then left to correct only the unpredictable remainder, which is a much smaller job.

Four things are usually predictable and usually ignored:

  • Gravity on an arm — a cosine of joint angle, exactly computable.
  • Friction — the deadband compensation from tutorial 20 is feedforward.
  • Acceleration — if you command a trajectory, you know its acceleration, so you know the torque it needs: τ = J·α.
  • Back-EMF — in a current loop, the voltage needed to overcome back-EMF is Kv times speed, and speed is measured.

Here is the property that makes it worth the effort: feedforward is not in the loop, so it changes nothing about stability. It cannot cause oscillation, it costs no phase margin, and if the model is wrong the feedback simply covers the difference. It is the one way to improve tracking without spending margin, and it is underused precisely because it feels like cheating.

Worked example: fixing the 3,400 RPM overshoot

The opening failure. Velocity loop, Kp 0.030, Ki 0.20 from tutorial 19, 10 A current limit, commanded 0 to 3,000 RPM.

Step 1 — confirm it is windup rather than tuning.

Step size Time saturated Overshoot
0 → 500 RPM 0 ms 2%
0 → 1,500 RPM 120 ms 6%
0 → 3,000 RPM 400 ms 13%

Overshoot tracks time saturated, not step size — 500 RPM never saturates and behaves perfectly. That is the signature. A tuning problem would show overshoot on the small step too.

Step 2 — how much has it accumulated? During 400 ms of saturation the average error is about 1,500 RPM, so the integral gains Ki × ē × t = 0.20 × 1,500 × 0.4 = 120 units of command, against a limit of 10. It is twelve times over the limit and must unwind all of it.

Step 3 — add conditional integration.

Before After
Overshoot on the 3,000 RPM step 13% 1.5%
Time to settle within 1% 2,100 ms 260 ms
Rise time 410 ms 410 ms

Rise time is unchanged, which is the point: anti-windup does not slow the loop, because during acceleration the actuator was saturated anyway and the integral was contributing nothing useful.

Step 4 — and now feedforward, for comparison. Add an acceleration term: the commanded trajectory has a known ramp, J is measured at 4.1e-6 kg·m², so the torque needed is J·α and the current is that over Kt.

Feedback only With anti-windup Plus feedforward
Overshoot 13% 1.5% 0.6%
Settle to 1% 2,100 ms 260 ms 190 ms
Peak error during ramp 1,500 RPM 1,500 RPM 280 RPM

The last row is the interesting one. Anti-windup fixed the consequence of a large tracking error; feedforward removed most of the error itself, because the loop no longer has to discover that acceleration needs torque — it was told.

And the honest caveat. The feedforward gain depends on J, which changes if you bolt something to the shaft. A wrong J is not dangerous, since feedback covers the difference, but it is one more constant with a physical meaning that somebody has to maintain. That maintenance burden is the real cost of feedforward and the reason it is skipped.

Do it

Twenty-five minutes on hardware.

  1. Command a step big enough to saturate your actuator and log the output. If overshoot grows with saturation time rather than step size, you have windup.
  2. Add conditional integration — three lines. Re-run the same three step sizes and confirm rise time is unchanged while overshoot collapses.
  3. Take the filter off your derivative term, if you have one, and watch the output. Then work out what ceiling the filter was imposing.
  4. Then add one feedforward term. Acceleration is the easiest: you know the commanded trajectory, so estimate J by measuring how much current a known acceleration actually took, and feed it forward. Watch the tracking error during the ramp rather than the overshoot at the end.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 4 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

  1. 1What is the signature that an overshoot is windup rather than bad tuning?

    Not quite. The two usually correlate because bigger steps saturate for longer. The distinguishing variable is the saturation time, not the amplitude.

    Not quite. Lower gain reaches the limit less readily, which masks it at some step sizes. A large enough command still saturates and still winds up.

    Right. A step too small to saturate behaves perfectly, while a longer saturation gives a longer recovery. Tuning problems appear on small steps too.

    Not quite. Windup is a property of the integrator and the limit, and occurs with derivative switched off entirely.

  2. 2Why must conditional integration keep integrating when the error would bring the output out of saturation?

    Not quite. Discarding history is exactly what you want during saturation, since the accumulated value is meaningless. The issue is being able to unwind.

    Not quite. Derivative acts on the error's rate and is independent of the accumulator. The two terms do not need to be kept consistent with each other.

    Right. If the integrator can never move while saturated, nothing reduces the command and the output stays pinned. Recovery needs the integral to fall.

    Not quite. Proportional does respond immediately once the error reverses, and helps a great deal. The integral still has to come down from where it climbed.

  3. 3What does one encoder count of jitter do to an unfiltered derivative at 1 kHz?

    Not quite. It does average to zero over time, which is why the mean position is fine. The instantaneous command still swings to full scale each sample.

    Right. Differentiating divides by the sample interval, so a single count becomes a large apparent rate. Multiplied by a physical gain it commands full scale.

    Not quite. Discrete differentiation does introduce a half-sample phase shift as a separate matter. The amplification of quantisation noise is the larger problem.

    Not quite. One count is exactly the resolution, and differentiation amplifies it by the sample rate. Nothing about it stays small.

  4. 4Why can feedforward not destabilise a control loop?

    Not quite. Accumulation is an integrator property rather than a stability criterion. Feedforward would be safe even if it were held between updates.

    Not quite. It is often the dominant part of the command, especially against gravity. Its size is not what keeps it safe.

    Not quite. Ordering within one computation does not determine stability. What matters is that the signal never travels around the loop.

    Right. Stability is a property of the loop transfer function, and feedforward contributes nothing to it. A wrong model degrades tracking and nothing else.
