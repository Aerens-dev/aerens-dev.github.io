PID as it is tuned, not as it is derived

Three terms in three tenses, the order that converges, what Ziegler-Nichols was actually aiming at, and why the plant will not hold still.

Tutorial 01 of 06 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Tune a loop in an order that converges rather than by adjusting three things at once
  • Read rise, overshoot, ringing and standing error off one step response
  • Say why a loop tuned perfectly at one operating point is tuned badly at the others

The question

You have a velocity loop on the test stand. It is a bit slow, so you raise P. It overshoots, so you add D. It now buzzes, so you lower D and raise I. It settles slowly, so you raise I again, and now it oscillates at a frequency it did not have before.

Four changes, each reasonable, and the loop is worse than when you started. The problem is not the gains — it is that you changed three things whose effects overlap and then tried to attribute the result.

Course 1 will derive why the loop behaves as it does. This tutorial is about the narrower, more urgent skill: getting a working tuning on a machine in front of you, in a way that converges.

Three terms, three tenses

The one framing that makes PID intuitive rather than arbitrary: each term acts on a different tense of the error.

Proportional acts on the error now. It is fast and it is the main strength of the loop. On its own it leaves a standing error, because if the error went to zero the output would too, and something has to hold against the load.

Integral acts on the accumulated past. It is the term that drives standing error to zero, and it is the reason to have a loop rather than a gain. It costs phase — every integrator adds 90° of lag — and it introduces windup, which is tutorial 22.

Derivative acts on a guessed future, extrapolating from the current rate. It damps overshoot and it amplifies noise ferociously, because differentiating a signal multiplies each frequency component by its own frequency.

One knob at a time, in this order

The order matters because the terms interact, and a sequence that isolates each one converges while any other sequence wanders.

  1. I and D to zero. Raise P until it just rings, then halve it. This finds the plant’s stability limit empirically, which is more reliable than calculating it from a model you do not have.
  2. Raise I until the standing error closes in a time you can accept. Stop when it starts to overshoot — that is the integral winding past the target.
  3. Add D only if overshoot is still a problem, and filter it when you do. Many motion loops are better with no D at all, and a surprising number of “PID” controllers in the field are running with D at zero.
  4. Disturb it by hand and watch it recover.

Step 4 is the only real test. Steps 1 to 3 tune against a step command, which is a thing you chose; step 4 tunes against a disturbance, which is what the machine will actually meet. A loop can look excellent on a step and respond poorly to a push, and the push is the case that matters on the test stand.

What Ziegler-Nichols was aiming at

Everyone meets the Ziegler-Nichols rules first, and they are worth understanding rather than either trusting or dismissing.

They were derived in 1942 for process control — heat exchangers, flow loops, chemical plants — and their design target was quarter-amplitude damping: each oscillation peak a quarter of the last. That corresponds to roughly 50% overshoot, which is a deliberate choice, and for a slow thermal plant where getting there quickly matters more than arriving smoothly, it is a sensible one.

It is the wrong target for anything with mass. 50% overshoot on a motion axis is a machine that visibly lurches past its setpoint, and on a multirotor it is unflyable.

So: use Z-N to find the order of magnitude, which it does well, then halve the proportional gain and expect to lengthen the integral time. Åström and Hägglund’s own later work says much the same thing, and their AMIGO rules are a better starting point if you want a formula.

Reading one step response

A single step response, captured on a scope or a log, shows four things, and each one points at a different term. Learning to read it replaces most guessing.

Slow to rise — P is too low. Overshoots the target — P is too high, or add D. Rings for several cycles — P is too high; lower it before reaching for D, because D used to mask excess P gives you a loop that is both slow and noisy. Settles somewhere other than the target — I is too low or absent.

The discipline that makes this work is to capture the step before and after every change, and to change one gain at a time. That sounds obvious and is almost never done, which is why the opening scenario is so common.

The plant will not hold still

The last idea is the one that separates a loop that works on the bench from a loop that works.

Your plant’s gain moves. A battery goes from full to empty and the available voltage drops 15-20%, so the same duty cycle produces less torque. A robot arm folds and extends and its inertia changes by 4:1. A cold machine has perhaps twice the friction of a warm one. Bearings wear, belts stretch, loads vary.

A loop tuned to perfection at one operating point is, by construction, tuned too tight at every point where the plant gain is higher. The margin you left is what absorbs that, which means margin is not slack — it is the feature.

Two practical approaches. Tune at the worst case — highest gain, lowest inertia, warmest, fullest battery — and accept that it is conservative elsewhere; this is what the test stand does and what most systems should. Or schedule the gains against a measured variable, which is more work and is right when the variation is large, known and measurable.

Worked example: tuning the stand’s velocity loop

The BLDC test stand. FOC current loop already closed from tutorial 17, so the plant for the velocity loop is torque in, speed out — which is an integrator with friction, and about as friendly as plants get.

Step 1 — find the P limit.

Kp What happened
0.002 Sluggish, 400 ms to reach speed
0.010 Better, 90 ms, no ringing
0.030 40 ms, small overshoot
0.060 Sustained ringing at 62 Hz

The ringing at Kp = 0.060 is the useful measurement, and it tells you two things. The ultimate gain is 0.060, so take Kp = 0.030. And the oscillation period is 1/62 = 16 ms, which is the plant telling you its own timescale — every time constant you choose from here should be referenced to it.

Step 2 — close the standing error.

At Kp = 0.030 with no integral, commanding 2,000 RPM against a hand load gives a steady 1,870 RPM: a 6.5% standing error, which is P doing exactly what P does.

Ki Time to close Overshoot
0.05 900 ms none
0.20 240 ms none
0.60 95 ms 4%
1.50 70 ms 18%, and ringing

Take Ki = 0.20. Note that 240 ms is fifteen times the 16 ms plant period, which is the usual healthy ratio — an integral fast enough to be comparable to the plant’s own dynamics is an integral that is fighting the proportional term.

Step 3 — does it need D? Overshoot at Kp 0.030 / Ki 0.20 is under 2%. No D. Adding it would import the encoder’s quantisation noise into the output for no benefit, and the stand runs better without it. This is the common case and it is worth saying out loud.

Step 4 — the test that matters. Command 2,000 RPM and push the shaft by hand.
Speed dip under a firm push 240 RPM
Recovery to within 1% 180 ms
Overshoot on release 3%
Current during recovery peaks at 6.2 A, limit is 10 A

That is the gate for track 4 met, and the last row is the one to keep watching: the loop is asking for 6.2 A of the 10 A available, so a harder push saturates the current limit — and what a loop does when its output saturates is exactly what tutorial 22 is about.

Now change the plant and re-check. Drop the supply from 12 V to 10 V, which is the battery going flat.

12 V 10 V
Rise time 40 ms 48 ms
Overshoot 2% 1%
Standing error after 1 s 0 0

Slower and better damped, because the plant gain fell and the tuning is now slightly conservative rather than slightly aggressive. That is the right direction to fail, and it is why you tune at the high-gain end.

Do it

Twenty-five minutes on real hardware, and step 4 is the one people skip.

  1. Zero I and D on any loop you have, and find the gain at which it rings. Note the oscillation frequency — that number characterises your plant and you will use it repeatedly.
  2. Capture a step response before and after a single gain change. Look at the two together rather than at the second one alone.
  3. Try removing D entirely from a loop that has it. A good fraction of the time nothing gets worse and the output gets quieter.
  4. Then change something about the plant — supply voltage, added inertia, a warm machine versus a cold one — and re-run the same step. If the response changes a lot, your margin is too thin.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 5 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

  1. 1Why does a purely proportional loop leave a standing error under load?

    Not quite. Delay costs phase margin and can cause ringing, which matters a great deal. It does not explain a steady offset that persists indefinitely.

    Right. The output is the gain times the error, so holding against a load requires the error to stay non-zero. Something has to supply that holding effort.

    Not quite. Quantisation does set a floor on how finely you can settle, and it is usually far below the standing error. The error exists with perfect resolution.

    Not quite. A sensor offset shifts where the loop thinks the target is, which is a separate fault. The standing error appears with a perfectly calibrated sensor.

  2. 2Why is disturbing the machine by hand the real test rather than a step command?

    Not quite. Many systems do ramp their setpoints, which is worth knowing about. The step can be applied after the ramp if you want the raw response.

    Right. A step command tests tracking of something you chose. Load rejection is the job, and a loop can look excellent on one and respond poorly to the other.

    Not quite. You can command a step of any size you like, so magnitude is not the distinction. Where the input enters the loop is what differs.

    Not quite. Timing jitter in the response would show up on a commanded step too. The point is the path the disturbance takes rather than when it arrives.

  3. 3What was Ziegler-Nichols tuning designed to achieve?

    Not quite. That is closer to what a motion system wants and is not what the rules produce. They deliberately accept substantial overshoot.

    Not quite. That describes an optimal-control formulation such as LQR, which came later. Ziegler-Nichols targets a specific decay ratio instead.

    Not quite. Rejection does improve with the gains the rules give, as a consequence. The stated design criterion was the shape of the decay.

    Right. Each peak a quarter of the last was a deliberate target for slow process plants in 1942, where arriving fast beat arriving smoothly.

  4. 4A loop rings for several cycles after a step. What should you change first?

    Not quite. It will suppress the symptom, and it imports noise while doing so. The better first move is removing the gain that caused the oscillation.

    Not quite. A faster loop does buy phase margin and is worth checking. If the ringing is at the plant's natural frequency the gain is the direct cause.

    Not quite. Excess integral produces overshoot followed by a slow correction rather than sustained ringing at the plant's own frequency.

    Right. Ringing is usually too much proportional gain. Using derivative to damp it leaves a loop that is both slower and noisier than simply backing P off.

  5. 5Why should a loop be tuned at the operating point where plant gain is highest?

    Not quite. Windup is worst when the output saturates, which tends to happen at low authority rather than high. It is a separate concern with its own tutorial.

    Not quite. Sensor accuracy is largely independent of plant gain. What matters is which direction the loop moves when conditions change.

    Right. Tuning at a low-gain point and then moving to a high-gain one raises the loop gain you did not choose. Tuning at the worst case fails in the safe direction.

    Not quite. More authority does help the loop respond, and it is why that point feels good to tune at. The reason to choose it is the stability margin elsewhere.
