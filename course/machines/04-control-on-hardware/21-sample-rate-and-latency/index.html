Sample rate, latency, and the phase you lose for free

Why Nyquist is the wrong rule for a control loop, where the delay actually comes from, and why jitter is worse than latency.

Tutorial 03 of 06 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Convert a delay into degrees of phase at the frequency your loop works at
  • Add up the delay chain and find the term that dominates, which is rarely the maths
  • Say why a jittery loop is worse than a slower loop with fixed timing

The question

Your velocity loop was tuned and stable. You add a small low-pass filter on the current measurement, because the signal looked noisy and a cleaner signal must be better. The loop now oscillates.

You did not change a single gain. What you changed was time — you added 80 µs of delay in exchange for a tidier plot, and at the frequency the loop was working at, 80 µs is 36° of phase margin you no longer have.

Delay is the one thing in a control loop that cannot be bought back. Gain you can lower, noise you can filter, nonlinearity you can compensate. Phase lost to delay is gone.

Delay costs phase, in proportion to frequency

The arithmetic is one line and it is worth having by heart:

phase lost = 360° × delay × frequency

So 100 µs costs 0.36° at 10 Hz — invisible. At 100 Hz it is 3.6° — measurable. At 1 kHz it is 36°, which is most of the 45 to 60° of phase margin a healthy loop has.

Two consequences follow. First, a delay is harmless until it is fatal, and the transition is fast because the relationship is linear in frequency while your ambitions for bandwidth usually are not. Second, you cannot evaluate a delay without knowing the loop’s crossover frequency — the same 100 µs is nothing or everything depending on where the loop is working.

This is also why raising the loop rate helps and raising the gain does not. Gain raises crossover, which makes the same delay cost more phase.

Where the microseconds actually go

Add up the whole chain, because the term you control is rarely the one that dominates. A 20 kHz FOC loop:
Sampling — on average half a period 25 µs
Computation — tutorial 17 measured it 4 µs
Hold until the next PWM update 50 µs
A 2 kHz filter on the current signal 80 µs
Total 159 µs

The computation is 2.5% of it. The filter somebody added to tidy the plot is half. And the PWM hold — the fact that a new duty cycle does not take effect until the next period boundary — is the largest structural term and is invisible in any code listing.

This reorders what is worth optimising. Hand-tuning the Park transform saves 1 µs of 159. Removing an unnecessary filter saves 80. Updating the PWM registers at both the underflow and overflow events of a centre-aligned timer halves the hold term and costs one configuration bit.

Why Nyquist is the wrong rule

Nyquist says sampling at twice the highest frequency is enough to reconstruct a signal. That is true and it is about reconstruction. A control loop has to act on the signal, and acting is a harder requirement.

At 2× bandwidth, the half-period sampling delay alone is 90° of phase at the loop’s crossover. There is no margin left for the plant, the actuator or anything else. The loop is unusable, while satisfying Nyquist perfectly.

The working rule is 10 to 20 times the desired closed-loop bandwidth:

  • 5× costs about 36° — still poor.
  • 10× costs about 18° — affordable.
  • 20× costs about 9° — comfortable.

So a 100 Hz position loop wants a 1 to 2 kHz sample rate, and a 1 kHz current loop wants 10 to 20 kHz — which is exactly why motor drives run their PWM at 20 kHz and close the current loop at the same rate. That number was not chosen for audibility alone.

Every filter is a delay you agreed to

A low-pass filter reduces noise by averaging over time, and averaging over time is delay. There is no filter that removes noise without costing phase; the only question is how much and where.

A first-order filter costs 45° at its corner frequency, about 27° one octave above it, and roughly 11° at five times the corner.

So the rule for placing one: put the corner at least five times the loop crossover, and preferably ten. If the noise you are trying to remove is below that, a filter is the wrong tool — the noise is inside your control bandwidth and filtering it means filtering the signal too.

And the honest trade for derivative terms specifically: D amplifies noise, so it needs a filter, and the filter costs phase, which is part of what D was buying. Beyond a certain noise level a filtered D term gives back everything it contributes, which is a large part of why so many working loops run with D at zero.

Jitter is worse than latency

A constant 120 µs delay is a known quantity. You can measure it, include it in a model, design margin for it, and in some cases compensate for it directly.

A delay that varies between 80 and 160 µs has the same average and is worse, because the margin has to cover the worst case while the tuning is only optimal at the average. You get a loop that is over-damped most of the time and marginal occasionally, and the occasions are not reproducible.

Which is why a control loop runs from a timer interrupt at a fixed priority, never from the main loop. Tutorial 10’s superloop analysis arrives here with teeth: a loop whose period depends on what else ran this pass has jitter equal to the variation in everything else in the system.

Measure it rather than assuming. Toggle a pin at the top of the control ISR and look at the period on a scope with infinite persistence — the width of the band is your jitter, and if it is a significant fraction of the period, fix that before touching any gain.

Worked example: recovering the margin the filter took

The opening failure. Velocity loop at 1 kHz, crossover at 80 Hz, was stable with about 50° of phase margin. Adding a 2 kHz first-order filter on the current feedback made it oscillate.

Step 1 — account for the phase, before and after.

Source Delay Phase at 80 Hz
Sampling at 1 kHz, half period 500 µs 14.4°
Computation 40 µs 1.2°
PWM hold at 20 kHz 50 µs 1.4°
Plant and current loop — 24°
Before the filter 41°, so 139° used of 180
New 2 kHz filter — 2.3°

2.3°? That cannot cause an oscillation. So the filter is not the direct cause, and this is the point of the exercise: the arithmetic refutes the obvious explanation.

Step 2 — find what the filter actually did. Look again at what changed. The filter was implemented in the velocity loop at 1 kHz, not in the current loop at 20 kHz. A 2 kHz corner cannot exist at a 1 kHz sample rate — the discrete filter coefficient was computed from a formula assuming a much higher rate, and what was actually implemented has a corner near 160 Hz.
Intended corner 2,000 Hz
Sample rate it runs at 1,000 Hz
Actual corner, from the coefficient 160 Hz
Phase at 80 Hz crossover 26.6°

26.6° against 41° of margin leaves 14°, which is an oscillating loop. There it is.

Step 3 — three fixes, and their arithmetic.

Margin after Cost
Move the filter into the 20 kHz current loop 38.7° free, it belongs there
Keep it at 1 kHz, corner at 400 Hz 29.7° some noise remains
Remove it and accept the noise 41° noisier plots

Take the first. The signal being filtered is a current measurement, it is sampled at 20 kHz, and filtering it at 1 kHz was a category error — the noise was already aliased into the velocity loop’s band by the time it got there.

The transferable lesson, and it is the reason this example is here: a filter specified by its corner frequency behaves completely differently depending on the rate it runs at, and a coefficient copied from elsewhere silently carries that rate with it. Always express a digital filter as a ratio of corner to sample rate, and check that ratio when you move it.

Do it

Twenty minutes, and step 1 is the number most loops have never had measured.

  1. Toggle a pin in your control ISR and measure both the period and its variation on a scope with persistence on. The band width is your jitter.
  2. Add up your delay chain the way the table above does. Find the largest term. It is very unlikely to be the arithmetic.
  3. Work out your loop’s crossover frequency — where the open-loop gain passes through 1 — and convert your total delay to degrees at that frequency. That is how much margin the timing costs you before anything else.
  4. Then take every filter in the loop and check its corner against the sample rate it actually runs at. A filter whose corner is above half the sample rate is not the filter you think you have.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 5 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

  1. 1Why does raising the proportional gain make an existing delay more damaging?

    Not quite. Amplitude and phase are separate effects, and the instability comes from the timing. A larger signal with adequate margin stays stable.

    Right. The delay is unchanged, but the loop now works at a higher frequency where the same microseconds buy more degrees of lag.

    Not quite. Gain does not change the loop rate at all. The sample period is set by the timer and is unaffected by any tuning constant.

    Not quite. Saturation does introduce nonlinear behaviour and matters for windup. The mechanism here is linear and applies well below any limit.

  2. 2In a 20 kHz control loop, which term is usually the largest?

    Not quite. Conversion is genuinely a few microseconds and is worth overlapping with computation. It is small beside the hold and the sampling interval.

    Not quite. Measured at around 4 microseconds of a 159 microsecond chain, it is about 2.5 per cent. Its reputation outlives the hardware that earned it.

    Not quite. That is well under a microsecond on a Cortex-M with the loop at high priority. It matters for jitter more than for total delay.

    Right. A new duty cycle waits for a period boundary, so it contributes a full period. It is structural and invisible in the source.

  3. 3What does sampling at twice the loop bandwidth cost in phase?

    Right. At that ratio the sampling delay consumes the entire margin before the plant is counted. Nyquist guarantees reconstruction rather than controllability.

    Not quite. That is roughly what a 10x ratio costs, which is the common design point. Two times bandwidth is five times worse than that.

    Not quite. Representation is preserved and that is the point of the theorem. Acting on the signal requires it to arrive in time, which is a stronger condition.

    Not quite. That figure corresponds to roughly a 4x ratio. At exactly Nyquist the half period is a full half cycle at the bandwidth.

  4. 4Where should a first-order filter's corner sit relative to loop crossover?

    Right. At the crossover itself it costs 45 degrees, which is most of a healthy margin. Five times above is the usual affordable compromise.

    Not quite. A corner below crossover filters the control signal itself and costs well over 45 degrees. The loop stops responding to what it needs to.

    Not quite. That is where it does most damage, spending 45 degrees. Noise at the crossover is inside the control band and filtering removes signal with it.

    Not quite. A corner at Nyquist barely filters anything and wastes the component. It also cannot be realised meaningfully in a discrete implementation.

  5. 5Why is a varying delay worse than a constant one of the same average?

    Right. The loop ends up over-damped most of the time and marginal occasionally, and the marginal occasions do not reproduce on demand.

    Not quite. Integral action corrects steady-state error, not phase lag. A constant delay still consumes margin; it is merely predictable.

    Not quite. Modulation can produce sidebands and it is worth knowing about. The dominant practical problem is that the worst case sets the design.

    Not quite. The average is whatever it is, and the premise fixes it as equal. What differs is the spread rather than the mean.
