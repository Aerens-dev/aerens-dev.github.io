The multimeter and the scope, and what each one cannot see

Why a meter cannot show you a two-microsecond collapse, why the probe changes the circuit, and why the trigger is the instrument.

Tutorial 04 of 06 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Say what a meter averages over, and what class of fault that hides
  • Choose between a 1x and a 10x probe for a given measurement, and say what each costs
  • Describe a fault to a trigger rather than watching for it

The question

Your board resets about once a minute. The 3.3 V rail measures 3.29 V on the meter, rock steady, whenever you check it. You check it a lot. You start suspecting the firmware, and you spend two days there.

The rail is collapsing to 1.8 V for four microseconds every time the SD card writes. Your meter reported the average of a million such events and it was right to. This tutorial is about the gap between what an instrument reports and what happened.

What a meter averages over

A digital multimeter samples, filters and updates a display three or four times a second. Everything it tells you is an average over roughly a third of a second, and the whole class of fault that lives below that window is invisible to it — not reported badly, but not reported at all.

That is not a defect. It is what makes a meter good at the thing it is for: telling you a level with four digits of confidence. But it means a steady reading is evidence about the mean and nothing else, and “the rail measures fine” is a claim with a time constant hidden inside it.

The rule worth keeping: a meter answers what is the level. It cannot answer is the level ever something else.

The probe is part of the circuit

A scope probe is not a wire. It puts capacitance across the node you are measuring, and on a fast node that capacitance is a real component in your circuit.

A 1x probe presents something like 100 pF. Against a 1 kΩ source that is a 100 ns time constant — enough to visibly slow an edge you are looking at, which means you can measure a rise time that only exists because you are measuring it.

A 10x probe divides the signal by ten before it reaches the scope, and in exchange presents about 10 pF. That is the default for anything digital. The cost is a tenth of the amplitude, so on a 20 mV sensor signal you want 1x and you accept the loading.

The related habit: compensate the probe against the scope’s calibration square wave before you trust an edge. An uncompensated 10x probe over- or undershoots by design, and it looks exactly like a circuit problem.

The ground lead rings

The crocodile clip on a standard probe is about 150 mm of wire, and together with the probe tip it forms a loop. That loop has inductance — roughly 150 nH — and it resonates with the tip capacitance somewhere in the 50 to 200 MHz range.

Hit that resonance with a fast edge and it rings. The ringing appears on your screen, sitting neatly on top of the edge, looking exactly like a circuit with poor damping. Engineers have redesigned perfectly good boards to fix ringing that was in their probe.

The fix is a spring tip or a short ground: replace the clip with a few millimetres of wire to a ground point right next to the signal. If the ringing changes when you shorten the ground lead, the ringing was yours. That test takes ten seconds and it is worth doing before believing any overshoot.

The trigger is the instrument

A scope showing everything shows nothing. Millions of events pass the probe every second and the screen holds one of them; the trigger is what decides which. That makes the trigger, not the bandwidth, the feature you actually use to find faults.

Edge triggering handles every periodic signal, and is where you start. Pulse width triggering is what finds the runt, the stretched bit, the glitch narrower than a real pulse — you tell it “show me a high shorter than 100 ns” and it waits for hours if it has to. Serial triggering decodes the bus in hardware and fires on a particular address or byte.

The habit this builds is worth more than the technique: instead of watching for a fault, describe it. What would be different on the screen at the moment it happens? That description is a trigger condition, and once you have written it down the scope does the waiting.

Which box answers which question

Most wasted bench time is picking up the wrong instrument, so it is worth having this as reflex rather than as reasoning.

Is the rail at the right level is a meter question. Did the byte arrive, and what was in it is a logic analyser question, and a €12 analyser answers it better than a €700 scope. What shape was the edge, and what is the rail doing during the transient is the scope, and nothing else can do it. How much is the board drawing in total is the supply’s own display, free and already there.

And where is the hot part is a finger, carefully. That last one is not a joke; it is often the fastest diagnostic on the bench and it costs nothing.

Worked example: finding the reset

Back to the board from the opening. It resets about once a minute and the meter says the rail is fine.

Step 1 — decide what you are hunting. A reset means the supervisor saw the rail below its threshold, or the watchdog fired, or the firmware faulted. Those are three different investigations, so start by reading the reset cause register if the MCU has one. Say it reports a brown-out.

Step 2 — describe it to the trigger. A brown-out means the rail went below about 2.9 V. That is a trigger condition: falling edge on 3V3, level 2.9 V, single shot. Set it, arm it, walk away. This is the step that replaces staring.

Step 3 — read what it caught. The single-shot fires and holds this:
Nominal rail 3.30 V
Minimum during the event 1.81 V
Width below 2.9 V 4.2 µs
Repeat interval roughly 900 ms
Coincident signal SD card CMD line active

Step 4 — do the arithmetic before believing it. A 1.5 V droop for 4.2 µs. If the regulator is fine, this is the bulk capacitance being emptied faster than it is refilled. The SD card’s write burst draws about 100 mA above baseline. Q = I·t = 0.1 × 4.2e-6 = 420 nC. For that to cost 1.5 V, the capacitance holding the rail is C = Q/V = 420e-9 / 1.5 = 280 nF.

There it is. The board has 280 nF of decoupling where it needed a few tens of microfarads of bulk near the card. Not a firmware bug, not a bad card, not a marginal regulator — a missing capacitor, found in four steps, none of which involved reading any of your own code.

Step 5 — confirm the fix moves the number, not just the symptom. Add 47 µF close to the socket and re-run the same single-shot trigger. The droop should fall to roughly 1.5 × 0.28/47 ≈ 9 mV, which is to say it should not trigger at all. If it still triggers, the capacitor is not where you think it is.

Do it

Fifteen minutes, and step 3 is the one that will surprise you.

  1. Put a scope on any rail on any board you have, AC-coupled, at 20 mV per division. Look at what is actually on your supposedly clean 3.3 V.
  2. Measure a fast edge with a 10x probe and the standard ground clip. Then measure the same edge with the ground lead shortened as far as you can. Note how much of the overshoot was yours.
  3. Set a pulse-width trigger for something narrower than any real pulse in your system, arm it, and leave it running while you work. Anything it catches is free information.
  4. Then the habit: next time something is wrong, write the sentence at the moment it fails, ___ would look different before you touch an instrument.
