What to buy, and what not to

The four instruments that unblock everything else, the one feature that decides whether a mistake costs a board, and the tempting purchases that sit in a drawer.

Tutorial 01 of 06 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Name the one supply feature that turns a short circuit into a beep
  • Size a scope's bandwidth against the edge you actually need to see
  • Order a first bench by what each tool unblocks rather than by price

The question

You wire up your first board, plug in the €12 USB supply that came in the kit, and a regulator gets hot enough to smell. Nothing on the bench tells you what went wrong, because nothing on the bench was measuring anything. You have one data point — it is broken now — and no way to find out which of six mistakes did it.

A bench is not a collection of instruments. It is a set of questions you are able to ask, and the failure above is what it looks like when you cannot ask any of them.

Four tools, four questions

Almost everything you will want is optional. Four things are not, and each one earns its place by answering exactly one question that nothing else answers.

  • The iron makes a joint that does not move. Intermittent connections produce bugs that look like firmware bugs, and you will chase them in the wrong file for a day.
  • The multimeter answers is that rail the voltage I think it is. Half of all first-board failures end here.
  • The bench supply decides whether a mistake costs you five minutes or a board.
  • The scope is the only one that can see time. Everything below about a millisecond is invisible without it.

The current limit is the feature

If you buy one thing carefully, buy the supply, and buy one whose current limit actually works. It is not a safety accessory. It is the difference between a short being an event and a short being a purchase.

A bench supply is really two supplies in one. Below the current you set, it holds the voltage and lets the load draw what it wants. Above it, it gives up on voltage and holds the current instead — the voltage collapses to whatever the fault needs. A dead short then means the display reads 0.6 V at your limit, and the board is cold.

Set it to a little above what the board should draw. Now a solder bridge you have not found yet announces itself as a supply in constant-current mode, at the moment you power on, before anything has time to heat up.

Bandwidth, not sample rate

Scope specs lead with sample rate because it is the bigger number. Bandwidth is the one that decides what you can see.

The useful relationship is rise time × bandwidth ≈ 0.35. A 100 MHz scope cannot show you an edge faster than about 3.5 ns, and — this is the part that costs people a day — it will not tell you it is struggling. It draws a slow, rounded edge that looks exactly like a real slow, rounded edge. You measure the instrument and write down the signal.

The rule of thumb is to buy about five times the bandwidth of the fastest edge you care about. For the work in this course — I²C at 400 kHz, SPI at a few MHz, PWM at 20 kHz — 100 MHz is comfortable and 200 MHz is comfortable for longer.

What can wait

The instruments that feel like the real purchase are mostly the ones that wait for a project that needs them. Hot air is wonderful and you will want it the first time you have to lift a QFN — which is not this month. A function generator is displaced by the microcontroller you already have. A bench multimeter is more accurate than your handheld in a digit you are not using.

Meanwhile the things that get touched every single session cost almost nothing: cutters that cut flush, tweezers fine enough to hold an 0402, a third hand, and enough light to see what you are doing. All of them together cost less than one item on the deferred list.

The order to buy in

Buy in the order of what each tool unblocks, which is not the order of price and not the order of excitement.

Iron first, because without it you cannot build anything to measure. Meter next, because it turns it does not work into the 3.3 V rail is at 1.8 V. Then the supply, because until you have one, every mistake is paid for in hardware. The scope last of the four — it is the most expensive and the most capable, and it is also the only one you can partly fake for a while with a €12 logic analyser if what you need is digital timing rather than analogue shape.

Now what each one cannot do. The minimum bench cannot see an analogue shape at all — it will tell you a line went high at 4.1 µs, and nothing about the ringing on the edge, the droop on the rail, or the noise that is coupling into your ADC. That is a real hole, and it is exactly the hole you fall into in track 3 when a motor starts switching amps a few centimetres from your MCU.

The sensible bench does everything in this course. The 100 MHz scope is inside its 5× rule for every signal up to about 20 MHz, which covers SPI at 10 MHz comfortably.

The comfortable bench buys you two things: a second supply channel, which matters the moment you have separate logic and motor rails, and enough bandwidth that you stop having to think about the 5× rule.

The honest recommendation is the middle column, and if that is too much at once, buy it in the order of the figure above and spread it over three months. The supply is the one to bring forward, not the one to defer.

Do it

Fifteen minutes, and it produces a list rather than a feeling.

  1. Write down the fastest edge you expect to care about in the next six months. For this course that is probably a SPI clock at 10 MHz, so an edge of perhaps 5 ns.
  2. Apply the 5× rule. Then look up three scopes in that class and note their real bandwidth against their advertised sample rate — the gap between how the two are marketed is the point.
  3. Price the middle column above against what is actually available where you are. Currency, stock and shipping will move it.
  4. Then the useful one: for each of the four tools, write the sentence without this I cannot find out ___. If you cannot finish the sentence, you have found something to defer.

  2. 2You set the limit to 200 mA and power a board with a solder bridge across the 3.3 V rail. What does the supply do?

    Not quite. Some supplies offer that as a separate protection mode, but the current limit itself regulates rather than trips. It keeps supplying 200 mA.

    Not quite. Holding 3.3 V into a short is what an unlimited supply does, and it is the failure this feature exists to prevent. Voltage is what gives way.

    Right. That is constant-current mode. The rail collapses to a fraction of a volt, the fault dissipates well under a watt, and nothing gets hot.

    Not quite. That is roughly what a cheap fixed supply does, and by then the board has had several watts through it. A current limit acts immediately.

  3. 3Why is a scope short of bandwidth more dangerous than one short of sample rate?

    Right. Insufficient bandwidth is a filter, and a filtered edge looks exactly like a genuinely slow edge. You measure the instrument and write down the signal.

    Not quite. True of most scopes, and it is a reason to buy carefully, but not why the measurement misleads you. The problem is that the result looks correct.

    Not quite. Both are usually specified accurately enough. What differs is that a bandwidth shortfall produces a plausible waveform rather than a broken one.

    Not quite. Aliases can be quite convincing too, so this understates the sample-rate risk. The real distinction is that the bandwidth limit hides itself.

  4. 4What makes hot air rework a reasonable thing to defer on a first bench?

    Right. It is the right tool for QFNs and rework, and neither shows up in the first months. Deferring it is about sequence rather than about value.

    Not quite. Cheap stations are mostly adequate for hobby rework. That is not the reason to wait; the reason is that nothing on the bench yet needs one.

    Not quite. An oven does initial assembly, not rework, so it displaces very little of what hot air is for. The point stands on timing rather than on substitutes.

    Not quite. It does not — removing a QFN with an iron is genuinely awkward. The argument for deferring is that the job itself arrives later, not that it is easy.

  5. 5Why does the supply come before the scope in the buying order?

    Not quite. Scopes generate their own calibration square wave. The ordering argument is about what each tool protects, not about what powers what.

    Not quite. It covers digital timing only, and misses ringing, droop and analogue shape entirely. It is a stopgap for part of the job, not a replacement.

    Not quite. Rather the opposite — a scope earns most of its keep on boards that do not work. It is later in the order because of cost and blast radius.

    Right. The scope shows you what went wrong. The supply stops it going wrong expensively, and that changes how freely you can experiment.
