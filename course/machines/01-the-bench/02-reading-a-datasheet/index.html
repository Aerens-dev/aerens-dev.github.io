Reading a datasheet

The three sections that carry design numbers, why absolute maximum is not a rating, and the second document nobody links to.

Tutorial 02 of 06 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Say what lives between recommended maximum and absolute maximum, and why you may not design there
  • Design against min and max rather than typical, and explain what typical actually is
  • Size an I2C pull-up from the datasheet numbers rather than from a forum post

The question

Your I²C bus works on the bench and fails on the second board. Same firmware, same parts, same code path. The difference is that the second board has a longer run to the sensor, and you picked the pull-up resistors by copying a schematic you found online.

Every number you needed to get that right was in two datasheets you already had open. This tutorial is about extracting them, and about the three ways a datasheet is read wrongly by people who are perfectly good at reading.

The running order, and what is worth reading

Datasheets are boring in a specific, useful way: they are all laid out the same. Features, pinout, absolute maximum ratings, recommended operating conditions, electrical characteristics, timing, package drawings, ordering information.

Of those, exactly three carry numbers you design against. Recommended operating conditions says where the part is allowed to live. Electrical characteristics says what it does there. Timing says how fast. The features page is written by marketing and the block diagram is usually a lie of simplification.

Start at the electrical characteristics table and read backwards into the prose only when a symbol is unclear. It is faster and it skips the part designed to make you want the chip.

Absolute maximum is not a rating

This is the single most common misreading, and it is a misreading of one word.

Absolute maximum is the point beyond which the manufacturer expects damage. Recommended operating is the range in which the specifications hold. Between them is a third region that has no name and is the dangerous one: the part is not damaged, and nothing about its behaviour is specified.

A design sitting in that band works on your bench. It is undefined at −10 °C, undefined on a part from a different lot, and undefined next year when the process shrinks. It will pass every test you run and fail in somebody else’s unit.

The habit worth building: when you read a maximum, look for which of the two tables it came from before you use it.

Typical is a census, not a specification

Electrical characteristics tables give min, typ and max. Typical is the middle of a production distribution — a description of what most parts do. It carries no commitment whatsoever. The manufacturer will not take a return because your part missed typical.

Min and max are the numbers under warranty, and they are the ones to design against. This matters most where a typical value is much better than the guaranteed one: quiescent current typ 1 µA / max 12 µA is a twelve-fold difference, and if your battery life calculation used the first number it is wrong by an order of magnitude for some fraction of what you ship.

The related trap is a blank. If a parameter has a typ and no max, it is not guaranteed at all, and it will move.

Timing diagrams are read with a ruler

A timing diagram is a picture with a table under it, and the picture is not to scale. Only the table is real.

Two numbers do most of the work. Setup time is how long the data must be stable before the active clock edge. Hold time is how long it must remain stable after. Both are minimums you must satisfy — the part is telling you what it needs, not what it will do.

The direction of that obligation is the thing people invert. A propagation delay max is a promise the part makes to you and constrains your clock period. A setup time min is an obligation you take on and constrains your driver. Read every timing parameter by asking: is this a promise, or a requirement?

The errata is a separate document

The datasheet describes the part as designed. The errata sheet describes the part as it came out, and it is a different file, usually not linked from the datasheet, revised without announcement, and often the shortest and most valuable document the vendor publishes.

This is where you find that the I²C peripheral locks up if a NACK arrives during a repeated start, that the ADC has an undocumented settling requirement, that a DMA channel corrupts a transfer at a particular alignment. Every one of those is a week of debugging your own perfectly correct code.

Download it at the same time as the datasheet, before you write anything. On STM32 parts it is called the device errata; on TI parts it is the silicon errata. Search the part number plus “errata” and then read all of it once — it is short, and you only need to remember that something exists in order to find it again at three in the morning.

Worked example: sizing an I²C pull-up

The bus from the opening failure. 3.3 V, fast mode at 400 kHz, one MCU and one sensor, and — because the second board has a longer run — about 200 pF of bus capacitance.

Two constraints bracket the resistor, and both come out of the I²C specification and the sensor’s electrical characteristics table.

The lower bound comes from the sink current. When the bus is pulled low, the open-drain output has to sink the pull-up current and still stay under its specified low level. For fast mode that is VOL ≤ 0.4 V at IOL = 3 mA.
Supply 3.3 V
Maximum low level 0.4 V
Available sink current 3 mA
R minimum (3.3 − 0.4) / 0.003 = 967 Ω

The upper bound comes from the rise time. The pull-up and the bus capacitance form an RC, and the specification allows 300 ns to cross from 0.3 to 0.7 of VDD. That works out at R × Cb × 0.8473 ≤ 300 ns.
Allowed rise time 300 ns
Bus capacitance 200 pF
R maximum 300e-9 / (0.8473 × 200e-12) = 1,770 Ω

So anything from about 1.0 kΩ to about 1.8 kΩ, and 1.5 kΩ sits comfortably in the middle. Note what happened to the window: on the first board, with 60 pF of bus capacitance, the upper bound was 5.9 kΩ and the 4.7 kΩ resistor from the forum worked. Adding 140 pF of trace and cable moved the ceiling below the value you fitted, and the bus started failing — not because the resistor changed, but because the board did.

Read it back as a rule: the pull-up is a function of the bus, not of the parts on it. Every time the wiring changes, the number changes.

Do it

Fifteen minutes, on a part you actually have.

  1. Pick any microcontroller or sensor on your desk and open its datasheet and its errata. Note how much harder the second one was to find.
  2. In the electrical characteristics table, find one parameter where typ and max differ by more than 3×. Work out what a design assuming typ would get wrong.
  3. Find one absolute maximum rating and the corresponding recommended operating figure. Write down the gap between them — that is the band you may not use.
  4. Then the one worth doing properly: take a bus or interface on your own board and re-derive one passive value from the datasheet numbers instead of from whatever it currently is. If they disagree, you have learned something.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 5 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

  1. 1Which three sections of a datasheet carry the numbers you design against?

    Not quite. The features page is written to sell the part and the block diagram simplifies aggressively. The application circuit is a starting point, not a spec.

    Right. One says where the part may live, one says what it does there, and one says how fast. Everything else is context or packaging.

    Not quite. You need the pinout to lay out and the maximums to stay safe, but neither gives you a value to design with. Revision history is housekeeping.

    Not quite. All three matter later — a damage boundary, a suffix and a footprint — but none of them tells you how the part behaves in your circuit.

  2. 2A rail sits above the recommended maximum but below the absolute maximum. What is true of the part?

    Not quite. Damage is expected above the absolute maximum, not above the recommended one. The point of the middle band is that it is silent, not fatal.

    Not quite. Specification stops at the recommended maximum. Beyond it there is no margin being consumed, because there is nothing being guaranteed.

    Not quite. Temperature makes it worse, but staying cool does not restore a guarantee that was never given. Nothing in that band is specified at any temperature.

    Right. That band has no guarantees attached to it. The part will very likely run, and nothing about how it runs is promised across lots or temperature.

  3. 3A regulator lists quiescent current as typ 1 µA, max 12 µA. What should the battery-life calculation use?

    Right. Max is the warranted number. Sizing a battery on typ means some fraction of shipped units miss the life you promised, and you cannot return them.

    Not quite. Derating a typical value is guesswork stacked on a figure that carries no commitment. The max already bounds the part across its stated range.

    Not quite. Averaging a census figure with a guarantee produces a number with no standing at all. The max is what you can hold the manufacturer to.

    Not quite. A twelve-fold gap between typ and max says the distribution is not tight at all. Designing on the centre of it accepts the tail as a defect rate.

  4. 4What is the difference between a setup time minimum and a propagation delay maximum?

    Not quite. Datasheets do mark some parameters as design-guaranteed, and it is worth noticing. It is a separate axis from which way the requirement points.

    Not quite. Either can be specified against either edge, and good datasheets say which. The distinction that matters is who has to satisfy the number.

    Not quite. Both are specified at the pins under stated load conditions. What separates them is the direction of the obligation, not the measurement point.

    Right. Setup min constrains what your driver must do. Propagation max is what the part guarantees, and it constrains your clock period instead.

  5. 5Why does reading only the datasheet leave you designing against a part that does not exist?

    Not quite. They are revised regularly, and the electrical numbers are usually accurate. What they omit is the list of things that came out wrong.

    Not quite. On many families the reference manual does carry the register detail, and it is worth having. Neither document lists the silicon deviations.

    Not quite. That happens for a few specialised parts, but for general-purpose silicon the public datasheet is the real one. The gap is the errata, not secrecy.

    Right. Deviations live in the errata, which is a separate file, rarely linked, and quietly revised. The peripheral bug that costs you a week is in there.
