Voltage, current, and the three things that kill boards

Why a five amp supply does not push five amps, and the three failure modes that account for most dead first boards.

Tutorial 03 of 06 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Say which of voltage and current you choose, and which the load chooses
  • Name the three common kill mechanisms and the preventive part for each
  • Work out the peak inrush into a bulk capacitor and decide whether it matters

The question

Three boards, three deaths, three different weeks. The first died the instant you plugged the barrel jack in backwards. The second was fine for a month and then the connector started arcing every time you powered it up. The third ran perfectly until you added a relay, and then the microcontroller reset every time the relay released.

None of these is a subtle failure. All three are prevented by a part costing less than a euro. What they have in common is that they are all about change — of polarity, of current, of state — and none of them shows up in a steady-state calculation.

One is set, the other is taken

Start with the thing that quietly confuses everybody once: a power supply sets voltage. The load takes as much current as it takes.

A 5 A supply does not push 5 A into your board. It offers up to 5 A and waits to see what is asked for. So the current is a fact about the load, and a short circuit is not a property of the supply at all — it is a load with almost no resistance, drawing whatever the supply will give.

This is why the current limit from the previous tutorial is the only thing in the room arguing back. Without it, I = V/R runs to completion, and you chose only one of the three terms.

Reverse polarity

Plug the supply in backwards and the entire rail appears across every part on the board with the wrong sign. Electrolytic capacitors fail quickly and sometimes loudly. Every body diode in every MOSFET conducts. Silicon that has no reverse rating at all — which is most of it — is gone before you can react.

The fix is one part in series. A Schottky diode is the simple answer and costs you about 0.3 V of drop. A P-channel MOSFET with its source at the rail and its gate at ground is the better answer: it costs a few tens of millivolts, and on anything battery-powered that difference is real running time.

Fit one. This is the single cheapest insurance on the board, and the failure it prevents is the one you will make while tired.

Inrush

An uncharged capacitor is a short circuit. Not approximately — at the instant you apply voltage, the current into a discharged capacitor is limited only by whatever resistance happens to be in the path: the wire, the connector, the capacitor’s own ESR.

For a 470 µF bulk capacitor with 50 mΩ of ESR on a 12 V rail with 30 mΩ of wiring, the peak is 12 / 0.08 = 150 A for a few tens of microseconds. That is what pits a connector, welds the contacts of a switch you thought was adequate, and trips a supply that is perfectly happy with your running current of 300 mA.

The mitigations are all about slowing the edge: an NTC thermistor in series that starts at several ohms and heats down to milliohms, a soft-start circuit that ramps a MOSFET’s gate, or simply a smaller bulk capacitor if you can justify it. The one thing that does not help is a bigger supply.

Inductive kick

An inductor opposes a change in current. Interrupt the current through a coil and the inductor will drive its terminal voltage to whatever value is required to keep that current flowing — through the air, if that is what is left.

V = L·di/dt, and by opening a switch in a microsecond you chose a very small dt. A 12 V relay coil switched off by a bare transistor will happily produce a couple of hundred volts across that transistor, which is how a 12 V circuit destroys a part rated for 30 V.

The flyback diode across the coil is the standard answer: it gives the current a path, and clamps the excursion to one diode drop above the rail. The cost is a slower release, because the energy now decays through the diode rather than being dumped. Where release speed matters, a zener or an RC snubber trades a controlled overshoot for a faster decay.

This is the one to internalise now, because track 3 is entirely about switching current through coils on purpose.

The three, and how to tell them apart afterwards

Each of the three leaves a different corpse, which is worth knowing because the post-mortem tells you which mistake to stop making.

Reverse polarity kills broadly: several parts hot, often the regulator and the electrolytics together, and the board draws current the moment it is powered. Inrush does not usually kill silicon at all — it damages the mechanical parts in the path, so look at the connector and the switch. Kick kills narrowly and specifically: one switching device dead, everything else untouched, and the failure appears when a coil turns off rather than on.

Worked example: does the inrush matter on this board?

A 12 V motor driver board. 1,000 µF of bulk capacitance across the input, fed through 300 mm of 22 AWG wire and a 2.1 mm barrel jack.

First, the path resistance.

mΩ
300 mm of 22 AWG, there and back 32
Barrel jack contact 30
Capacitor ESR, 1,000 µF electrolytic 60
Total 122

So the peak current is 12 / 0.122 = 98 A, and the time constant is RC = 0.122 × 0.001 = 122 µs. The capacitor is 95% charged in three time constants, so the whole event is over in about 370 µs.

Now decide whether it matters, which is three separate questions.

Does it damage the connector? A 2.1 mm jack is typically rated 5 A continuous. 98 A for 122 µs is well inside its thermal capacity — I²t here is about 1.2 A²s, which will not heat it. But the jack makes contact by wiping, and drawing 98 A across a partially made contact is what causes arcing and pitting. It will not fail today; it will develop an intermittent in six months.

Does it trip the supply? Almost certainly, if the supply is fast. A bench supply set to a 1 A limit sees a 98 A demand and drops into constant-current mode, so the rail rises slowly instead — which is fine and is one reason to power a new board from the bench rather than a wall adapter.

Does it matter for the motor? No. The running current is what the motor draws, and it has nothing to do with this.

So: not urgent, but fit the NTC before the board goes into anything that gets plugged and unplugged. A 10 Ω NTC turns 98 A into 1.2 A, and costs one part and about 0.3 W of running loss.

Do it

Fifteen minutes, and the third step is the one that changes a habit.

  1. Find the bulk capacitor on any board you own and read its value. Estimate the path resistance the way the table above does, and work out the peak.
  2. Look at the power connector on that board. Is there anything in series to slow the edge? On most hobby boards there is not.
  3. Now find one inductive load in your projects — a relay, a solenoid, a motor, a buzzer — and check whether there is a diode across it. If there is, note which way round it is, since a flyback diode fitted backwards is a dead short across the rail.
  4. Then set your bench supply’s current limit to twice what your board should draw, and leave it there. That is the habit this tutorial is really for.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 5 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

  1. 1Why does swapping a 2 A supply for a 5 A one not endanger a board that draws 300 mA?

    Not quite. A linear regulator passes whatever the load draws and a switcher takes what it needs. Neither is limiting anything at 300 mA.

    Right. Rating is a ceiling, not a delivery. The board still draws what V over R gives, so the larger supply changes nothing until something faults.

    Not quite. The output filter smooths ripple and stores a little charge. It does not absorb unused capacity, because unused capacity is not flowing anywhere.

    Not quite. There is one rail here, and sharing is not the mechanism. The reason is simply that current is drawn rather than pushed.

  2. 2Why is a P-channel MOSFET usually preferred to a Schottky diode for reverse protection?

    Not quite. Both act far faster than a human plugging a connector, so speed is not the deciding factor here. The steady-state drop is what separates them.

    Not quite. Leakage does rise with temperature, but a Schottky still blocks reverse voltage perfectly well. The reason to prefer the FET is the forward loss.

    Not quite. It needs thermal thought around an amp or so, not milliamps. The case for the FET is efficiency in normal operation rather than heat alone.

    Right. It conducts through a low channel resistance rather than a junction. On battery-powered hardware that difference is measurable running time.

  3. 3What limits the peak current into a discharged bulk capacitor at power-on?

    Right. Nothing else is in the path. An empty capacitor holds no voltage, so the full rail appears across whatever series resistance happens to exist.

    Not quite. A fast supply's limit does intervene, which is why a bench PSU is the safe way to power a new board. From a wall adapter there is often no limit at all.

    Not quite. The bulk capacitor sits upstream of the regulator and charges regardless of what the regulator is doing. It is not in that path.

    Not quite. Capacitance sets how long the event lasts, not how tall it is. The peak is the same for 100 uF and 1000 uF through the same resistance.

  4. 4A bare transistor switches a 12 V relay coil and dies. What happened?

    Not quite. A relay coil is a modest steady load and most transistors handle it. The damage happens at turn-off, when the current is being interrupted.

    Not quite. Copper resistance rises with temperature rather than falling, so the current drops slightly. The failure is a turn-off transient, not a thermal creep.

    Right. V equals L di/dt, and opening the switch made dt tiny. The inductor produces whatever voltage keeps its current flowing, which was hundreds of volts.

    Not quite. Contact arcing is a real problem worth snubbing on its own, but here the coil side is the culprit and it fails with nothing on the contacts.

  5. 5A board has one dead switching transistor and everything else is untouched. Which mechanism does that suggest?

    Not quite. A body diode clamps to the rail, not away from it, so it would conduct rather than fail alone. The turn-off timing points elsewhere.

    Not quite. Reversal appears across everything at once, so it usually takes the electrolytics and the regulator with it. A single casualty argues against it.

    Not quite. Inrush damages mechanical contacts rather than silicon, so the evidence is a pitted connector. It rarely kills a transistor at all.

    Right. The energy is dumped into whatever opened the circuit. A narrow, single-part failure appearing at turn-off is the signature of a missing flyback path.
