Field-oriented control, implemented rather than derived

One change of reference frame, two DC loops instead of three AC ones, and the calibration step that is worth more than any tuning.

Tutorial 06 of 08 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Say what rotating the reference frame buys, in terms of what the PI loops see
  • Explain what d and q currents do, and why d is held at zero below base speed
  • Calibrate an encoder offset, and put a number on what leaving it costs

The question

Six-step gives you 86% of the available torque at the worst point, a rasp you can hear, and current waveforms that look like a square wave apologising.

The obvious fix is to stop stepping the field and rotate it smoothly, which means each phase carries a sine wave and the controller has to track three moving targets at 700 Hz. A PI controller tracking a sinusoid has permanent phase lag and permanent amplitude error — that is what PI does to a moving reference — so the naive version is worse than what it replaced.

Field-oriented control is the trick that makes the problem easy again, and the trick is not mathematical sophistication. It is a change of viewpoint.

Sit on the rotor

Watch the currents from the stator and they are three sine waves at the electrical frequency. Watch the same currents from a frame rotating with the rotor and they are two numbers that barely move.

That is the whole idea. Nothing about the motor changed; the observer moved. And in the rotating frame the control problem is transformed from track a 700 Hz sinusoid with no lag — which a PI loop cannot do — into hold a constant, which is the one thing a PI loop is perfect at.

This is why FOC is not really about motors. It is a change of coordinates that turns a hard problem into an easy one, and the difficulty in implementing it is entirely in keeping track of where the rotor is.

Two axes, and only one of them useful

In the rotor frame the current has two components, and they do completely different things.

q-axis current is perpendicular to the rotor’s magnetic field, and it is the one that produces torque. τ = (3/2)·p·λ·i_q, and every term but i_q is a property of the motor, so q current is torque and the constant is Kt from tutorial 13.

d-axis current is aligned with the rotor’s field. It pushes directly against the magnets, produces no rotation whatsoever, and dissipates I²R while doing it.

So the setpoint below base speed is i_d = 0 and i_q = whatever the outer loop asks for. That is the entire control law, and it is why FOC gives you a motor that behaves like a torque source you can command directly.

The one exception is worth knowing about: above base speed, back-EMF approaches the supply rail and the motor cannot go faster. Deliberately commanding negative i_d weakens the rotor field, drops the back-EMF and buys more RPM — field weakening, and it costs efficiency to get speed you could not otherwise have. That is a trade you make on purpose, not a thing that happens.

Two matrices and a sine table

The implementation is less than it sounds.

Clarke collapses three phase currents into two orthogonal components in the stationary frame. It is a fixed 2×3 matrix — no angle involved, and since the three currents sum to zero it is really just arithmetic on two of them.

Park rotates those two into the rotor frame, using the measured rotor angle. This is where the encoder enters, and it is the only place trigonometry appears: one sine and one cosine per cycle, from a lookup table.

Then two PI controllers, one on d and one on q. Then the whole chain backwards — inverse Park, then space-vector modulation — to turn the two voltage commands into three duty cycles.

Cost it honestly: about forty multiply-accumulates and two table lookups. On a Cortex-M4F at 168 MHz that is roughly 3 µs, against a 50 µs budget at 20 kHz. FOC is not expensive. It was expensive in 1985, and the folklore that it needs a DSP has outlived the hardware that made it true.

The angle is the whole thing

Everything above depends on knowing the rotor angle, and torque goes as the cosine of the angle error. Get it wrong and you are back to six-step’s problem by a different route.

Angle error Torque lost
1° 0.015%
5° 0.4%
10° 1.5%
20° 6%
30° 13.4% — no better than six-step

Small errors are nearly free, which is the good news. The bad news is that the most common error is not small and not random: it is a constant offset between where your encoder says zero is and where the rotor’s magnetic zero actually is. Nobody aligns an encoder to a magnetic axis when they glue it on.

That offset is free to remove and it takes one calibration at startup: energise the d axis with a modest current, wait for the rotor to be dragged into alignment and settle, and record the encoder reading. That angle is your zero. Store it, or re-run it at each power-on.

This one step is worth more than any amount of loop tuning, and skipping it is the commonest reason a first FOC implementation produces less torque than the six-step it replaced.

What you actually gained

Back to the figure that motivated all this, and now with FOC’s number next to it.

Six-step’s field sits still for 60° while the rotor turns, giving ±30° of angle error, 13.4% torque loss at the extremes and about 14% peak-to-peak ripple at six times electrical frequency.

FOC holds the angle error at whatever your encoder resolution and calibration allow — typically under 2° — so the torque loss is under 0.1% and the ripple that remains comes from the motor’s own cogging rather than from the controller.

Concretely, on the test stand motor: 4.5% more average torque for the same current, no audible rasp, smooth current waveforms, and a machine that responds to a torque command rather than to a voltage. The last one is what track 4 needs, and it is the real reason this tutorial exists.

Worked example: the FOC loop, cycle by cycle

20 kHz, centre-aligned, Cortex-M4F at 168 MHz. One pass of the loop, measured with a pin toggle.

Step Cycles µs
ADC conversions complete, DMA delivered — 0
Read encoder over SPI 210 1.25
Clarke, two currents to alpha-beta 24 0.14
Park, using sin and cos from a 1,024-entry table 68 0.40
PI on d, PI on q, with anti-windup 96 0.57
Inverse Park 62 0.37
Space-vector modulation to three duties 140 0.83
Write three compare registers 18 0.11
Total 618 3.7 µs

3.7 µs of a 50 µs period: 7.4% of the CPU. Comfortable.

Now the interesting part, which is where the time actually went. The maths — the part with a reputation for being expensive — is 2.3 µs. The encoder read is 1.25 µs, a third of the total, and it is a SPI transaction rather than arithmetic.

Two consequences follow, and both are the kind of thing you only see after measuring.

Push the rate to 40 kHz and the budget is 25 µs, so the loop is at 15%. Still fine. Push to 100 kHz and the budget is 10 µs, the loop is at 37%, and the encoder read alone is 12.5% — at which point moving the encoder to a DMA-driven background transfer buys more than optimising every transform.

The other consequence is latency, not load. Between the current measurement and the new duty cycle there is 3.7 µs of computation, plus up to one full PWM period before the new duty takes effect. That total, about 54 µs, is dead time in the current loop, and tutorial 21 in the next track is entirely about what such a delay does to the phase margin you have to work with.

So the honest summary of FOC’s cost on modern hardware: cheap in CPU, non-trivial in latency, and the expensive part is the sensor rather than the arithmetic.

Do it

Twenty-five minutes, and step 2 is the one with the biggest payoff.

  1. Implement Clarke and Park on paper for one set of numbers — three currents and an angle — and check that a pure q current with the rotor at 0° gives you the three phase currents you expect. Do this before writing any code.
  2. Run the alignment calibration on whatever motor you have: energise d, let the rotor settle, read the encoder. Compare that angle to what your code assumes zero is. The gap is usually tens of degrees.
  3. Log i_d and i_q while the motor runs under load. If i_d is not near zero, the angle is wrong or the current loop is not keeping up.
  4. Then measure the loop with a pin toggle rather than estimating it, and split the total between arithmetic and sensor reading. Deciding what to optimise without that split is guesswork.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 5 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

  1. 1What does rotating the reference frame with the rotor change for the PI controllers?

    Not quite. The signal is not merely slowed, it becomes constant in steady state. Describing it as a frequency reduction understates what the transform does.

    Not quite. Loop rate is still set by the electrical time constant and the switching frequency. The transform changes the shape of the reference, not the plant.

    Right. A PI loop following a moving reference has permanent phase and amplitude error. In the rotor frame the reference stops moving and the error goes away.

    Not quite. The inner loops still regulate current and output a voltage command, exactly as before. What changed is the coordinate system they work in.

  2. 2Why is the d-axis current setpoint zero below base speed?

    Not quite. Saturation is a concern at very high current on either axis. The reason d is held at zero is that it produces no torque in the first place.

    Not quite. The two axes are orthogonal and do not subtract from each other. A d component wastes current rather than cancelling the useful one.

    Right. Only the perpendicular component produces rotation. Current along the rotor field dissipates I squared R while contributing nothing to the shaft.

    Not quite. An induction machine genuinely does need d current to establish its field, which is a real difference. On a permanent-magnet motor the magnets supply it.

  3. 3Which transform in the chain requires the rotor angle?

    Right. Clarke is a fixed matrix with no angle in it. Park is the rotation, and it is the only place trigonometry and the encoder reading appear.

    Not quite. The three phase axes are fixed in the stator at known angles, so the matrix is constant. No measurement enters that step.

    Not quite. Only the rotation does. Treating Clarke as angle-dependent suggests the two steps are doing the same kind of work, and they are not.

    Not quite. Modulation works from the voltage vector it is handed, in the stationary frame. The rotation back has already happened by then.

  4. 4What is the commonest angle error in a first FOC implementation, and why is it cheap to fix?

    Not quite. A mounted encoder's zero is mechanically fixed and does not wander in normal service. The offset is there from the moment it was fitted.

    Not quite. This is real and grows with speed, and some drives compensate for it. It is a smaller effect than an uncalibrated offset of tens of degrees.

    Not quite. Quantisation is usually well under a degree on any reasonable encoder, and averaging adds lag. It is not where the torque is going.

    Right. Nobody glues an encoder aligned to the magnetic axis. Energising d, letting the rotor settle and recording that reading removes the whole error.

  5. 5What remains of the torque ripple once FOC is working properly?

    Not quite. Current ripple at the carrier does exist and inertia filters it out mechanically. It is not usually the dominant remaining torque variation.

    Not quite. Dead-time distortion is a genuine effect and matters most at low current, where compensation helps. Cogging is the larger residual in most motors.

    Not quite. The field rotates continuously under FOC and does not step at all. That is precisely the mechanism being removed.

    Right. With angle error under a couple of degrees the controller's contribution is below a tenth of a per cent. What is left is magnetic, from the slots.
