H-bridges, and why the MOSFET is hot

Four states and one forbidden combination, the two loss terms, and why a hot transistor is usually a gate driver problem.

Tutorial 03 of 08 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Separate conduction loss from switching loss, and say which frequency each favours
  • Explain why a weak gate drive makes a transistor hot without changing its rating
  • Find the dominant term in a thermal path and size the copper accordingly

The question

You build a bridge from MOSFETs rated 60 V and 80 A. You drive 3 A through a motor. Within a minute the transistors are too hot to touch, and one of them eventually fails short.

Nothing is over-rated. 3 A of 80, 12 V of 60. The part is not the problem — the 80 A rating assumes a gate driven hard to 10 V, a case held at 25 °C, and a switching transition measured in tens of nanoseconds. You supplied none of those, and the datasheet number quietly assumed all three.

Four states, and one forbidden

An H-bridge is four switches around a load, and the useful combinations are fewer than the sixteen the arithmetic suggests.

Forward closes the high side on one leg and the low side on the other. Reverse is the mirror. Coast opens all four, and the motor’s inductance freewheels through the body diodes until its energy is gone. Brake closes both low sides, shorting the winding so the motor’s own back-EMF opposes rotation — which is why a shorted motor is hard to turn by hand.

Then the one that must never happen: both switches on one leg, which is the supply connected to ground through two transistors and nothing else. No inductance, no resistance, no limit. This is shoot-through, and dead time from tutorial 08 exists to make it structurally impossible rather than merely unlikely.

Two losses, and which one you are paying

A switching transistor dissipates in two ways, and they behave completely differently, so the first diagnostic question is always which one dominates.

Conduction loss is I²R_DS(on) while the device is fully on. It scales with the square of current and does not care about frequency at all.

Switching loss is the energy burned during each transition — while the device is partly on, it has both significant voltage across it and significant current through it, which is the worst place to be. That energy is fixed per edge, so the loss scales linearly with frequency.

The diagnostic: if the transistor gets hotter when you raise the PWM frequency at the same current, it is switching loss. If it does not, it is conduction. And switching loss is the one that is usually your fault rather than the part’s.

The gate is a capacitor

Here is where the opening failure comes from.

A MOSFET’s gate is, electrically, a capacitor of a few nanofarads. Turning the device on means moving a fixed quantity of charge into it — the datasheet calls this Q_g, and for a decent power FET it is 30 to 100 nC.

Transition time is therefore charge divided by drive current. A proper gate driver sourcing 1 A moves 30 nC in 30 ns. A microcontroller pin sourcing 20 mA moves the same charge in 1,500 ns, fifty times longer.

And the whole of that transition is spent in the high-dissipation region. So driving a power FET from a GPIO does not merely switch it slowly — it multiplies the switching loss by fifty, at the same current, with the same part. That is the entire explanation for the opening scenario.

The rule with no exceptions worth the words: a power MOSFET gets a gate driver. They cost under a euro, and the alternative is a transistor that is hot for reasons its rating gives no hint of.

Bootstrapping the high side

An N-channel MOSFET turns on when its gate is about 10 V above its source. On the low side the source is ground, so 10 V does it. On the high side the source is the switching node, which is at the supply rail when the device is on — so the gate needs to be at rail plus 10, which is above the highest voltage in the circuit.

The standard solution is a bootstrap capacitor. While the low side conducts, the switching node is at ground and the capacitor charges to 12 V through a diode. When the high side turns on, the node rises to 12 V and the capacitor rides up with it, holding its gate at 24 V relative to ground and 12 V relative to its source.

It works, and it has one consequence to design around: the capacitor only recharges while the low side is on. Hold the bridge at 100% duty and it never recharges. The gate droops, the FET leaves saturation, R_DS(on) rises sharply, and it gets hot doing nothing unusual.

So a bootstrapped bridge has a maximum duty — typically 95 to 98% — and a control loop that can command 100% needs either a clamp or a charge pump instead. This is a real constraint in track 8, where full throttle is a state the aircraft visits.

The thermal path, and the term that dominates

Heat gets from the die to the air through resistances in series, measured in kelvin per watt, and the arithmetic is the same as a voltage divider.

Junction to case is the package, typically 1 to 2 K/W, and it is the part’s property. Case to pad is the solder joint, a fraction of a K/W. Pad to ambient is copper area and airflow, and on a bare SMD footprint it is 40 to 60 K/W.

Look at those magnitudes. The last term is thirty times the other two combined, which means the package you chose barely matters and the copper you drew is almost the entire answer. At 2 W and 50 K/W total, the junction sits 100 °C above ambient — over the limit in a warm room, with a part that is nowhere near its current rating.

Doubling the copper pour under the part is worth more than any choice between two similar transistors. Thermal vias to a ground plane on the other side are worth more still. Both are free at layout time and impossible afterwards, which is why this belongs in your head before track 7 rather than after it.

Worked example: why the bridge is hot

The opening board. IRLZ44N-class FETs, 12 V, 3 A motor current, 20 kHz PWM, gates driven from a GPIO through a 220 Ω resistor. R_DS(on) 0.022 Ω, Q_g 48 nC.

Conduction loss.
Current 3 A
R_DS(on) at 10 V gate 0.022 Ω
P = I²R 9 × 0.022 = 0.20 W

Two devices conduct at a time, so 0.4 W in the bridge. Comfortable, and this is the number people compute.

Switching loss, with a proper driver.

Transition time = Q_g / I_drive = 48 nC / 1 A = 48 ns. Energy per edge is roughly ½·V·I·t = 0.5 × 12 × 3 × 48e-9 = 0.86 µJ. Two edges per cycle at 20 kHz:

P = 2 × 0.86e-6 × 20,000 = 0.035 W. Negligible, as intended.

Switching loss, driven from a GPIO.

The pin sources about 20 mA, and 220 Ω into a gate at 5 V gives roughly (5 − 2.5)/220 = 11 mA at the plateau. Transition time = 48 nC / 0.011 A = 4,360 ns.

P = 2 × (0.5 × 12 × 3 × 4360e-9) × 20,000 = 3.1 W.

There it is. Conduction 0.20 W, switching 3.1 W — the loss is fifteen times the conduction term and ninety times what a real driver would produce, at identical current with the identical part.

Now the temperature. 3.3 W total per device, on a bare TO-220 pad with no heatsink at about 62 K/W:

ΔT = 3.3 × 62 = 205 °C above ambient. The part is destroyed, and it is destroyed by the driver rather than by the load.

Two fixes, and their arithmetic. Fitting a 1 A gate driver takes total dissipation from 3.3 W to 0.24 W, and ΔT to 15 °C. Alternatively, keeping the GPIO drive and dropping the PWM to 1 kHz takes switching loss to 0.16 W and ΔT to 22 °C — which also works, and puts the drive squarely in the audible band.

The transferable point: when a transistor is hot, compute both terms before buying a bigger one. A bigger FET has more gate charge, so if switching loss is dominant, the upgrade makes it worse.

Do it

Twenty minutes, and step 3 is the measurement that settles arguments.

  1. Find the gate charge and R_DS(on) of any power FET you have. Compute both loss terms at a current and frequency you actually use.
  2. Raise the PWM frequency by 4× at constant current and feel the transistor. If the temperature moves, switching loss dominates and you have your answer.
  3. Put a scope on the gate and measure the actual rise time. Compare it to Q_g / I_drive. This is the measurement that tells you whether your driver is doing what you think.
  4. Then look at the copper around the part on your board and estimate the pad- to-ambient resistance. Multiply by your total loss. If the answer is over about 60 °C of rise, the layout is the problem rather than the part.
