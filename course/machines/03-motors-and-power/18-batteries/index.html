LiPo C-rating, sag, and the BMS you do not have

Why the pack is not the voltage on the label, what a C-rating is worth, and the four protections a hobby pack does not include.

Tutorial 07 of 08 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Measure a pack's internal resistance and predict its sag at a given current
  • Say why voltage is a poor state-of-charge estimate over most of the discharge
  • List what a hobby pack does not protect against, and who has to

The question

You size the drive for a 4S pack: 14.8 V, motors that want 60 A at full throttle, a 100C battery so the current is well within rating.

At full throttle the pack reads 12.3 V, the motors produce noticeably less than you calculated, the low-voltage alarm fires, and the pack is warm afterwards. Nothing failed. The 14.8 V was a resting figure, the 100C was a number with no test behind it, and 144 W of your power budget is being dissipated inside the battery.

The battery is the one component in the system that changes its behaviour based on how hard you use it, and every calculation downstream of it needs the loaded figure rather than the label.

The pack is not the voltage on the label

A cell is a voltage source in series with a resistance, and that resistance is the whole story. Terminal voltage is V_rest − I·R_internal, so the harder you pull, the less you get.

For a decent 4S pack, each cell has perhaps 8 to 12 mΩ of internal resistance, so the pack has about 40 mΩ. At 60 A:

  • Voltage lost: 60 × 0.040 = 2.4 V
  • Power dissipated inside the pack: 60² × 0.040 = 144 W

Both numbers matter and the second is the one people miss. That 144 W is not reaching the motors; it is heating the battery, which is why a pack worked hard comes back warm and why a warm pack sags more than a cold one — resistance rises with temperature, so the effect compounds during a flight.

The design consequence: every voltage-dependent calculation uses the loaded voltage. Motor RPM from Kv, available power, bootstrap headroom from tutorial 14 — all of them see 12.3 V, not 14.8 V.

What a C-rating is worth

C-rating is capacity multiplied by a number: a 1,500 mAh pack at 100C claims 150 A.

There is no standard test behind it. No agreed duration, no stated temperature, no maximum acceptable sag, and no independent verification. Two packs both labelled 100C can differ by a factor of two in what they actually deliver, and the number has inflated steadily for years because nothing constrains it.

The honest alternative takes two minutes and gives you a number about this pack at this age:

  1. Measure the resting voltage.
  2. Apply a known load — the motors at a known throttle, with a current meter.
  3. Measure the voltage again.
  4. R = ΔV / I.

Now you can predict sag at any current, which is what you actually wanted from the C-rating. And repeating it every few months tells you how the pack is ageing, because internal resistance is the property that degrades first and it degrades long before capacity does.

Why voltage is a bad fuel gauge

A lithium polymer cell’s discharge curve is deliberately flat — that is a feature, since it means your electronics see a stable rail. It is also what makes state-of-charge estimation hard.

Across the middle 80% of capacity the cell moves from about 3.9 V to 3.8 V. That is 0.1 V spread over most of the pack’s useful energy, against a sag that can be 0.6 V per cell under load. The measurement noise is six times the signal.

Then, in the last 15%, it falls off a cliff.

So: gauge by coulomb counting — integrate current over time — and use voltage only for the cutoff at the bottom, where the curve is finally steep enough to mean something. And take the cutoff decision under load rather than at rest, because a pack that reads 3.7 V resting may be at 3.2 V while flying, and 3.2 V is where damage starts.

This is why every flight controller in track 8 reports both a percentage and a voltage, and why the percentage is the one to trust in the middle and the voltage the one to trust at the end.

What a hobby pack does not have

A laptop battery contains a protection board that prevents over-discharge, over-current, over-temperature and cell imbalance. A hobby lithium pack contains none of that. It is cells, wires and a plug.

So the four protections are your problem:

  • Over-discharge. Below about 3.0 V per cell the cell is damaged; below 2.5 it may be dangerous to recharge. You enforce a cutoff, and you enforce it under load.
  • Over-current. Nothing limits the pack. A short across a 4S pack delivers hundreds of amps into whatever caused it. A fuse costs one euro and is the only thing standing between a wiring mistake and a fire.
  • Cell imbalance. Cells drift apart over cycles, and one weak cell hits the danger zone while the pack average looks fine. The balance lead exists for this; use it every charge, not occasionally.
  • Temperature. Usually nothing at all watches this, and it is the honest gap in most hobby builds. A thermistor taped to the pack and read by the flight controller costs almost nothing.

None of this is alarmism. Lithium polymer stores a great deal of energy in a soft pouch with no protective circuitry, and the failure mode is a fire that supplies its own oxidiser. Treat charging as an attended activity and storage as something that happens in a metal box at 3.8 V per cell.

Worked example: sizing a pack for the quadcopter

Track 8’s aircraft, planned before buying. Four motors, 550 g all-up, a target of 10 minutes hover.

Step 1 — hover current. Hover is roughly 50% throttle, which for these motors and props is about 6.5 A each, so 26 A total plus 1 A for the electronics.

Step 2 — capacity, honestly. 27 A for 10 minutes is 27 × (10/60) = 4.5 Ah. But you may not use the whole pack: 80% depth of discharge is the sensible ceiling for cycle life, so the pack must be 4.5 / 0.8 = 5.6 Ah.

Step 3 — check the sag at full throttle, which is the step people skip.

5,000 mAh, 4S, measured 6 mΩ/cell
Pack resistance 24 mΩ
Hover current 27 A sag 0.65 V
Full throttle 95 A sag 2.3 V
Voltage at full throttle 14.8 − 2.3 = 12.5 V
Power lost inside the pack at full throttle 95² × 0.024 = 217 W

Step 4 — the part that closes the loop. 12.5 V instead of 14.8 V is a 16% reduction, and motor RPM follows voltage, so full-throttle thrust is about 16% lower than the datasheet’s bench figure. If your thrust-to-weight calculation used 14.8 V, your actual margin is smaller than you think — and this is the arithmetic behind the advice in track 8 to size for a hover at 50% throttle rather than 70%.

Step 5 — and the weight, which changes the answer. A 5,000 mAh 4S pack is about 480 g, against an all-up target of 550 g. That does not fit. Going to 3,000 mAh at 290 g brings the total to around 360 g of airframe plus battery, but the hover current drops with the weight, so the endurance does not fall proportionally.

Iterating once: at 360 g the hover current is roughly 27 × (360/550) = 17.7 A, and 3,000 mAh at 80% gives 2.4 Ah, so endurance = 2.4 / 17.7 × 60 = 8.1 minutes. Less than 10, from a pack 40% smaller.

That non-linearity is the whole of multirotor sizing: a bigger battery carries itself, and there is an optimum beyond which more capacity buys less flight time. Track 8 does this properly. What matters here is that the internal resistance you measured in step 3 is one of the inputs.

Do it

Twenty minutes, and step 1 gives you a number you will use repeatedly.

  1. Measure the internal resistance of a pack you own: rest voltage, then voltage under a known load, divide. Compare the implied maximum current to the C-rating printed on it.
  2. Work out the power dissipated inside that pack at your peak current. If it is more than a few percent of your total, the pack is a component in your thermal design.
  3. Log pack voltage through a full discharge and plot it. Find the point where the curve turns down, and set your cutoff there rather than at a number somebody recommended.
  4. Then check what your build does if a cell fails or the pack shorts. If the answer is nothing, fit a fuse before the next session — it is the cheapest item in this entire course.
