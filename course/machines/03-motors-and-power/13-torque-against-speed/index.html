Torque against speed, the curve that decides the design

One straight line, two constants that are the same constant, and why peak power and peak efficiency are nowhere near each other.

Tutorial 02 of 08 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Convert between Kv and Kt, and say why a motor cannot have both
  • Explain why stall torque is a thermal figure rather than a magnetic one
  • Find the operating point as the crossing of a motor line and a load line

The question

You size a motor from its datasheet: it makes 0.14 Nm, you need 0.11 Nm, so there is margin. You build the machine and the motor runs hot enough to smell after four minutes, and it never reaches the speed you wanted.

The 0.14 Nm was stall torque, available at zero speed for a few seconds before the winding cooks. The speed you wanted was no-load speed, available only when the motor is doing no work. Neither number describes the place you intended to operate, and the datasheet gave you both without saying so.

Everything in this tutorial comes off one straight line, and the line is the thing to internalise rather than any of the numbers on it.

The line

For a brushed DC motor at a fixed voltage, torque falls linearly with speed. Two endpoints define it: stall torque at zero speed, and no-load speed at zero torque. Draw the line between them and you have the motor.

Two consequences follow immediately, and both are counter-intuitive the first time.

Output power is a parabola on that line, zero at both ends — no power at stall because nothing moves, no power at no load because nothing is resisting — and peaking exactly in the middle, at half the stall torque and half the no-load speed.

Current is proportional to torque, not to speed. A motor spinning fast with no load draws almost nothing. The same motor held still draws its stall current, which is the supply voltage divided by the winding resistance and is often twenty times the running figure. That is the number your bridge, your shunt and your supply limit have to survive.

Kv and Kt are one constant

A brushless motor is sold by Kv, in RPM per volt. A servo motor is sold by Kt, in newton-metres per amp. These look like two independent specifications and they are the same physical property written from opposite ends.

They are related by a conversion that is worth memorising:

Kt [Nm/A] ≈ 9.55 / Kv [RPM/V]

So a 2,300 Kv racing motor makes 0.0042 Nm per amp. A 300 Kv gimbal motor makes 0.032 Nm per amp — nearly eight times more torque for the same current, and roughly one eighth the speed for the same voltage.

The important word is trade. Winding a motor for more turns of thinner wire raises Kt and lowers Kv; fewer turns of thicker wire does the reverse. The product is fixed by the magnetics and the iron, not by the winding, so no choice of winding escapes it.

Gearing buys back whichever end you gave up, and charges for it in backlash, noise, mass, efficiency and one more thing to wear out. That is a real option and often the right one — just not a free one.

Stall torque is a thermal number

Stall torque appears on the datasheet as a single figure, which invites you to treat it as a property of the motor. It is really a property of the motor and how long you ask.

Nothing magnetic stops a motor producing stall torque indefinitely. What stops it is that the winding is dissipating I²R with the rotor stationary, so there is no airflow from rotation and often no useful conduction path. The winding heats on a thermal time constant measured in seconds to minutes, and the insulation has a temperature limit.

So the datasheet’s stall torque is available for perhaps a second or two. The continuous rating — often a fifth to a tenth of it — is the one you may design around, and good datasheets give both. Datasheets that give only stall torque are quoting the impressive number.

The practical consequence for track 4: a control loop that saturates its output against a stalled load is a heating problem on a timescale the loop knows nothing about. Current limiting is not only about protecting the bridge.

The operating point is a crossing

Here is the piece that makes the line useful rather than decorative.

The motor’s line says what torque it produces at each speed. The load has a line too: a fan needs torque rising with the square of speed, a conveyor needs roughly constant torque, a pump is somewhere between. Plot both on the same axes and they cross at exactly one point.

That crossing is where the machine runs. Not where you wanted it to run — where the physics puts it.

And this reframes open-loop speed control entirely. Raising the applied voltage shifts the whole motor line to the right, so the crossing moves to a higher speed. Adding load steepens the load line, so the crossing moves left. There is no separate speed-setting mechanism; there is one intersection, and the two things you can move are the voltage and the load.

Sketching the two lines before buying anything takes five minutes and answers the question the datasheet does not: what speed will this actually run at.

Peak power is not peak efficiency

Two optima on the same curve, in different places, and confusing them is how motors get sized badly.

Peak output power is at the midpoint: half stall torque, half no-load speed. Peak efficiency is much closer to no load, typically around 80% of no-load speed and at fairly light torque.

At the peak-power point, efficiency is roughly 50% — half the electrical input is becoming heat in the winding. That is fine for a few seconds of acceleration and unpleasant as a steady state, because now you have a thermal design problem as well as an electrical one.

So the rule for sizing: put the normal operating point near peak efficiency and let the peak-power point be somewhere you visit briefly. A motor chosen so that its everyday load is the maximum it can produce is a motor that will be hot every day.

Worked example: sizing the test stand motor

A 2205-size BLDC, 2,300 Kv, 12 V, phase resistance 0.11 Ω. Everything else follows from those four numbers.

The two endpoints.
Kv 2,300 RPM/V
No-load speed at 12 V 2,300 × 12 = 27,600 RPM
Kt 9.55 / 2,300 = 0.00415 Nm/A
Stall current 12 / 0.11 = 109 A
Stall torque 0.00415 × 109 = 0.453 Nm

Stop at that stall current. 109 A is not a number this motor survives, and it is not a number your bridge survives either — it is what the line says happens if you apply 12 V to a stationary motor with nothing limiting it. Which is exactly what a naive velocity loop does on its first step command.

Peak power, for reference.

Half of each: 13,800 RPM at 0.227 Nm. Power = τω = 0.227 × (13,800 × 2π/60) = 328 W, drawing about 55 A. Also not survivable continuously.

Where it will actually live. The stand loads the motor by hand — call it 0.02 Nm.
Torque 0.020 Nm
Current = τ/Kt 4.8 A
Copper loss = I²R × 3 phases 4.8² × 0.11 × 3 = 7.6 W
Speed from the line 27,600 × (1 − 0.020/0.453) = 26,380 RPM
Mechanical output 0.020 × 2,762 = 55 W
Rough efficiency 55 / (55 + 7.6) = 88%

That is a sensible place to run: 88%, 7.6 W of heat in a motor that can shed it, and speed within 5% of no-load.

Now the numbers that size the hardware, which are none of the above.

  • The shunt and the bridge are sized by the transient, not the steady state. A step from rest with a 10 A software current limit means the bridge sees 10 A, so a 30 A bridge and a shunt rated for it.
  • The supply’s current limit goes at 10–12 A, per tutorial 01. Setting it at 6 A because “it only draws 4.8” means every acceleration trips the bench.
  • The current limit is what protects the motor from its own line. Without one, the 109 A stall figure is not hypothetical — it is what a shorted rotor or a jammed shaft delivers.

Read it back: of the six numbers on the datasheet, the one that determined the whole electrical design is phase resistance, which is the one nobody looks at.

Do it

Twenty minutes, and step 4 is the design habit.

  1. Take any motor you own and find its Kv or its Kt. Convert to the other one. Check whether the result matches your intuition about the motor’s size.
  2. Compute its stall current from voltage over winding resistance. If the datasheet omits resistance, measure it with a meter across two phases — remember that reads two windings in series.
  3. Sketch its torque-speed line and the load line of whatever it drives. Mark the crossing and predict the running speed. Then measure it.
  4. Then, before you buy anything for the gate: work out where your intended operating point sits as a fraction of no-load speed. If it is near 50% you are at peak power and it will be hot; near 80% and you are near peak efficiency.
