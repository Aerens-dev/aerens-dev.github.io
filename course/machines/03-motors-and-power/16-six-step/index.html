Six-step commutation

Six states, three sensors, two impossible codes, and the fourteen per cent of torque that six-step throws away.

Tutorial 05 of 08 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Decode three hall sensors into a commutation state, and detect a dead sensor for free
  • Say where the torque ripple in six-step comes from, and how large it is
  • Explain why sensorless starting is open loop and what that costs

The question

The brushes are gone and their job is yours. The windings still have to be energised in sequence as the rotor turns, and getting that sequence wrong by one state means the motor pushes the rotor away from where it is going instead of towards it.

Six-step is the crude answer, and crude is the right first answer: it is a lookup table with six rows, it runs on any microcontroller, and it works. It also throws away about a seventh of the available torque and makes a noise. This tutorial is both halves of that.

Six states, and what the gap costs

Six-step energises two of the three phases at a time and leaves the third open. Six such combinations exist, and cycling through them in order produces a rotating field that drags the rotor round.

The field, though, does not rotate smoothly. It jumps by 60° every commutation and then sits still while the rotor keeps turning. So the angle between the field and the rotor sweeps across a 60° window instead of holding at the ideal 90°, and the torque follows the cosine of the error.

That is the entire cost of six-step, and everything unattractive about it — ripple, noise, heat that does no work — comes from that one geometric fact.

Three sensors, six codes, two impossible

Knowing which of the six states to apply means knowing where the rotor is to within 60°, which is exactly what three hall sensors spaced 60° apart provide.

Three binary sensors give eight codes. Six of them occur, in a fixed cyclic order — 001, 011, 010, 110, 100, 101 — and 000 and 111 never occur, because the sensors are physically arranged so that at least one and at most two are active at any angle.

Those two impossible codes are a free diagnostic. Check for them on every read: one comparison, and it catches a disconnected sensor, a broken wire or a failed device before the commutation logic acts on a rotor angle that is not where the rotor is. Without the check, a dead sensor produces violent, expensive behaviour that looks like a control problem.

Read the halls in the interrupt driven by their own edges rather than polling them. The transitions are the events; the levels between transitions carry no new information.

Two driven, one floating

In each state, one phase is pulled high, one is pulled low and the third is open — neither switch on that leg conducts.

That floating phase is more useful than it looks. With no current flowing in it, the voltage on its terminal is the motor’s own back-EMF for that winding. And the point where that back-EMF crosses the midpoint of the supply falls exactly 30° before the next commutation should happen.

So a sensorless drive works like this: watch the floating phase, detect the zero crossing, wait 30° worth of time based on the current speed, then commutate. Three phases take turns floating, so you get six measurements per electrical revolution — precisely the six you need.

That is why six-step and sensorless go together, and why field-oriented control in the next tutorial is harder to do without a sensor: FOC drives all three phases all of the time, so nothing ever floats and there is nothing to observe.

What the ripple actually costs

Put a number on it, because the number decides whether six-step is good enough.

The angle error sweeps ±30° around ideal, and torque goes as the cosine. At the extremes, cos 30° = 0.866, so torque is 13.4% below ideal. Averaged across the sweep it comes out about 4.5% down, and the peak-to-peak ripple is around 14% at six times the electrical frequency.

Where that matters and where it does not:

  • A propeller or a fan — barely at all. The inertia is large relative to the ripple period and the load does not care.
  • Direct-drive positioning, a gimbal, a robot joint — it matters a great deal. 14% ripple at six times electrical frequency is visible in the position and audible in the room.
  • Anything battery-powered — the 4.5% average loss is 4.5% of flight time.
  • Low speed — worst case, because the ripple frequency drops into the range where the mechanics resonate and the inertia no longer smooths it.

Starting without sensors

Back-EMF is proportional to speed. At standstill it is zero, so a sensorless drive has no idea where the rotor is at the moment it most needs to know.

The standard start is three phases and none of them is elegant. Align: apply a fixed state and wait, so the rotor is dragged to a known angle. Ramp: advance the field open loop on a rising speed profile and hope the rotor follows, since nothing is checking. Hand over: once back-EMF is detectable, usually somewhere between 5 and 10% of rated speed, switch to closed loop.

Every one of those steps can fail. If the load is too heavy the rotor does not follow the ramp and you get a stall with full current flowing. If the alignment kick is too weak the rotor starts from the wrong place. If handover happens too early the zero crossings are noise.

This is why hall sensors persist despite costing three wires and a connector: they know where the rotor is at zero speed, which is the one thing sensorless cannot do. The test stand uses halls or an encoder, and it does so to make tracks 4 and 17 about control rather than about starting.

Worked example: commutation timing at speed

The 2205 motor from tutorial 13, which has 14 poles — 7 pole pairs. Running at 6,000 RPM mechanical.

Electrical frequency and the commutation rate.
Mechanical 6,000 RPM = 100 rev/s
Pole pairs 7
Electrical frequency 700 Hz
Commutations per electrical rev 6
Commutation rate 4,200 per second
Time per state 238 µs

Now the timing budget. Each hall transition must be detected and acted on inside a fraction of 238 µs, because latency there is angle error.

Time As angle error
Interrupt latency, tutorial 07 0.45 µs 0.11°
Hall debounce, if you add 10 µs 10 µs 2.5°
Total 10.5 µs 2.6°

2.6° of error on top of the ±30° six-step already has. Negligible — and note what dominates: the debounce, which is a software choice, is twenty times the interrupt latency. That is the wrong instinct arriving from button handling. Hall sensors on a spinning motor do not bounce; they are magnetic. Debouncing them adds error and buys nothing.

Where it stops working. Push to 30,000 RPM: electrical 3,500 Hz, commutations at 21,000 per second, 47.6 µs per state. Now 10 µs of debounce is 12.6° of error, which is a fifth of the whole window, and the motor loses noticeable torque and gets hot.

Read it back: at 6,000 RPM every timing sin is forgiven, and at 30,000 the same code is a real problem. The transferable habit is to express every latency as an angle rather than a time, because angle is what the motor experiences and it scales with speed while your microseconds do not.

Do it

Twenty minutes, and step 3 is the one that shows you the ripple.

  1. Count the magnets on a brushless motor you own — that is the pole count, and pole pairs is half it. Work out the commutation rate at a speed you use.
  2. Write the hall decode as a six-entry lookup with an explicit illegal-code branch. Confirm the branch fires by unplugging one sensor.
  3. Spin a six-step-driven motor slowly and listen. The rasp is the ripple, at six times electrical frequency. Then look at the current waveform on a scope and find the six lumps per revolution.
  4. Then express your commutation latency as an angle at your top speed. If it is more than a couple of degrees, find out which part of it is a debounce you did not need.

  3. 3What makes the floating phase useful in a sensorless drive?

    Right. With no current in that winding the terminal voltage is the induced EMF. Its midpoint crossing falls thirty degrees before the next state is due.

    Not quite. An open phase carries no current at all, which is what makes its voltage readable. Return current flows through the driven pair.

    Not quite. Inductance sensing is a real technique for finding a stationary rotor, and it is how some drives start. It is a different method from watching back-EMF.

  4. 4In which application does six-step's ripple matter least?

    Right. The load's inertia is large relative to the ripple period, so the speed barely responds. The average torque loss still costs some efficiency.

    Not quite. The 4.5 per cent average loss is 4.5 per cent of range, which is exactly the quantity such a design is optimising.

    Not quite. This is the worst case rather than the best: low speed puts the ripple frequency into the mechanical range and there is no gearing to absorb it.

  5. 5Why can a sensorless drive not close the loop at standstill?

    Right. The observable the scheme depends on is produced by motion. Starting therefore means forcing the rotor open loop until the signal appears.

    Not quite. The table is indexed by rotor angle rather than by speed, and a stationary rotor has a perfectly good angle. The difficulty is measuring it.
