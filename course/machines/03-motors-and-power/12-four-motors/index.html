The four motors you will actually use

What each motor asks of the electronics, why the brush is a consumable, and what deleting it hands to your firmware.

Tutorial 01 of 08 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Pick a motor type from what the electronics must do rather than from the shaft output
  • Say what a stepper's missed step costs and why nothing reports it
  • Explain what removing the brushes transfers to the controller

The question

You need something to move. The catalogue offers brushed motors, steppers, RC servos and brushless motors, and the specifications you can compare — torque, speed, size, price — are the ones least likely to decide it.

What actually decides it is what each motor asks of the electronics and the firmware. A brushed motor and a brushless motor of the same size do nearly the same mechanical job; one needs a single H-bridge and the other needs three half bridges, rotor position, and about four hundred lines of code. That is the choice, and the shaft is nearly incidental to it.

What each one asks of the electronics

Brushed DC is two wires and one bridge. Apply voltage, it spins; reverse it, it reverses. The commutation — switching current between windings as the rotor turns — is already solved, mechanically, inside the motor.

Steppers hold position without any sensor, by moving a known angle per command. Two bridges, a fixed step sequence, and no feedback loop at all.

RC servos are a complete position loop in a box. One signal wire, a pulse width in, and a shaft that goes there.

Brushless — BLDC or PMSM — needs three half bridges and needs to know where the rotor is. Everything the brushes were doing is now yours.

The pattern: as you go down that list the electronics get harder and the motor gets better. Where you stop is a judgement about how much firmware the job is worth.

The brush is a consumable

A brushed motor’s cleverness is that the switching problem is solved by geometry. Carbon brushes press on a segmented commutator, and as the rotor turns, the contact moves from segment to segment and the current in the windings reverses at the right moment, automatically, with no electronics at all.

It works, and it charges rent in three currencies. Arcing every time contact breaks, which is a broadband noise source sitting inside your enclosure and coupling into everything. Carbon dust, which accumulates and eventually tracks across insulation. And contact resistance, which is a fixed voltage drop that matters most at low speed and high current, exactly where you often want the motor to work hardest.

The number that ends the argument for anything that runs continuously: brushed motor life is measured in hundreds to a few thousand hours. Brushless is tens of thousands, limited by bearings. That is why a drone motor is not brushed, and it is a life figure rather than a performance one.

Position without measuring it

A stepper’s appeal is that it gives you position with no encoder. Command 200 steps and the shaft has turned exactly one revolution, because the rotor has 200 stable equilibria and it settles into each one in turn.

The catch is the word exactly, which holds only while the load stays below the motor’s torque at that speed. Exceed it for one moment — a jam, an acceleration too steep, a resonance — and the rotor slips to the next equilibrium instead of the one you meant.

Then two things happen, and the second is the problem. The position is wrong, and the controller does not know. It is counting commands, not measuring angle, so it carries on with a position that is permanently off by however many steps were lost. The error never washes out, and the first evidence is usually the machine hitting something.

This is why serious stepper systems either run with generous torque margin — often 50% — or add an encoder and stop being open loop, at which point they have most of the complexity of a brushless drive and less of the performance.

A loop you cannot see into

An RC servo is a small closed-loop position controller sold as a component: a potentiometer on the output shaft, a comparator, an H-bridge and a gearbox. The pulse width you send is the setpoint — 1.0 to 2.0 ms across the range — and the loop inside does the rest.

That is genuinely useful, and it is worth being clear about what it costs. The signal is one-way. Nothing comes back out: no measured position, no current, no fault, no indication that it reached the commanded angle or that it is still trying.

So a servo stalled against an obstruction keeps pushing at full current until something gives — the gears, usually nylon, are the intended sacrifice. The only way to detect it from outside is to watch the supply current, which means your board needs a current sense on the servo rail if you care.

The design rule that follows: an RC servo is right where the load is well-behaved and a failure is cheap, and wrong anywhere you need to know what happened.

Deleting the brushes moves the work

Take the brushes out and the wear item, the arcing and the contact drop all go with them. What does not go is the commutation — the windings still have to be energised in sequence as the rotor turns. That job moves into the electronics, and it needs two things it did not need before: three half bridges instead of one, and knowledge of where the rotor is right now.

That second requirement is the whole of the difficulty, and it is why the rest of this track exists. Tutorial 16 solves it crudely with hall sensors and six discrete states. Tutorial 17 solves it properly with field-oriented control, which is the same problem answered continuously instead of in sixty-degree lumps.

What you buy is worth the trouble for anything that runs: roughly twice the power density, efficiency in the high eighties rather than the seventies, and a life limited by bearings. The multirotor in track 8 is not possible any other way.

Worked example: choosing a motor for the test stand

The track 3 gate needs a motor to hold velocity under a load you apply by hand. Four candidates, same rough size and price.

Brushed Stepper RC servo BLDC
Bridges needed 1 2 0 3
Rotor position needed no no no yes
Feedback available none none none encoder or hall
Firmware, roughly 40 lines 120 10 400+
Continuous life ~1,000 h 10,000+ ~500 h 10,000+
Useful for track 4? partly no no yes

The last row decides it, and it is worth spelling out why the obvious cheap answer fails.

The stepper is out because there is nothing to control. It goes where it is told until it does not, and a velocity loop around it has no measurement to close on. Track 4 needs a plant with feedback.

The RC servo is out for the same reason from the other direction: the loop already exists and is sealed. You cannot tune what you cannot see, so it teaches nothing about cascade control.

The brushed motor with an encoder would work for track 4 — it is a real plant, it has a real velocity loop, and it is much less firmware. It is a legitimate cheaper path and worth naming as one. What it does not give you is current control inside the velocity loop, because a brushed motor’s current is not something you resolve into components; and the cascade in tutorial 23 is built around exactly that.

BLDC with an encoder is the answer. It costs the most firmware and it is the only one that carries you into tracks 4, 5 and 8 without being replaced.

One number to size it: a 2205-class motor at 12 V draws about 1.2 A holding a modest load, and peaks near 8 A on a step change. The supply’s current limit from tutorial 01 goes at 10 A, not at 2 — set it for the transient, not the average, or your first velocity step trips the bench.

Do it

Fifteen minutes, and step 3 is the one that changes a purchase.

  1. Take any motorised thing near you — a printer, a drill, a fan, a hard drive — and work out which of the four it uses. The clue is the wire count: two, four, three, or three plus signal.
  2. Find a stepper datasheet and its pull-out torque curve. Note how fast the torque falls with speed, and pick the speed where you would stop trusting it.
  3. Price a brushed motor and a BLDC of the same shaft power, then add the driver for each. The motors are close; the drivers are not, and that gap is the real cost of the choice.
  4. Then, for the gate: pick the motor you will actually buy and write down its stall current. That number sets the bridge, the shunt, the wire gauge and the supply limit, and everything in the next six tutorials refers back to it.
