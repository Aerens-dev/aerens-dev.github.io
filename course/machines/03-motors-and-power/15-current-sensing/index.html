Current sensing you can afford

Three places to put the sensor, the shunt value that is neither hot nor invisible, and why the timer triggers the ADC rather than software.

Tutorial 04 of 08 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Choose a shunt value from amplifier offset rather than from thermal headroom
  • Say why a low-side shunt must be sampled in the middle of the on time
  • Explain what two shunts give you on a three-phase motor, and what it costs

The question

Your current reading is noise. Not noisy — noise. It bears no relation to the torque the motor is producing, it changes when you touch the board, and at 30% duty it reads about half what it reads at 60% for the same load.

Nothing is broken. You put the shunt in a sensible place, chose a sensible value, and read it whenever the control loop came round. Each of those three decisions is wrong in a different way, and current sensing is the part of a motor drive where being approximately right produces something completely useless.

Torque is current. Everything in track 4 is a current loop underneath. So this measurement is the one the rest of the course rests on.

Three places to put the sensor

Low-side shunt sits between the bottom of the bridge and ground. Cheap, and the signal is referenced to ground so an ordinary op-amp reads it. The cost is that it only sees current while the low-side switch conducts — during freewheeling the current is going round through the motor and the diodes and the shunt sees nothing.

In-line shunt sits in the phase wire itself and sees the current continuously, whatever the switches are doing. The cost is that it floats: one end swings between ground and the rail at 20 kHz, so it needs a differential amplifier with high common-mode rejection and enough bandwidth to ignore a 12 V step while measuring 50 mV.

Hall effect sensors measure the field around the conductor. Galvanically isolated, no insertion loss, and available as a chip with the conductor built in. The cost is bandwidth — typically 50 to 200 kHz — plus offset drift with temperature.

For field-oriented control in tutorial 17, in-line is what you want, and two low-side shunts with careful timing is the affordable answer.

Choosing the shunt value

The obvious trade is signal against heat: a bigger shunt gives more millivolts and dissipates more watts. At 10 A, a 100 mΩ shunt gives a full volt and burns 10 W, which is absurd. So smaller is better, and people then reach for the smallest shunt the thermal budget permits.

That is the wrong end to optimise from. The real floor is the amplifier’s input offset and its drift, because that error is added to your signal and no amount of averaging removes it.

A typical current-sense amplifier has 50 to 150 µV of offset. Against a 50 mV full-scale signal that is 0.3%, which is fine. Against a 5 mV signal it is 3%, and the drift with temperature is another 1% you cannot calibrate out because it moves while you run.

So: pick the smallest shunt that gives an amplifier a signal it can work with, which in practice means aiming for 50 to 100 mV at maximum current. At 10 A that is 5 to 10 mΩ, dissipating 0.5 to 1 W — real, and manageable with a proper power resistor and some copper.

When to sample

Here is the source of the opening symptom — the reading that changes with duty cycle.

A low-side shunt sees current only while its switch is on. Sample outside that window and you read zero or ringing. And the switching edges are violent: di/dt through the parasitic inductance of the shunt and its traces produces hundreds of nanoseconds of ringing that dwarfs the signal.

So there is exactly one good instant: the middle of the on time, after the ringing has died and before the next edge. At 20 kHz and 50% duty that window is about 25 µs wide, and at 10% duty it is 5 µs.

Software cannot hit that reliably — an interrupt with jitter lands anywhere in the period, which is precisely why the reading tracked duty cycle. The answer is that the timer triggers the ADC in hardware. Every motor-capable timer has this: a compare channel dedicated to firing the ADC at a programmed point in the period, with no software in the path and no jitter.

Set that up before writing any control code. A current loop built on a badly-timed measurement will be tuned to compensate for the timing, and then break when the duty cycle changes.

The amplifier has to have settled

The sample instant is fixed by the timer. Whether the amplifier has caught up by then is a separate question, and it turns the amplifier’s bandwidth from a specification into a requirement.

A current-sense amplifier sees a step from zero to full scale at every switching edge, and it needs to settle to within your resolution before the sample. A 100 kHz part has a step response measured in tens of microseconds; at 20 kHz PWM and low duty, that is longer than the whole window, so the ADC reads a number partway up the settling curve. It looks plausible, it is repeatable, and it is wrong by an amount that varies with duty cycle.

The rule: amplifier bandwidth at least ten times the PWM frequency, and check the settling time against the shortest on-time you will use rather than against the average. At 20 kHz that means 200 kHz or better, which is inexpensive and is not what a general-purpose op-amp gives you.

Two shunts, three currents

A three-phase motor with no neutral connection obeys Kirchhoff: i_a + i_b + i_c = 0, always. So measuring two phases gives the third by subtraction, and you save a shunt, an amplifier and an ADC channel.

This is standard and it is what most drives do. Two things are worth knowing before adopting it.

Error concentrates in the calculated phase. The third current carries the noise and offset of both measurements, so it is roughly 1.4× noisier than either and carries the sum of both offsets. If your control algorithm treats the three symmetrically, one of them is quietly worse.

Measuring all three buys a check. With three sensors the sum should be zero, and it is a continuous, free validation of the whole sensing chain. A shunt that has gone open, an amplifier that has saturated, an ADC channel misconfigured — all of them show up as a sum that is not zero, immediately, rather than as a control loop that behaves oddly.

On a board where a sensing failure means an uncontrolled motor, that check is worth the third channel. On the test stand, two is fine.

Worked example: a sensing chain for the test stand

The 2205 motor from tutorial 13. 10 A software current limit, 20 kHz centre-aligned PWM, a 12-bit ADC with a 3.3 V reference. Design the chain from the ADC backwards.

Step 1 — what resolution do you need? The current loop in track 4 wants about 1% of full scale to be several LSBs, so aim for 10 mA resolution at 10 A full scale, which is 1,000 counts. A 12-bit ADC has 4,096, so there is room.

Step 2 — the shunt. Target 50 mV at 10 A, giving 5 mΩ and 0.5 W. Use a 1 W 2512 part, and note that its tempco matters: 100 ppm/°C over a 50 °C rise is 0.5%, which is inside the budget but not negligible.

Step 3 — the gain. The signal is bipolar (current flows both ways), so bias the amplifier output to mid-rail at 1.65 V and swing ±1.5 V around it.

Step 4 — check the bandwidth. 20 kHz PWM, and the shortest on-time to be usable is about 10% duty, so 5 µs. Settling to 12-bit accuracy takes roughly nine time constants, so τ ≤ 0.55 µs, so bandwidth ≥ 290 kHz. Specify a 500 kHz part and stop worrying.

Step 5 — the sample point. Centre-aligned PWM puts the counter’s underflow event exactly in the middle of the on time for the low-side switches, and every motor timer can trigger the ADC on underflow. One register write, zero software.

Now check the whole chain against the requirement.

1.2% of 10 A is 120 mA, against a 10 mA resolution target. Read that back: the resolution is fifteen times better than the accuracy, and the dominant term is the shunt’s own tolerance — a 1% resistor, not any of the clever parts.

That is the useful conclusion. If you want better, buy a 0.1% shunt for a few euro more, which improves the total to 0.6%. Buying a better amplifier improves nothing, and buying a 16-bit ADC improves nothing at all.

Do it

Twenty minutes, and step 2 is the one that explains the opening symptom.

  1. Find a current-sense amplifier’s datasheet and note its input offset voltage and bandwidth. Work out what shunt value makes offset 0.5% of full scale.
  2. If you have a bridge running, scope the shunt voltage directly across the resistor. Look at how long the ringing lasts after each edge, and compare it to the on-time at low duty.
  3. Set up your timer to trigger the ADC rather than reading it from software, and compare the two readings at 20% and 80% duty with the same load.
  4. Then do the error budget for whatever you plan to build. If the dominant term is not the one you expected, you have just saved yourself money on the wrong component.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 5 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

    Not quite. Tolerance is proportional, so it scales with the value and does not worsen as the shunt shrinks. It matters for accuracy at any size.

    Not quite. Johnson noise in a milliohm resistor is nanovolts and is far below the amplifier's own contribution. It is not a practical limit here.

    Not quite. Gain between the shunt and the ADC solves resolution cheaply. Gain amplifies the offset along with the signal, which is why offset is the real floor.

  3. 3Why must the timer rather than software trigger the ADC for a low-side shunt?

    Not quite. Triggering selects when the conversion happens and applies no filtering of its own. Any averaging is configured separately in the ADC.

    Not quite. Software-triggered conversions run at the same speed once started. What hardware triggering supplies is a precise and repeatable instant.

    Not quite. It can read at any time, which is the difficulty. Nothing prevents the read; the problem is that most instants give a meaningless answer.

    Right. The reading is valid only in the settled middle of the on time. Software arrives at an unpredictable point, so the value tracks duty cycle instead of current.

  4. 4How does PWM frequency constrain the current-sense amplifier's bandwidth?

    Not quite. Passing the switching fundamental is a much weaker requirement than settling to full accuracy. Ten times PWM is a useful rule of thumb for that reason.

    Not quite. The ADC samples once per period while the amplifier must track a step. The two requirements are unrelated in magnitude.

    Not quite. Rolling off below the switching frequency does suppress noise and is tempting. It also guarantees the output has not settled when you sample.

    Right. The amplifier sees a step at every edge, and the sample happens partway through the on time. At low duty that window is a few microseconds.

  5. 5What does measuring all three phase currents buy over calculating the third?

    Not quite. A connected neutral breaks the sum-to-zero assumption, which is precisely when two shunts stop working. Three would then be required rather than optional.

    Right. A sum that drifts off zero reveals an open shunt, a saturated amplifier or a misconfigured channel at once, rather than as a loop behaving oddly.

    Not quite. Parallel conversion depends on how many sample-and-hold units the ADC has rather than on the sensing topology. It is not what the third shunt is for.

    Not quite. The two measured phases are equally accurate either way, so only the third improves. The self-check is the larger benefit.
