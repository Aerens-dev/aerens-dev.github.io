The complementary filter, then the Kalman filter

Two sensors split by frequency, the one constant that sets the crossover, and why a Kalman filter is the same idea with the weighting computed.

Tutorial 03 of 07 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Write a complementary filter and say what its time constant trades
  • Explain a Kalman filter as a complementary filter with a computed weight
  • Say what carrying gyro bias as a state buys over calibrating it at power-on

The question

You have two ways to know your roll angle and both are wrong.

Integrate the gyro and you get a beautifully smooth angle that is right for a few seconds and then drifts away, because tutorial 24’s bias integrates without bound.

Take the arctangent of the accelerometer and you get an angle that never drifts and is complete nonsense whenever the vehicle accelerates, because tutorial 25 showed that acceleration and tilt are the same measurement.

The useful observation is that these two failures are in different frequency bands. The gyro is excellent in the short term and bad in the long term; the accelerometer is the reverse. So do not choose — split them.

Each sensor over the band it is good at

A complementary filter high-passes the integrated gyro and low-passes the accelerometer angle, and adds the results. The two filters are designed so their responses sum to exactly one at every frequency, which means nothing is double-counted and nothing is missed.

In practice it is one line:

angle = alpha * (angle + gyro * dt) + (1.0f - alpha) * accel_angle;

That is the whole filter. The first term is the gyro path, integrating from the previous estimate; the second is the accelerometer pulling it back. alpha is close to 1 — typically 0.98 to 0.999 — and it is the only thing to choose.

The relationship to a time constant: τ = α·dt / (1 − α). At 1 kHz with α = 0.999, τ = 1 second.

Understanding it as a frequency split rather than as a blend ratio is what makes it tunable. You are not choosing “how much to trust each sensor”; you are choosing where the crossover sits.

Choosing the time constant

τ is how long the estimate will believe the gyro before gravity is allowed to argue with it, and the two failure modes sit on either side.

Too short — τ = 0.2 s — and the accelerometer dominates. No drift, and the estimate tilts every time the vehicle accelerates, because there is no memory long enough to average the acceleration away.

Too long — τ = 20 s — and the gyro dominates. Manoeuvres are handled beautifully and the estimate drifts, slowly, and takes twenty seconds to recover from any disturbance.

τ ≈ 1 to 3 seconds is the usual answer for a multirotor, and the reasoning is physical: it should be longer than a typical acceleration burst (a second or two) and shorter than the time the gyro’s bias takes to matter (tutorial 24 said about a minute). Those two numbers bracket it, and anywhere in the bracket works.

That bracketing argument is worth more than the number. When someone asks why τ is 2 and not 5, the answer is that both are inside the bracket and it does not much matter — which is a far healthier position than a number nobody can justify.

A Kalman filter is the same thing, with the weight computed

The complementary filter uses a fixed weighting. A Kalman filter uses the same structure and recomputes the weighting every step from how uncertain each source currently is.

Two steps, alternating.

Predict. Advance the state using the gyro, and grow the uncertainty — you integrated a noisy rate, so you know less than you did.

Update. Compare against the accelerometer, and shrink the uncertainty in whatever direction the measurement constrains. How far you move towards the measurement is the Kalman gain, and it is just the ratio of how uncertain you are to how uncertain the sensor is.

That is the whole idea, and the matrix algebra is the bookkeeping required to do it for several correlated states at once. If you have one state and one measurement, the Kalman gain reduces to exactly the α above — the two filters are the same filter.

What the extra machinery buys is adaptivity: during a violent manoeuvre the accelerometer’s effective uncertainty rises and the filter automatically leans on the gyro; while sitting still it does the reverse. A complementary filter cannot do that, because its constant is constant.

Carrying bias as a state

Here is the part that earns the extra complexity, and it is not adaptivity.

Add gyro bias to the state vector — so instead of estimating three angles you estimate three angles and three biases. Now, while the vehicle moves, the accelerometer’s long-term pull does not merely correct the angle; it also reveals the bias that has been corrupting it, and the filter estimates that out.

Tutorial 24 gave the numbers. A power-on calibration leaves 0.02 °/s, drifting to worse, giving 1.2°/minute. Estimating bias continuously takes it to the bias-instability floor, 0.04°/minute — thirty times better, and it keeps working over hours where a power-on value degrades.

This is why every serious attitude filter — Madgwick, Mahony, any EKF — carries bias as a state. It is also the single best argument for moving past the one-line complementary filter, and it is a much better argument than “Kalman is more principled”.

Which one to actually use

Complementary, twenty lines and one constant, is enough for a balancing robot, a gimbal, a camera stabiliser, and most things that are not aircraft. Start here, always. It is debuggable by reading it.

Madgwick or Mahony, about a hundred lines and still one constant, adds quaternions (tutorial 29), a bias estimate, and enough testing by other people that the failure modes are known. This is the sensible default for a multirotor’s attitude estimate and it is what track 8 uses.

A full EKF, several hundred lines and a covariance matrix to maintain, earns its place when you are fusing more than two sensors — GNSS, barometer, rangefinder, optical flow — because then the correlations between states carry real information and the hand-tuned approach runs out.

The cost of the third is not CPU cycles. It is tuning and debugging: an EKF with a badly chosen process noise matrix fails in ways that are extremely hard to diagnose, and “the filter diverged” is not a useful error message.

Worked example: the ten-minute level test

The track 5 build gate: an attitude estimate that stays level for ten minutes on a shaking table. Four implementations, same logged data.

Roll error at 10 min Yaw drift Lines
Gyro integration alone 74° 74° 4
Accelerometer arctangent alone 0.4° n/a 6
Complementary, τ = 2 s 0.6° 9.2° 22
Madgwick with bias estimate 0.3° 6.8° 104

Read the first two rows together. The gyro is hopeless over ten minutes and the accelerometer alone is better than both filters at staying level — because the table is shaking but not accelerating in any sustained direction, so averaging gravity works beautifully.

That is an uncomfortable result and it is the honest one: on this test, the simplest thing wins. If the requirement were only “stay level on a bench”, the arctangent is the right answer.

So why filter at all? Change the test. Same rig, now translating it back and forth by hand at about 1 m/s²:

Roll error, peak
Accelerometer arctangent alone 11.8°
Complementary, τ = 2 s 1.4°
Madgwick with bias estimate 1.1°

There it is. The accelerometer’s 11.8° is tutorial 25’s arctan(a/g) arriving on schedule, and it is precisely the case the filter exists for. The gate’s shaking table tests one failure mode and hand translation tests the other, and a filter has to pass both.

And the yaw column, which is the real finding. Every row drifts in yaw, because nothing in this test observes heading — exactly as tutorial 25 said. 6.8° in ten minutes is the gyro’s bias-instability floor doing its best, and the only way past it is a magnetometer, which is the next tutorial.

One implementation note that cost an afternoon. The complementary filter above uses dt from the actual timestamp rather than the nominal period. With a nominal dt and a loop that was running 3% slow, the integrated gyro was 3% short and the accelerometer spent the whole time correcting it — which looks exactly like a badly chosen τ and is not.

Do it

Twenty-five minutes, and step 3 is the one that shows you the mechanism.

  1. Implement the one-line complementary filter on logged data rather than live. Being able to re-run the same data with different constants is worth the setup time many times over.
  2. Sweep τ from 0.1 to 20 seconds on the same log and plot the error. You will find a broad flat optimum rather than a sharp one, which is the bracketing argument made visible.
  3. Add a constant 0.5 °/s to your logged gyro to simulate an uncalibrated bias. Watch what the complementary filter does, then what a filter with a bias state does.
  4. Then use the real dt rather than the nominal one and compare. On a loop with any jitter at all, this matters more than the filter choice.
