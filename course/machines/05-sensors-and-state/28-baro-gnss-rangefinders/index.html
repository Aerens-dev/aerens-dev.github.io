Barometers, GNSS and rangefinders

A barometer that resolves centimetres and drifts metres, the best number in a GNSS packet, and which rangefinder fails on which surface.

Tutorial 05 of 07 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Say what a barometer is good for and what it is not, on two timescales
  • Name the most accurate quantity in a GNSS fix, and why it is usually ignored
  • Pick a rangefinder from what it will be looking at rather than from its range

The question

You want the aircraft to hold altitude. There are three sensors that claim to know it, and they disagree by several metres.

The barometer says 41.2 m and is beautifully smooth. GNSS says 47 m and jumps around by two or three metres. The rangefinder says 1.8 m, which is obviously right, until the aircraft flies over a hedge and it says 0.4 m without anything having changed about the altitude.

All three are working correctly. Each is answering a slightly different question, over a different band of timescales, and the job is to know which one to believe about what.

The barometer: superb relatively, poor absolutely

A modern MEMS barometer resolves pressure changes corresponding to about 10 cm of altitude, at tens of hertz. That is remarkable and it makes vertical speed and short-term altitude hold easy.

Its absolute reference is worthless. Atmospheric pressure at a fixed point changes with the weather by an amount equivalent to tens of metres over a day, and by several metres over an hour. So an altitude computed from pressure is an altitude relative to whatever the pressure happened to be when you zeroed it, and it drifts from there.

The practical split: use it for vertical rate and for holding an altitude over minutes. Do not use it to know how high you are, and do not use it to return to an altitude recorded twenty minutes ago.

One physical detail that catches every first build: a barometer near a propeller is in a pressure field of its own making. Prop wash produces local pressure changes far larger than the signal. The fix is foam over the port and a sealed enclosure with one small vent — this is why flight controllers ship with a scrap of foam over one chip, and why removing it makes altitude hold much worse.

The best number in a GNSS packet

A GNSS fix gives horizontal position to 2-5 m, and people build on that.

Vertical position is two to three times worse — 5-15 m — and this is geometry rather than a defect. All the satellites are above you, so the vertical component is estimated from a much poorer geometric spread than the horizontal one. No receiver escapes it.

And then the number nearly everyone ignores: velocity, from Doppler shift, accurate to about 0.05 m/s. It is computed from the rate of change of the carrier phase rather than by differentiating positions, which is why it is extraordinarily good.

That matters because differentiating a 3 m position to get speed gives you something dreadful, and the receiver already did the job properly and put the answer in the message. If you are estimating velocity for a position controller, use the velocity field. This is one of the highest-value, lowest-effort improvements available in a navigation stack.

Rangefinders, and what each one cannot see

Three technologies, and the useful way to choose is by what it will be looking at rather than by the quoted range.

Ultrasonic is cheap and slow, around 20 Hz. It fails on soft surfaces that absorb sound — long grass, snow, a ploughed field — and it is badly disturbed by prop wash, which is both acoustic noise and a moving medium.

Infrared time-of-flight is small and fast, up to a few metres. It fails in direct sunlight, which swamps the receiver, and on dark or matte surfaces that return too little light. Indoors it is excellent.

Lidar works to tens of metres at 100 Hz and costs more. It fails on water, glass and wet tarmac — specular surfaces that reflect the beam away rather than back.

And the property they share, which is the one that produces surprises: they measure to the nearest thing below, not to the ground. Flying over a hedge, a parked car or a table gives a step change in reading with no change in altitude, and a naive altitude hold will climb over the obstacle and then descend on the far side.

Fusing altitude is the same argument again

Four sources, each good over a different band — which is exactly tutorial 26’s complementary argument with more terms.

Accelerometer, integrated twice: excellent over milliseconds, and double-integration means its drift is quadratic in time, so it is useless within a second or two on its own.

Barometer: good over seconds to minutes, drifting beyond that.

GNSS vertical: poor but absolute, and its error does not grow with time.

Rangefinder: extremely good and genuinely absolute, but only near the ground, and it measures to whatever is underneath.

So the filter takes short-term motion from the accelerometer, medium-term from the barometer, long-term from GNSS, and switches to the rangefinder below a few metres. That last switch is worth designing carefully, because a source appearing and disappearing is a discontinuity, and a landing is the worst moment for one.

Worked example: an altitude hold that survives a hedge

The quadcopter, holding 2 m over a field with a 1.2 m hedge across it.

The naive version. Rangefinder straight into the altitude loop, setpoint 2 m.
Over the field reads 2.0 m, holds
Crossing the hedge reads 0.8 m, climbs
Climb commanded 1.2 m
Past the hedge reads 3.2 m, descends
Result a 1.2 m bounce at every obstacle

Why the obvious fixes fail. Slew-rate limiting the rangefinder just delays the bounce. A median filter removes a narrow spike and a hedge is not narrow — the aircraft is over it for a second or more.

The version that works treats the rangefinder as measuring terrain height rather than altitude, and fuses it with the barometer, which knows nothing about terrain and everything about the aircraft’s own motion:

  1. Maintain a barometric altitude estimate, drifting slowly.
  2. Maintain a terrain estimate: terrain = baro_alt − rangefinder.
  3. Filter the terrain estimate slowly, with a time constant of a few seconds.
  4. Hold altitude against baro_alt, corrected by the slowly-filtered terrain.

Now a hedge produces a fast change in the rangefinder and therefore a fast change in the terrain estimate — which the slow filter rejects. A genuine altitude change moves both the barometer and the rangefinder together and leaves the terrain estimate unchanged, so it passes straight through.

Naive Terrain-fused
Bounce crossing a 1.2 m hedge 1.2 m 0.11 m
Response to a real 1 m climb command 0.4 s 0.45 s
Behaviour on a gentle slope tracks it tracks it

And the thing to notice, which is the general lesson: the fix was not a better filter on the rangefinder. It was changing what the rangefinder is taken to measure — from altitude to terrain — after which an ordinary low-pass filter does the right thing automatically. Most sensor-fusion problems that resist filtering are really a question about what the sensor is measuring.

Do it

Twenty minutes, and step 2 is the free improvement.

  1. Log a barometer indoors for an hour without moving it. Plot it as altitude. The wander you see is your absolute error budget, and it is larger than people expect.
  2. Find the velocity fields in your GNSS receiver’s messages and compare them to what you get by differentiating position. The difference is usually an order of magnitude.
  3. Point a rangefinder at four surfaces — carpet, glass, a white wall, and out of a window in sunlight. Note which ones return nothing.
  4. Then take any altitude source you have and ask the question this tutorial ends on: is it measuring height above the ground, or height above the nearest thing? The answer changes how it should be fused.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 4 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

  2. 2Why is GNSS velocity far more accurate than differentiated position?

    Not quite. Both solutions use the same satellites in view. What differs is which observable is being used from each one.

    Not quite. Atmospheric delay does dominate position error and it varies slowly, which is precisely why its effect on rate is small. That helps rather than hurting.

    Right. Carrier phase rate is measured directly and very precisely, giving around five centimetres per second. Differentiating a few metres of position gives far worse.

    Not quite. Averaging positions would add lag and still leave correlated errors. The velocity is an independent measurement rather than a smoothed one.

  3. 3What assumption do all three rangefinder technologies share, and why does it matter?

    Right. A hedge, a car or a table gives a step change with no altitude change. An altitude hold built on the raw reading will climb over the obstacle.

    Not quite. A tilted surface does lengthen the measured range and is worth compensating for with attitude. Every technology still reports the nearest return.

    Not quite. Prop wash genuinely disturbs ultrasonic measurements, and optical methods are largely unaffected. It is not shared across all three.

    Not quite. This is true and is exactly where each one fails differently. It is the mechanism behind their individual limits rather than the shared blind spot.

  4. 4Why is an accelerometer useless for altitude on its own within a couple of seconds?

    Right. Double integration turns a small bias into a quadratic position error. It remains excellent over milliseconds, which is exactly its band in the fusion.

    Not quite. Over milliseconds it resolves motion very well, which is why it anchors the short end. Noise integrated twice becomes the random walk that ends it.

    Not quite. The vertical channel carries plenty of information about vertical motion once gravity is removed. The problem is what integration does to its errors.
