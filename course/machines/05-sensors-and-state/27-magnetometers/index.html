Magnetometers, hard iron and soft iron

The shape of the distortion tells you which fault it is, why heading needs attitude first, and why your own motor current is the biggest disturbance.

Tutorial 04 of 07 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Diagnose hard iron and soft iron from the shape of a rotated sample cloud
  • Say why a tilted magnetometer gives a wrong heading, and by roughly how much
  • Estimate the field from a motor lead and place the sensor accordingly

The question

Tutorial 25 left yaw unobservable and tutorial 26 left it drifting at about 7° per ten minutes. A magnetometer fixes that, in principle, by giving an absolute heading reference.

In practice your first magnetometer reading will be wrong by tens of degrees, it will change when you advance the throttle, and it will change again when you tilt the aircraft. Each of those has a different cause and a different fix, and none of them is the sensor being faulty.

This is the messiest sensor in the course, and it is worth the trouble because nothing else gives you heading without either GNSS motion or a camera.

The shape is the diagnosis

Rotate a magnetometer through every orientation and plot the three axes against each other. A perfect sensor in a clean environment traces a sphere centred on the origin, because the field has constant magnitude and you are sampling its direction.

Two things distort it, and they distort it differently.

Hard iron is a permanent magnetic field travelling with the sensor — magnets in the motors, magnetised steel screws, the speaker in a phone. It adds a constant vector, which moves the sphere off centre without changing its shape. Correction: subtract a vector, three numbers.

Soft iron is ferrous material that distorts the external field without generating one — a steel bracket, a battery case. It scales the field differently along different directions, squashing the sphere into an ellipsoid. Correction: a 3×3 matrix.

The diagnostic value is that you can see which you have from the plot. Off centre and round is hard iron alone, which is the common case and the easy fix. Distinctly elliptical means soft iron as well.

Heading needs attitude first

Earth’s field does not lie flat along the ground. At Belgian latitudes it dips about 66° into the ground — the inclination — so only about 40% of the total field is horizontal and the rest points down.

That matters because a magnetometer measures the field in the body frame. If the vehicle is tilted, part of that large vertical component lands on the axes you are using for heading, and the heading is wrong. At 10° of tilt the error can be tens of degrees, not a few.

So heading is computed in two steps, in this order:

  1. Get roll and pitch from the accelerometer and gyro, per tutorial 26.
  2. Rotate the magnetometer reading into the horizontal plane using those angles, then take the arctangent of the two horizontal components.

This is tilt compensation, and skipping it is the commonest magnetometer mistake. It also explains a symptom that otherwise makes no sense: a heading that is correct while hovering level and swings wildly during a banked turn.

Your own current is the biggest disturbance

A straight wire carrying current produces a field of about 2 µT per amp at 10 mm, falling off as 1/r.

Earth’s horizontal field in Belgium is about 19 µT. So a motor lead carrying 10 A at 10 mm from the sensor produces a disturbance the size of the entire signal you are trying to measure — and it varies with throttle, which means the heading error is correlated with exactly what the aircraft is doing.

Three consequences that shape the physical build:

  • Distance is the whole fix, because the field falls off linearly. Doubling the separation halves the error. This is why GNSS-and-compass modules sit on a mast.
  • Twisting the motor leads helps a great deal: the go and return currents cancel, and the residual falls much faster than 1/r.
  • Current-based compensation — measure total current and subtract a fitted field — works and is used in real flight stacks, and is far inferior to moving the sensor 50 mm.

The general form of this is worth carrying beyond magnetometers: a disturbance correlated with your own control action is much worse than a larger uncorrelated one, because it enters the loop rather than averaging out.

What the calibration dance is doing

The figure-of-eight waving that every drone and phone asks for is sampling the sphere. What it needs is coverage of orientations, not duration — thirty seconds of thorough tumbling beats five minutes of rotating about one axis.

Then a fit:

Sphere fit gives you the centre, which is the hard-iron offset. Subtract it. This is most of the available benefit, and for many installations it is all you need.

Ellipsoid fit gives you the axes as well, which is the soft-iron matrix. Only worth doing if the sphere fit leaves a poor residual — fitting nine more parameters to noisy data can easily make things worse.

And the part that catches people: the calibration belongs to the vehicle, not to the sensor. Move the battery, add a steel bracket, re-route a motor lead, or remount the board, and it is invalid. A stored calibration with no record of what configuration it was taken in is a number nobody can trust later.

Magnetic north is not true north

Finally, the easy one that is easy to forget. A calibrated, tilt-compensated magnetometer gives you magnetic heading. Navigation wants true heading, and the difference is the declination.

In Brussels it is currently about 1.4° east, which is small enough to ignore for many purposes and large enough to matter for a long autonomous leg. Elsewhere it is much larger, and everywhere it drifts by a fraction of a degree per year.

The right approach is to look it up from position — the World Magnetic Model is a small table and GNSS gives you the position — rather than hard-coding a constant that silently goes stale. If you do hard-code it, write the date next to it.

Worked example: where to put the compass

The track 8 quadcopter. 4S, four motors, up to 95 A total at full throttle. Decide the mounting position before the frame is drilled.

Step 1 — the error budget. Target: heading good to 3° under all throttle settings. Earth’s horizontal field is 19 µT, so 3° corresponds to a disturbance of 19 × tan(3°) = 1.0 µT.

Step 2 — what the leads produce. Four leads, each up to 24 A, twisted pairs. A twisted pair’s residual field falls roughly as 1/r² rather than 1/r; measured on the bench at 24 A:

Distance Field
10 mm 4.8 µT
25 mm 0.9 µT
50 mm 0.22 µT
100 mm 0.06 µT

Step 3 — the naive answer and why it fails. 25 mm looks adequate against the 1.0 µT budget. But there are four leads, and they do not cancel — the worst case is roughly the sum, so at 25 mm the total is about 3.6 µT, giving 10.7° of error at full throttle.

Step 4 — where it actually has to go. Needing 1.0 µT from four leads means 0.25 µT each, which the table puts at about 50 mm. On a 250 mm frame that means a short mast or a position on the top plate well away from the power distribution.

Step 5 — check the other sources before committing.

Source Field at the chosen spot Heading error
Four motor leads at 50 mm 0.88 µT 2.7°
Battery current path 0.31 µT 0.9°
Motor magnets, static 2.1 µT calibrated out
Steel M3 screws nearby 0.4 µT calibrated out
Varying total 1.19 µT 3.6°

Slightly over budget, and note which rows matter: the static sources are large and harmless, because hard-iron calibration removes anything constant. Only the current-dependent rows survive calibration, and they are the small ones.

That is the whole design insight. Calibration handles the big constant fields and cannot touch the small varying ones, so mounting decisions should be made entirely on the varying sources — which means keeping the sensor away from current paths matters far more than keeping it away from magnets.

The fix to get under budget: move it to 65 mm, or accept 3.6° and note that the yaw estimate is good to about 4° in flight. For a vehicle that navigates by GNSS and uses the compass mainly at low speed, 4° is fine, and knowing that is better than chasing a number.

Do it

Twenty-five minutes, and step 2 is the one that will surprise you.

  1. Log a magnetometer while tumbling it through every orientation, and plot the three axes pairwise. You will see the offset immediately, and probably the ellipse too.
  2. Now log it while running a motor at increasing throttle, holding the sensor still. Plot heading against current. The slope is your disturbance, and it is usually larger than expected.
  3. Compute heading with and without tilt compensation while tilting the device 30°. The difference is why the compensation exists.
  4. Then look up the declination where you are, with today’s date, and note how much it has moved in ten years. That tells you whether hard-coding it is acceptable for your use.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 5 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

    Not quite. A three-axis sensor measures the full vector regardless of orientation, so sensitivity is not the obstacle. The geometry of the projection is.

  3. 3Why is a throttle-dependent magnetic disturbance particularly damaging?

    Not quite. Calibration removes constant fields, and a varying one was never captured by it. The offset itself is unchanged.

    Not quite. Most parts have ranges well above Earth's field and a few microtesla of disturbance. Clipping would be a separate and more obvious failure.

    Right. A disturbance tied to what the vehicle is doing enters the loop rather than washing out over time. An uncorrelated one of the same size matters less.

  4. 4What makes a magnetometer calibration good?

    Right. The fit needs samples spread over the sphere. Thirty seconds of thorough tumbling beats five minutes of rotating about a single axis.

    Not quite. More samples in the same few orientations leave the fit poorly constrained however clean they are. Geometry beats quantity here.

    Not quite. The ellipsoid adds nine parameters and can fit noise when the sphere residual is already small. It is worth doing when the sphere fits badly.

    Not quite. Sources fixed to the vehicle are exactly what you want to capture, since they travel with it. External sources should indeed be avoided.

  5. 5Why should declination be looked up from position rather than hard-coded?

    Not quite. Declination is a property of Earth's field and is entirely independent of the vehicle. Hard iron is corrected separately.

    Right. The World Magnetic Model is a small table and position comes from GNSS anyway. A hard-coded value silently ages and is wrong elsewhere.
