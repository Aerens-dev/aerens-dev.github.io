Quaternions, and why not Euler angles

Where Euler angles break, what four numbers buy, the renormalisation that is not optional, and the sign check that stops the vehicle spinning the long way.

Tutorial 06 of 07 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Say what gimbal lock is and which vehicles it actually affects
  • Read a unit quaternion as an axis and an angle
  • Name the two implementation steps that are always needed and often missed

The question

Roll, pitch and yaw are three numbers anybody can picture. They work fine for the test stand, fine for a balancing robot, and fine for a multirotor that never exceeds 45° of bank.

Point the nose straight up and they stop working — not gradually, but with an arctangent going to infinity and an attitude estimate that produces a step change in yaw for an infinitesimal change in orientation.

Quaternions are the standard fix and they have a reputation for being exotic. They are not: four numbers, one constraint, and two implementation details that matter far more than the algebra.

Where Euler angles break

Euler angles describe an orientation as three rotations applied in a specific order — typically yaw, then pitch, then roll.

At pitch = 90°, the first and third axes become parallel. Rotating about yaw and rotating about roll now produce the same motion, so one degree of freedom is lost: gimbal lock. The orientation is still perfectly well-defined; the parameterisation has failed.

And it fails badly rather than gracefully. Near 90° the arithmetic involves a tangent that grows without bound, so a tiny measurement change produces a huge change in the reported angles, and a filter working in those coordinates goes unstable before the singularity is reached rather than at it.

The practical question is whether your vehicle goes there. A ground robot: no. The test stand: no. A camera gimbal: possibly. A multirotor doing anything aerobatic, or a fixed-wing in a vertical climb: certainly. If the vehicle cannot exceed 45°, Euler angles are fine and simpler, and there is no virtue in quaternions for their own sake.

An axis and an angle, in four numbers

Any rotation in three dimensions can be described as a single rotation by some angle about some axis — Euler’s rotation theorem. A quaternion is that statement, packed:

  • w = cos(θ/2)
  • x, y, z = the unit axis, times sin(θ/2)

with the constraint w² + x² + y² + z² = 1.

So the identity rotation is (1, 0, 0, 0): no angle, any axis. A 90° rotation about z is (cos 45°, 0, 0, sin 45°) = (0.707, 0, 0, 0.707).

What this buys, concretely: no ordering to get wrong, no singularity anywhere, cheap composition — combining two rotations is one quaternion multiply, sixteen multiplies and twelve adds — and a clean integration step for a gyro measurement.

What it costs is legibility. Nobody looks at (0.924, 0.038, −0.114, 0.362) and knows what the aircraft is doing. That is a real cost and the answer is straightforward: convert to Euler angles at the boundary, for display and logging, and never inside the loop.

Renormalise, every step

Here is the first of the two implementation details that matter more than the theory.

Integrating a quaternion with gyro measurements accumulates floating-point error, and that error changes its magnitude. A quaternion that is not unit length does not encode a pure rotation — it encodes a rotation and a scaling, so every vector you rotate with it comes out slightly the wrong length, and the error compounds through the filter.

The fix is one line per step: divide all four components by the norm. It costs a square root and a divide, and on a Cortex-M4F both are single instructions.

float n = sqrtf(w*w + x*x + y*y + z*z);
w /= n; x /= n; y /= n; z /= n;

Skipping it does not fail loudly. The estimate degrades slowly over minutes, in a way that looks like sensor drift and is not — which makes it one of the more frustrating bugs to find if you do not know to look for it.

The sign check nobody mentions

The second detail, and it produces the most spectacular symptom in this tutorial.

q and −q represent exactly the same orientation. Negating all four components leaves the rotation unchanged — geometrically, it is the difference between rotating θ about an axis and rotating (2π − θ) about the opposite axis.

That is harmless until you compute an attitude error for a controller. The error quaternion between current and desired attitude can come out with a negative scalar part, which describes the same final orientation reached the long way round. Feed that to a rate controller and it obediently commands a 330° rotation to correct a 30° error.

The fix is one line, immediately after computing the error:

if (q_err.w < 0) q_err = negate(q_err);

Without it, the vehicle behaves perfectly almost all the time and occasionally does a full rotation to reach an orientation next door. It is intermittent, it depends on the path taken to get there, and it is nearly impossible to reproduce deliberately — which is why it deserves a line in a tutorial rather than a footnote.

Where each representation belongs

Three representations, three jobs, and using each where it fits removes most of the difficulty.

Quaternion — store and integrate. Four numbers, no singularity, cheap to compose, and stable under repeated integration. This is the state variable.

Rotation matrix — transform vectors. Nine numbers, and rotating a vector is one matrix-vector multiply. If you have many vectors to rotate by the same attitude, convert once and use the matrix, which is cheaper than rotating each one by quaternion sandwich product.

Euler angles — show a human. Three numbers somebody can picture, on a display, in a log, in a parameter file.

The rule that follows: convert at the boundary, never in the loop. A filter that converts to Euler angles to apply a correction and back again has reintroduced every problem quaternions were adopted to remove, and this is a real pattern in real code.

Worked example: the track 5 gate, in quaternions

The build gate wants roll and pitch within a couple of degrees after ten minutes on a shaking table, with yaw drift measured and stated. Madgwick’s filter, quaternion state, at 500 Hz.

The loop, per step.

Step Cost
Integrate gyro into the quaternion 32 flops
Compute the accelerometer correction gradient 94 flops
Apply the correction, weighted by beta 12 flops
Renormalise 1 sqrt, 4 divides
Total about 150 flops, 1.1 µs at 168 MHz

Choosing beta, which is this filter’s single constant and plays the role τ played in tutorial 26. Madgwick derives it as the maximum expected gyro error rate: β = √(3/4) × ω_error. With a bias-calibrated gyro at 0.02 °/s residual and some noise, ω_error ≈ 0.1 °/s = 0.0017 rad/s, giving β ≈ 0.0015.

That is far lower than the 0.1 in most example code. Sweeping it on logged data:

beta Roll error, static Roll error, translating by hand
0.1 0.2° 9.8°
0.02 0.3° 2.4°
0.005 0.4° 1.1°
0.0015 0.6° 0.9°
0.0005 2.1° 1.4°

The derived value wins on the case that matters, and the popular default is ten times too high — it tracks gravity so eagerly that it follows linear acceleration straight into the estimate. This is the same trade as τ, arrived at from the sensor’s own specification rather than by taste.

The gate result.
Roll error after 10 min on the table 0.6°
Pitch error 0.5°
Yaw drift, no magnetometer 6.8°
Yaw drift, with the compass from tutorial 27 0.9°
CPU at 500 Hz 0.055%

Gate met. And the two lines that made the difference during development were not in the filter at all: renormalising each step, which cured a slow degradation that looked exactly like a gyro problem; and the error sign check, which cured an occasional full rotation during large attitude commands that appeared perhaps once in fifty tests.

That ratio is the honest summary of quaternions in practice. The algebra is two afternoons. The two implementation details are where the time goes.

Do it

Twenty-five minutes.

  1. Write the Euler-to-quaternion and quaternion-to-Euler conversions and check they round-trip. Then push pitch to 89.9° and watch the yaw value become unstable.
  2. Integrate a quaternion with a constant gyro rate for a simulated ten minutes without renormalising, and plot the magnitude. Then add the one line.
  3. Compute an error quaternion between two attitudes 30° apart, construct the case where the scalar part is negative, and confirm what the rate command would be without the sign check.
  4. Then look through any attitude code you have for a conversion to Euler angles and back inside the loop. If there is one, that is the singularity you thought you had removed.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 5 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

  1. 1What exactly fails at gimbal lock?

    Not quite. Gyros and accelerometers are indifferent to attitude and report normally throughout. The failure is entirely in the representation.

    Right. The orientation itself remains perfectly well defined. It is the three-number description that becomes degenerate at that attitude.

    Not quite. Nothing physical is constrained; the aircraft rotates exactly as before. Only the coordinates used to describe it break down.

    Not quite. Error does grow badly near it, which is the visible symptom. The underlying cause is the lost degree of freedom rather than integration itself.

  2. 2What does the scalar part of a unit quaternion represent?

    Not quite. The axis lives entirely in the three vector components. The scalar carries no directional information at all.

    Right. Half the angle appears because a quaternion applies its rotation twice in the sandwich product. The identity rotation therefore has a scalar part of one.

    Not quite. The unit constraint applies to all four components together rather than making this one a magnitude. It is a trigonometric function of the angle.

    Not quite. It is half the angle, which is the detail that surprises people. Using the full angle gives a rotation of twice what you intended.

  3. 3What happens if a quaternion is integrated without renormalising?

    Not quite. Loud failure would be easier to find. The error accumulates over minutes and looks like a sensor problem instead.

    Not quite. Magnitude error affects all three axes together rather than singling one out. Yaw drift comes from the absent heading reference.

    Right. A non-unit quaternion encodes a rotation and a scaling together. The estimate degrades slowly in a way that resembles sensor drift.

    Not quite. Quaternions have no singularity at any attitude, which is the reason to adopt them. Magnitude drift is a numerical issue rather than a geometric one.

  4. 4Why negate an error quaternion whose scalar part is negative?

    Right. A rate controller given that error commands a rotation of well over three hundred degrees to correct a small one. One line prevents it.

    Not quite. The gains are indifferent to the convention as long as the error is the short path. The issue is which of two valid paths was encoded.

    Not quite. Sign and magnitude are independent, and a perfectly normalised quaternion can have a negative scalar. Renormalising is a separate fix.

    Not quite. Frame conversion is a multiplication by another rotation rather than a negation. Negating changes neither frame.

  5. 5When is a rotation matrix preferable to a quaternion?

    Right. One conversion then one matrix multiply per vector beats a sandwich product each time. For storing and integrating, the quaternion stays better.

    Not quite. A matrix accumulates error in nine entries and needs re-orthogonalising, which is more work than renormalising four numbers.

    Not quite. Nine numbers are no more readable than four. Euler angles are the representation to convert to at that boundary.

    Not quite. Matrix multiplication is twenty-seven multiplies against sixteen for quaternions, so it is the more expensive option here.
