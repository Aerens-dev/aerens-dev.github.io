Every sensor is a liar

Four error terms that behave nothing alike, why averaging stops helping, and the plot that tells you how long to average for.

Tutorial 01 of 07 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Separate noise, bias, drift and scale factor, and say which averaging fixes
  • Read an Allan deviation plot for a sensor's floor and its optimum averaging time
  • Decide where each error is removed: factory, power-on, or continuously

The question

Your gyroscope says the vehicle is rotating at 0.4 °/s. It is sitting on the desk.

You average a thousand samples. It still says 0.4 °/s, with less jitter. You average ten thousand. Still 0.4, very smooth now. You conclude the sensor is broken and order another, and the new one says 0.6 °/s.

Neither is broken. You are averaging away noise, which was never the problem, and the thing you actually want to remove is unaffected by averaging because it is not random. Sensor specifications collapse four quite different phenomena into the word “accuracy”, and getting anything out of a sensor starts with pulling them apart.

Four terms, four behaviours

Noise is random and zero-mean. Average it and it shrinks as 1/√N. It is the only one of the four that averaging touches, and it is usually the one people worry about most.

Bias is a constant offset. The 0.4 °/s above. Averaging does not move it at all — the mean of a biased measurement is the bias. You remove it by measuring it once while you know the truth.

Drift is bias that wanders, on timescales of minutes to hours, with temperature and ageing. A bias measured at power-on is right at power-on and progressively less right afterwards. This is the term that makes dead reckoning fail.

Scale factor is a wrong gain: the sensor reports 0.92× the real rate. It is exactly zero at zero, so every check you do sitting still says the sensor is perfect.

Where averaging stops

Averaging N samples reduces the noise by √N and leaves everything else where it was. So there is a crossing point: once the noise has fallen below the bias, further averaging buys delay and nothing else.

Concretely, for a typical MEMS gyro: noise density 0.01 °/s/√Hz, so at 1 kHz a single sample carries about 0.32 °/s of noise. Average 100 samples and it is 0.032 °/s — and the bias is 0.4 °/s. You are already twelve times below it after 0.1 seconds. Averaging for a further second improves the noise threefold and improves the measurement not at all.

The rule that falls out, and it applies well beyond sensors: find out which error term dominates before optimising anything. Reducing a term that is already an order of magnitude below another one is effort spent on a number nobody sees.

Allan deviation, and how long to average

There is a standard plot that separates these terms by the timescale each lives on, and it is the single most useful thing on an inertial sensor’s datasheet: Allan deviation against averaging time, on log-log axes.

Read it in three parts. On the left, a slope of −½: this is white noise, and it falls as you average, exactly as above. At the bottom, a flat region: the bias instability, which is the best this sensor can do and the averaging time that achieves it. On the right, a rising slope of +½: random walk, where the bias itself is wandering faster than you can average, so longer averaging makes the estimate worse.

That bottom point answers a question that otherwise gets guessed: how long should the power-on bias calibration run? For a typical MEMS gyro the floor sits somewhere between 10 and 100 seconds, which is far longer than the half-second most firmware allows — and also explains why a longer calibration does not keep improving indefinitely.

Scale factor hides at rest

Of the four, scale factor is the one that most often ships.

Every calibration done at standstill sees a scale-factor error of exactly zero, because the error is proportional to the signal and the signal is zero. The sensor passes. It then reports 8% low during an actual manoeuvre, and the integrated angle is 8% short, and nothing about the stationary behaviour hinted at it.

Finding it requires a known non-zero input. For a gyro: rotate the device through exactly 360° — by hand, against a mark — and integrate the reported rate. If the integral says 331°, your scale factor is 0.92 and now you know. For an accelerometer: the six-position test, with each axis pointed up and then down, gives you scale and bias per axis from one gravity vector you can trust.

Both take ten minutes and neither needs equipment. The reason they are skipped is that the sensor already looked fine.

Where each error gets removed

The four terms belong in different places, and putting one in the wrong place is a design error rather than a tuning one.

Scale factor — once, on a rig or with the rotation test, and stored. It is stable over a device’s life.

Bias at rest — every power-on, by averaging while stationary. Cheap, and it handles the day-to-day part of drift.

Bias in flight — this one cannot be measured, because you have no stationary reference. It must be estimated continuously as part of the filter, which is the single biggest reason tutorial 26’s filter carries bias as a state rather than treating it as a constant.

Hard-iron offset on a magnetometer — per installation, because it comes from the magnetic material near the sensor rather than from the sensor.

Noise — never. It is not an error to be removed; it is a property to be weighted, which is exactly what a Kalman filter does with it.

Worked example: how long can this gyro dead-reckon?

An ICM-42688 on the test rig. From the datasheet and ten minutes of logging:
Rate noise density 0.0028 °/s/√Hz
Bias instability, from the Allan floor 2.5 °/hr
Angular random walk 0.17 °/√hr
Power-on bias, measured 0.41 °/s
Bias repeatability between runs ±0.02 °/s

Without any calibration. Bias 0.41 °/s integrates to 0.41 × 60 = 24.6° of heading error per minute. Useless within seconds for anything that cares about direction.

With a power-on bias calibration. The residual is the run-to-run repeatability, 0.02 °/s, so 1.2°/minute. Two orders of magnitude better for thirty lines of code and two seconds of stillness. This is the single highest return available in the whole sensing chain.

With bias estimated continuously. Now the limit is the bias instability itself: 2.5 °/hr = 0.0007 °/s, giving 0.04°/minute. Another thirty-fold, and this is what the filter in tutorial 26 buys.

And the floor nobody can remove. Angular random walk at 0.17 °/√hr means the error grows with the square root of time no matter how good the bias estimate is: 0.17° after an hour, 0.02° after a minute. That term is irreducible.

Now assemble the answer to the actual question:

Time Bias calibrated Bias estimated Random walk
10 s 0.2° 0.007° 0.009°
1 min 1.2° 0.04° 0.022°
10 min 12° 0.4° 0.07°
1 hr 72° 2.5° 0.17°

Read the rows rather than the columns. With a good filter, random walk and bias instability are the same order of magnitude at around one minute — which is the useful design number: below a minute, this gyro’s heading is essentially free; beyond ten minutes, it needs an absolute reference or it is drifting degrees.

That threshold is why the multirotor in track 8 carries a magnetometer at all, and it is why a robot that runs indoors for an hour needs something the gyro cannot provide.

Do it

Twenty minutes, and step 2 gives you a number you will reuse for the rest of the course.

  1. Log any IMU you have, completely stationary, for ten minutes at full rate. Plot the raw gyro. The offset from zero is your bias; the fuzz around it is your noise.
  2. Compute the Allan deviation of that log — twenty lines of Python, or any of the standard scripts. Find the bottom of the curve. That time is how long your power-on calibration should run.
  3. Power-cycle the device five times, re-measuring the bias each time. The spread between runs is what a power-on calibration leaves behind, and it is the number that sets your dead-reckoning budget.
  4. Then do the rotation test: turn the device through exactly 360° and integrate the reported rate. The ratio is your scale factor, and it is almost certainly not 1.000.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 5 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

  1. 1A stationary gyro reads 0.4 degrees per second and averaging thousands of samples does not change it. What is that?

    Right. Averaging converges on the true mean, and the true mean of a biased signal is the bias. It has to be measured against a known truth instead.

    Not quite. A gain error is exactly zero when the input is zero, which is the case here. It cannot produce a reading on a stationary device.

    Not quite. Quantisation would pin the value to a multiple of the least significant bit and is usually far smaller. It also averages out with dither present.

    Not quite. Noise falls as one over root N, so thousands of samples reduce it by tens of times. A value that refuses to move is not random.

  2. 2When does further averaging stop improving a measurement?

    Right. Averaging touches one term. When that term is already an order of magnitude below another, more samples buy lag and no accuracy.

    Not quite. An internal filter changes the noise spectrum you are averaging, which is worth knowing. It does not set where averaging stops paying.

    Not quite. The window is measured in samples and can be any length. Output rate determines how long a given count of samples takes.

    Not quite. Averaging genuinely resolves below one code when noise dithers the input, so it can pass that point. The bias floor arrives first.

  3. 3What does the flat bottom of an Allan deviation curve tell you?

    Not quite. Datasheet specification is a separate guarantee with its own conditions. The curve describes statistical behaviour rather than compliance.

    Not quite. Quantisation appears as a distinct slope on some plots and is usually below the other terms. The flat region is bias instability.

    Right. Left of it noise still dominates and averaging helps; right of it the bias itself wanders. The minimum is the best available and when to stop.

    Not quite. Internal filtering shapes the left-hand slope rather than placing the minimum. The floor is set by bias instability.

  4. 4Why does a stationary calibration miss a scale-factor error?

    Right. A gain error produces no discrepancy when there is no signal, so the sensor passes. It then reports several per cent low during a real manoeuvre.

    Not quite. Each axis carries its own independent scale factor, so nothing cancels. There is simply no signal for the error to act on.

    Not quite. At full range it is several per cent, which is large and easy to see. At rest it is genuinely absent rather than merely small.

    Not quite. Gain errors are present across the band, including at very low frequency. Bandwidth affects the response shape rather than the scale.

  5. 5Why must gyro bias be estimated continuously rather than measured at power-on?

    Not quite. Gyro bias is largely independent of attitude, unlike an accelerometer's gravity component. Time rather than orientation is what invalidates it.

    Not quite. Warm-up drift is real and is one reason the value degrades. The deeper issue is that it keeps moving long after thermal settling.

    Right. A power-on value is right at power-on and progressively less right after. In motion nothing else knows the truth, so the filter has to infer it.

    Not quite. A second or two of averaging puts noise well below bias, so the measurement is perfectly good. Its validity is what expires.
