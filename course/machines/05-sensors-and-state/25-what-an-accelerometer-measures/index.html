What an accelerometer actually measures

Specific force rather than acceleration, why yaw is invisible to it, and why propeller vibration turns into a slow wobble.

Tutorial 02 of 07 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Say what an accelerometer reads at rest, in free fall and while tilted
  • Explain why an accelerometer fixes roll and pitch but never yaw
  • Choose a full-scale range, and say what a clipped sample does to an average

The question

The device is sitting perfectly still on your desk. The accelerometer reports 9.81 m/s² upward.

It is not accelerating. Nothing about it is moving. And yet the name on the part says accelerometer and the number says 9.81.

That is not a calibration error or a sign convention. The sensor is reporting exactly what it measures, and what it measures is not what the name suggests. Getting this straight is the difference between an attitude estimate that works and one that has a sign error nobody can find.

It measures specific force

Inside the part is a small proof mass on springs. The sensor reports the force per unit mass the springs are exerting to hold that mass in place — specific force, and it excludes gravity by construction, because gravity acts on the proof mass and the frame equally and stretches no spring.

Work through the three cases and the name stops being confusing.

On a table: gravity pulls the mass down, the table pushes the case up, the springs carry that push. Reading: 1 g upward. Acceleration: zero.

In free fall: nothing pushes on anything, the springs are relaxed. Reading: zero. Acceleration: 9.81 m/s² downward.

Hovering, tilted 10°: the thrust holding the aircraft up is 1 g along the tilted body axis, so the reading is 1 g rotated 10° in the body frame.

So an accelerometer at rest is a gravity direction sensor, which is exactly what an attitude estimate needs — and the words “at rest” are doing all the work.

Two angles from one vector

Knowing which way gravity points fixes roll and pitch: tilt the device and the 1 g vector moves across the three body axes, and two arctangents recover the angles.

It fixes nothing about yaw. Rotate about the vertical and gravity stays pointing exactly where it was — every axis reads the same value throughout. Yaw is not merely hard to measure with an accelerometer; it is unobservable.

This is why heading always needs a second reference: a magnetometer, a GNSS course over ground, or a visual one. And it is why yaw is reliably the worst of the three angles in any attitude estimate — roll and pitch have a strong, always-available absolute reference and yaw has a weak, easily-disturbed one.

The gyro can hold yaw between references, and tutorial 24 gave you the budget for how long: about a minute with a good bias estimate, before it is degrees out.

Acceleration looks exactly like tilt

Here is the ambiguity that shapes every attitude filter.

An accelerating vehicle experiences a horizontal specific force in addition to the vertical one. The sum is a vector tilted away from vertical — and it is identical to what a stationary, genuinely tilted device would produce. There is no information in the measurement that separates them.

At 2 m/s² of forward acceleration, the apparent tilt is arctan(2/9.81) = 11.5°. A multirotor accelerating briskly reports a tilt that is simply wrong, and a filter that trusts the accelerometer during that manoeuvre will rotate the vehicle to correct an error that does not exist — which produces more acceleration, and so on.

The resolution is the one every attitude filter uses: trust the gyro over short intervals and the accelerometer over long ones. Acceleration averages to roughly zero over time for most vehicles; gravity does not. That single observation is the whole design of the complementary filter in the next tutorial.

A refinement worth knowing: weight the accelerometer by how close its magnitude is to 1 g. If the vector’s length is 1.4 g, the vehicle is definitely accelerating and the reading should be largely ignored for attitude.

Choosing full scale

The range setting is a resolution decision in one direction and a clipping decision in the other, and clipping is much the worse failure.

On a 16-bit part, ±2 g gives 0.06 mg per code and ±16 g gives 0.49 mg. Eight times the resolution sounds decisive until you consider what happens at the limit.

A clipped sample is not a large reading — it is a wrong one. The true value was 3 g and the sensor reports 2 g, and that error is one-sided, so it biases every average, every filter and every integral downstream. One clipped sample per second contaminates the whole estimate, and nothing in the data marks it as suspect unless you check for saturation explicitly.

On an aircraft, a prop strike or a hard landing produces tens of g for a millisecond. ±8 g is the usual answer for a multirotor and ±16 g if the vehicle is small and stiff. And whatever you choose, count the saturated samples and expose the count — a rising saturation counter is one of the most informative diagnostics a flight log can carry.

Vibration becomes a slow wobble

The last one is the practical problem that dominates real installations.

Motors and propellers produce vibration at their rotation frequency and its harmonics — hundreds of hertz. If those frequencies are above half your sample rate, they alias: they fold down and appear as low-frequency content that looks exactly like slow vehicle motion.

A 320 Hz vibration sampled at 500 Hz appears at 180 Hz. Sampled at 250 Hz it appears at 70 Hz. And once it has been sampled, no digital filter can remove it, because it is now indistinguishable from real motion at that frequency.

The fixes are therefore all before the converter:

  • Soft-mount the sensor — this is why flight controllers sit on gel or O-rings, and it is the single most effective measure.
  • Balance the props, which is five minutes with tape and removes most of the fundamental.
  • Use the sensor’s own analogue anti-alias filter, which every modern IMU provides and many projects leave at its widest setting.
  • Sample fast and decimate, which puts the folding point above the vibration.

Track 8’s build gate includes a soft mount for exactly this reason, and a vibration check is part of it.

Worked example: why the hover drifts forward

A quadcopter holds attitude well but creeps forward in hover, about 0.4 m/s, consistently, in still air.

Hypothesis 1 — accelerometer bias. A pitch-axis bias would tilt the estimate, so the vehicle pitches to correct and flies forward. Check it: log the accelerometer with the aircraft level on a flat surface.

Axis Reading Expected
x (forward) +0.021 g 0
y −0.004 g 0
z +0.998 g +1

0.021 g on x. That is an apparent pitch of arctan(0.021) = 1.2°.

Does that explain the speed? In hover, a 1.2° tilt gives a horizontal acceleration of 9.81 × sin(1.2°) = 0.21 m/s². The vehicle accelerates until drag balances it. For a 550 g quad with a drag coefficient giving roughly 0.3 N at 0.5 m/s, terminal speed works out at about 0.45 m/s.

Measured 0.4, predicted 0.45. The bias explains it.

Now the fix, and the part that matters. Level calibration removes the 0.021 g and the drift stops. But ask why the bias was there, because that decides whether it stays fixed:

Candidate Test Result
Sensor bias rotate 180° and re-measure reading follows the vehicle
Mounting not level measure the frame with a level frame is 1.1° nose-down
Calibration done tilted check the surface used it was the workbench

The reading followed the vehicle rather than staying fixed in the world, which means it is not a sensor bias — the sensor is fine and the mounting is tilted 1.1°. Calibrating it out is correct, and it must be redone if the controller is ever remounted.

That distinction is the point of the example. A level calibration silently absorbs both sensor bias and mounting error, and the two have completely different lifetimes: one is a property of the part and one is a property of the last person who took the top plate off.

Do it

Twenty minutes, and step 4 is the one that decides your mounting.

  1. Put an accelerometer flat on a table and read all three axes. Then invert it and read again. Half the difference per axis is the bias, and the average of the magnitudes is your scale.
  2. Rotate it slowly through 360° about the vertical and watch all three axes. Nothing changes. That is yaw being unobservable, seen directly.
  3. Accelerate it by hand along a table and watch the apparent tilt. Compare the angle to arctan(a/g) and confirm they match.
  4. Then spin up a motor near the sensor and log at full rate. Look at the spectrum. Anything above half your filter rate is going to fold down, and you now know whether your mount needs work.
