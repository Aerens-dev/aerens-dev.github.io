Timers, and PWM as the primitive under everything

Four pieces that make every timer feature, the trade between PWM frequency and resolution, and why motor drives count both ways.

Tutorial 03 of 07 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Derive a PWM frequency and its duty resolution from a clock and a reload value
  • Say why a motor drive uses centre-aligned PWM rather than edge-aligned
  • Use input capture to measure an interval without interrupt latency in it

The question

You need to dim an LED, drive a motor, measure a pulse from an ultrasonic sensor, generate a servo signal, and schedule a control loop at exactly 1 kHz.

Those look like five problems. They are one peripheral used five ways, and the peripheral is simpler than its reference-manual chapter suggests — a timer is a divider, a counter, and some comparators. Everything else in that chapter is built from those three.

The reason to learn it properly rather than through a library is that the alternative is doing this work on the CPU, and the CPU is the one resource in the system you cannot buy more of.

Four pieces, and every feature is built from them

The clock comes in at the peripheral bus frequency. The prescaler divides it, giving you a tick of whatever size you like. The counter counts those ticks and wraps at a reload value, which sets the period. Compare units watch the counter and do something — set a pin, clear a pin, raise an interrupt — when it passes a value you choose.

That is the whole thing. PWM is a compare unit driving a pin. A periodic interrupt is the wrap event. A one-shot delay is a counter that stops. Input capture is the counter being read by the hardware instead of written to it.

Concretely: 84 MHz in, prescaler 84, so a 1 µs tick. Reload 1000, so a 1 ms period, which is a 1 kHz PWM. Compare at 250, and the pin is high for the first quarter of every period. That is 25% duty, and it took two register writes.

Frequency and resolution are the same number

Here is the trade that catches people, and it is forced rather than a design choice.

The reload value sets the period. It also sets how many distinct duty cycles exist, because the compare value can only be an integer between zero and the reload. So on a fixed timer clock, frequency and duty resolution are inversely related, and you cannot have both.

At 84 MHz: 1 kHz PWM means a reload of 84,000, which is over 16 bits of duty resolution — far more than you need. 20 kHz means a reload of 4,200, so about 12 bits. 200 kHz means 420, which is under 9 bits, and at that point a one-percent change in duty is four steps and quantisation starts to matter.

For a motor, 20 kHz is the usual landing spot and it is chosen for a physical reason: it is above the range most people hear, so the drive does not whine, and it is low enough that switching losses stay reasonable. 4,200 steps is plenty.

Counting both ways

By default a counter counts up, wraps to zero, and starts again. Every channel’s pulse therefore starts at the same instant — edge-aligned PWM.

For one channel that is fine. For three phases of a motor it means all three switching events happen simultaneously, so all three current spikes land on the same DC-link capacitor at the same moment. The ripple current is three times what it needs to be, and so is the conducted noise.

Centre-aligned PWM counts up to the reload and then back down to zero. Each pulse ends up centred in its period, so the three phases’ edges are spread across it. The DC-link ripple drops, the noise spectrum spreads out, and — the part that matters in track 3 — the current-sense window in the middle of the period is clean and identical for every phase.

The cost is that the period is twice the count, so a reload of 4,200 gives 10 kHz rather than 20. Set the prescaler accordingly.

Input capture writes down the time for you

The counter is free-running whether you look at it or not. Input capture puts a latch on it: when an edge arrives on a pin, the hardware copies the counter into a capture register at that instant, and then raises an interrupt.

The difference from reading the counter in an interrupt handler is the whole point. A handler that reads the counter measures the event plus the interrupt latency plus whatever critical section was in the way — and that error is variable, so it does not even average out. A capture register holds the time the edge actually arrived, and your handler can turn up whenever it likes.

Two captures give an interval exactly: 7,233 − 1,841 = 5,392 ticks, and at 1 MHz that is 5.392 ms with no software timing in it at all. This is how you measure a servo pulse, an ultrasonic echo, an RC receiver channel, or the period of a hall sensor — all of which appear later in this course.

Dead time, and why it is a timer feature

This one belongs to track 3 but it lives in the timer, so it is worth meeting here.

A half bridge has two switches on one leg: high side and low side. Exactly one may conduct at a time, because both conducting is a short circuit across the supply, through two transistors, with nothing to limit it. It is called shoot-through and it is destructive within microseconds.

The obvious approach — drive the low side with the inverse of the high side — fails, because turn-off is slower than turn-on. A MOSFET’s gate charge takes time to remove, so for a few hundred nanoseconds after you command it off it is still conducting, while the other one has already turned on.

So the timer inserts a programmable dead time between one output going low and its complement going high. In hardware, in the peripheral, with no software involvement — because software cannot be trusted with a deadline measured in tens of nanoseconds, and because a firmware crash must not be able to turn the bridge into a short.

Worked example: one timer, a servo and a motor

A 84 MHz APB timer. You need a standard hobby servo on channel 1 and a brushed motor on channel 2. Can one timer do both?

The servo’s requirement. 50 Hz frame, pulse between 1.0 and 2.0 ms, and you want at least 0.5 µs of positioning resolution, which is about 1/2000 of the range.

The motor’s requirement. 20 kHz so it does not whine, and 10 bits of duty is comfortable.

The two share a prescaler and a reload, and those are what set frequency. 50 Hz and 20 kHz cannot both come from one reload, so the answer is no — this needs two timers, and most parts have plenty. But work through the servo case anyway, because the arithmetic is the part worth having.
Timer clock 84,000,000 Hz
Target frame 50 Hz
Total ticks per frame 84,000,000 / 50 = 1,680,000

1,680,000 does not fit in a 16-bit reload, so the prescaler has to take it down. Prescaler 84 gives a 1 µs tick and a reload of 20,000 — which fits, and makes the arithmetic trivial: the compare value is the pulse width in microseconds. Compare 1,500 is centre. Compare 1,000 is one extreme, 2,000 the other.

Resolution: 1 µs steps over a 1,000 µs range is 1,000 positions. You wanted 2,000. So take the prescaler to 42, giving a 0.5 µs tick — now reload is 40,000, still inside 16 bits, and you have 2,000 steps with the compare value being twice the microseconds.

Prescaler 84 Prescaler 42
Tick 1.0 µs 0.5 µs
Reload for 50 Hz 20,000 40,000
Fits in 16 bits yes yes
Steps across the servo range 1,000 2,000
Compare value for 1.5 ms 1,500 3,000

Take the second column. And notice what the exercise really produced: the constraint that decided it was not the servo at all, it was the 16-bit reload register. Halving the tick again would need 80,000 and would not fit, which is the kind of thing you find by doing this arithmetic rather than by choosing a number that feels right.

Do it

Twenty minutes, and step 4 is the one that pays off in track 3.

  1. Set up a timer for 1 kHz PWM on a pin and sweep the duty from 0 to 100%. Measure both frequency and duty on a scope, and check the frequency against your arithmetic to three digits.
  2. Now take the same timer to 200 kHz without changing the prescaler. Watch the duty resolution collapse — step the compare by one and see how much the duty moves.
  3. Feed a signal into an input capture channel and measure its period. Then do the same thing by reading the counter inside an ordinary interrupt handler, and compare the jitter between the two methods.
  4. Then read your part’s reference manual on dead-time insertion and complementary outputs, even though you have no bridge yet. Track 3 assumes you know the peripheral exists.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 5 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

  1. 1An 84 MHz timer with a prescaler of 84 and a reload of 1000. What is the PWM frequency?

    Right. The prescaler gives 1 MHz, and dividing that by the reload of 1000 gives a 1 ms period. Frequency is clock over prescaler over reload.

    Not quite. That skips the prescaler, which has already taken 84 MHz down to 1 MHz. Both divisions apply, one after the other.

    Not quite. The arithmetic is the right shape but out by the wrong factor: 84 MHz over 84 over 1000 is 1000 Hz rather than 84.

    Not quite. That is the tick rate rather than the wrap rate. The counter still has to reach 1000 before the period completes.

  2. 2Why does raising PWM frequency on a fixed timer clock cost duty resolution?

    Right. A shorter period is a smaller reload, and the compare value is an integer bounded by it. Fewer counts means fewer distinct duty cycles.

    Not quite. The register keeps its full width whatever the frequency. What shrinks is the range of values that mean anything, because the reload shrank.

    Not quite. The prescaler usually falls or stays put when you want a higher frequency. It is the reload that has to come down, taking the steps with it.

    Not quite. Jitter is a real effect at high frequency and it does blur fine steps. The resolution limit here is arithmetic and applies with perfect timing.

  3. 3What does centre-aligned PWM buy a three-phase motor drive?

    Right. Edge alignment makes all three spikes land on the DC-link capacitor together. Centring interleaves them and cleans the current-sense window too.

    Not quite. Both alignments deliver the same average for the same duty. What differs is when within the period the switching happens.

    Not quite. It halves, because the counter travels up and down. That is a cost to compensate for in the prescaler, not a benefit.

    Not quite. Dead time is about the two switches on one leg, which still swap over. Centring changes when that happens, not whether it needs a gap.

  4. 4Why is input capture better than reading the counter inside an interrupt handler?

    Right. Hardware copies the counter the moment the edge arrives. A handler that reads it later measures the edge plus a variable delay that does not average out.

    Not quite. They are the same width, since one is a snapshot of the other. The advantage is when the snapshot is taken.

    Not quite. A second edge before you read the register overwrites the first, which is a real hazard to design around. Capture does not buffer history.

    Not quite. Capture still raises an interrupt to tell you a value is waiting. What it removes is the timing error, not the handler.

  5. 5Why is dead time inserted by the timer rather than by firmware?

    Right. Software cannot meet that timing reliably, and hardware insertion means a hung processor leaves the bridge safe rather than conducting through both switches.

    Not quite. It is set by the switch and gate drive rather than by duty, so a fixed value works. The reason it lives in hardware is the timescale.

    Not quite. They are designed to be updated on the fly, which is how duty changes each cycle. Nothing blocks firmware from writing them.

    Not quite. Some drivers do offer desaturation feedback, so sensing is possible. The argument is about timing precision and about failing safe.
