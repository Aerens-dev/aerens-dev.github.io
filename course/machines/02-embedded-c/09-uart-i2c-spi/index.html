UART, I2C and SPI, and how to debug each on a scope

Three buses separated by how they address and how they clock, and the scope signature of each common failure.

Tutorial 04 of 07 · 30 minutes

Afterwards you can

  • Choose between the three buses from addressing and rate rather than from habit
  • Read a framing error as a clock mismatch rather than as noise
  • Name the scope appearance of the five commonest bus failures

The question

The sensor worked yesterday. Today it returns 0xFF to every read. You have checked the wiring twice, rewritten the driver once, and swapped the sensor for a new one, which also returns 0xFF.

Thirty seconds with a probe on the clock line would have told you the bus is running at 12 kHz instead of 400 kHz, because you changed the system clock source last week and the peripheral divider came along for the ride.

This tutorial is mostly about that reflex — put the probe on before you open the driver — and about knowing what you are looking at once it is there.

Wires, addressing, speed

Three buses, and the useful way to separate them is by how a device is addressed and where the clock comes from.

UART has no clock and no addressing. Two wires, point to point, and both ends have independently agreed to run at the same rate. Simple, and the absence of a shared clock is the source of its one characteristic failure.

I²C has a clock and addresses devices in-band, by sending a seven-bit address that everyone hears and one device answers. Two wires for any number of devices, which is the whole appeal. The cost is speed — 100 or 400 kHz typically — and open-drain lines that need the pull-ups from tutorial 02.

SPI has a clock and addresses devices out-of-band, with a dedicated select pin each. Four wires plus one per device, no acknowledgement of any kind, and tens of megahertz. That is the trade: it is fast and it never tells you whether anyone was listening.

For the IMU in the track 2 build gate, at 1 kHz with 12 bytes per sample, SPI is the only comfortable answer.

A UART byte, and why framing errors are a clock story

A byte on the wire is a start bit (a falling edge, from an idle high), eight data bits least significant first, and a stop bit (high). No clock is transmitted.

The receiver starts its own clock on that falling edge and samples in the middle of each bit cell. That is the design’s whole cleverness and its whole weakness: resynchronisation happens once per byte, and after that the receiver is counting on its own.

So a rate error accumulates across the byte. At 3% error, by bit seven the sample point has drifted about a quarter of a cell; at 5% it is closing on a half and the stop bit gets sampled low. A framing error is therefore almost never noise — it is the two ends disagreeing about the rate, and the usual cause is an integer division that did not come out exactly.

Check the arithmetic rather than the wiring: 16 MHz / (16 × 115200) = 8.68, which the hardware rounds to 9, giving 111,111 baud and 3.5% error. That is marginal on its own and fails as soon as the other end is off in the same direction.

I²C, and the acknowledge as the only feedback

A transaction is: start, seven-bit address plus a read/write bit, acknowledge, then data bytes each acknowledged, then stop.

The acknowledge is the only feedback the protocol has, and that makes it the most informative thing on the bus. Three failures, three meanings.

No ACK after the address means nobody is at that address. Wrong address, wrong bus, device not powered, or — very commonly — the seven-bit versus eight-bit confusion, where the datasheet quotes 0xD0 including the direction bit and your driver wants 0x68.

No ACK mid-transfer means the device is there and refused that byte. Usually a register address it does not have, or a write while it is busy.

SCL stuck low means a device is clock-stretching and has not let go, or has lost sync and thinks it is mid-byte. The recovery is to clock SCL manually nine times with SDA released, which walks any stuck device to the end of a byte, then issue a stop. Worth having in your init code, because otherwise the fix is a power cycle.

SPI’s four modes, and the one that looks like a wiring fault

SPI has two configuration bits. CPOL says whether the clock idles low or high. CPHA says whether data is sampled on the first or the second edge of each clock cycle. Four combinations, numbered 0 to 3, and both ends must agree.

Getting CPOL wrong usually produces obvious garbage. Getting CPHA wrong is the interesting one: you sample half a bit period early or late, which means you consistently read the previous bit. Every byte comes back shifted by one bit — 0x2A reads as 0x54 or 0x15 — and that looks like a wiring fault or a broken sensor rather than a configuration line.

The tell is consistency. Noise gives you different wrong answers; a phase error gives you the same wrong answer every time, and one that is arithmetically related to the right one. When a device returns a value that is exactly double or exactly half what you expect, check CPHA before you check anything else.

What the scope shows, and what it means

Five appearances worth recognising on sight, because each one skips a different amount of debugging.

Rounded I²C edges are the pull-up losing to the bus capacitance — tutorial 02’s arithmetic, arriving in visible form. Nothing at all on a line means the peripheral clock is off or the pin is not muxed to the peripheral, and both are one register. The right shape at the wrong rate is a clock-tree assumption that stopped being true, and it is the commonest of the five and the least suspected. Data shifted by one bit is SPI phase. Only the first byte is a missing acknowledge on I²C or a receive overrun on any of them.

None of these needs the driver source. All of them need a probe.

Worked example: does SPI fit the 1 kHz IMU budget?

The track 2 gate wants an IMU read at 1 kHz with no dropped samples. Check it before building it.

What one sample costs. An ICM-42688 burst read is 1 register address byte plus 12 data bytes — accelerometer and gyroscope, three axes each, two bytes per axis — so 13 bytes, or 104 clocks.
SPI clock 10 MHz
Bits per sample 104
Time on the wire 10.4 µs
Budget at 1 kHz 1,000 µs
Wire utilisation 1.04%

Comfortable, obviously. Which means the wire is not the constraint, and the interesting question is what is.

The CPU cost, done three ways.

CPU per sample CPU at 1 kHz
Polled, byte at a time 10.4 µs busy-waiting 1.04%
Interrupt per byte 13 × 38 cycles = 5.9 µs 0.59%
DMA, one interrupt per burst 38 cycles = 0.45 µs 0.045%

Polled looks fine at 1 kHz and that is the trap: it scales with rate, and the whole 10.4 µs is spent doing nothing. Interrupt-per-byte is worse than polling in total cycles, because 24 cycles of entry and exit dominate the one byte of work — a genuinely counter-intuitive result and a good reason to count rather than assume. DMA costs one interrupt per burst and is twenty times cheaper than either.

Now the real constraint, which is neither. At 1 kHz you have 1 ms per sample, and the sample is useless unless the control loop consuming it also fits. Reading takes 10.4 µs of wall clock; the filter in track 5 takes about 40 µs; the FOC loop in track 3 takes about 25 µs. That is 76 µs of 1,000, so the budget is fine — but it is fine by 13×, not by 1000×, and that ratio is the number to remember when track 5 proposes a Kalman filter.

Do it

Twenty minutes. Step 1 will probably surprise you.

  1. Put a scope on the clock line of any bus you have running and measure the actual frequency. Compare it to what you configured. Clock-tree drift is extremely common and almost nobody checks.
  2. Deliberately misconfigure CPHA on a working SPI link and look at what comes back. Confirm the shifted-by-one signature so you recognise it later.
  3. Work out your UART’s real baud rate from the divisor your hardware actually holds, rather than the number you asked for. Note the percentage error.
  4. Then write the I²C bus-recovery routine — nine clocks on SCL with SDA released, then a stop — and put it in your init. It costs fifteen lines and saves a power cycle every time a sensor locks the bus.

Check yourself

One question per idea on this page, 5 in all. Pick an answer and it tells you why. Nothing is scored and nothing is stored.

  1. 1What does SPI give up in exchange for its speed?

    Not quite. That describes UART, which sends no clock. SPI transmits its clock on a wire, which is a large part of why it can run fast.

    Not quite. They share the clock and data lines and take one select pin each, so extra devices cost a pin rather than a bus.

    Not quite. SPI is full duplex by construction, with a line in each direction. That is one of its advantages rather than something it trades away.

    Right. There is no feedback in the protocol at all. A missing or dead device returns whatever the idle line is, which reads as valid data.

  2. 2You get framing errors on a link that is electrically clean. What should you check?

    Not quite. A parity mismatch raises a parity error rather than a framing one, and the two are reported separately. Framing is about where the stop bit landed.

    Right. The receiver resynchronises only on the start bit and counts from there. A few percent of error has drifted past the stop bit by the eighth data bit.

    Not quite. An overrun raises an overrun flag and loses whole bytes rather than mangling their framing. It is a separate error with its own bit.

    Not quite. Reflections do matter on long runs and produce genuine bit errors. The question says the link is clean, which points at timing instead.

  3. 3A device does not acknowledge its address, but it is powered and wired correctly. What is the likely cause?

    Not quite. Too fast a clock usually gives corrupted data or intermittent acknowledges. A consistent silence at the address stage points at the address itself.

    Not quite. An undersized pull-up does load the open-drain output, and it is worth checking against the sink current. It would corrupt data rather than suppress the acknowledge cleanly.

    Right. Datasheets often quote the eight-bit form, such as 0xD0, where the driver wants the seven-bit 0x68. It is the single commonest I2C mistake.

    Not quite. Repeated starts matter for combined read transactions after the address is accepted. The first addressing still uses an ordinary start.

  4. 4Every byte from a SPI sensor reads as exactly double the expected value. What does that indicate?

    Not quite. A word-length mismatch usually splits or merges bytes rather than scaling them cleanly. A consistent factor of two is a single-bit shift.

    Right. A phase error shifts the whole stream by one bit position, which is a doubling or halving. The arithmetic relationship is the giveaway.

    Not quite. Losing the last bit would halve rather than double, and it usually produces intermittent results. Worth checking, but the phase is likelier.

    Not quite. A full bit reversal scrambles values unpredictably rather than doubling them. Some devices do offer the option, so it is worth ruling out.

  5. 5Which bus failure is described as the commonest and the least suspected?

    Right. A clock-tree change upstream rescales every peripheral divider below it. The bus looks perfectly healthy, so nobody measures the frequency.

    Not quite. Distinctive and quickly diagnosed once seen, since the transaction visibly stops. It points at a short list of causes.

    Not quite. This one happens constantly, especially early on. Nothing at all on the line is unambiguous, which is why it does not survive long.

    Not quite. Common, and immediately visible once a probe is on the line. It looks wrong on screen, so it gets suspected quickly.
