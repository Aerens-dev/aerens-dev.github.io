Machines

From code to a thing that moves

A software engineer does not fail at this for lack of theory. The estimator and the control law are already writable. What is missing is everything physical underneath: getting twelve volts to a motor without letting the smoke out, holding a tolerance that turns a printed hole into a bearing seat, and working out which of six sensor readings is lying.

So this is not a reading list. Every track ends on a build gate — something that exists on the bench and does a measurable thing — and the tutorials before it are there to make that thing work. The ladder runs from a blinking LED to a multirotor flying an autonomous square, legally.

Before you start

  • A mechanical engineering degree is assumed, so there is no statics, no materials and no free-body diagrams. Track 6 skips CAD-from-zero entirely and spends its time on the parts a mechanical curriculum tends to cover badly: design for additive manufacturing, tolerance as it behaves on a printed part, and exact constraint.
  • C is assumed, and so is comfort with a debugger. What is not assumed is that you have ever read a datasheet, scoped a signal, or reasoned about a return path.
  • The hardware costs roughly a thousand to two thousand euro from nothing. Every gate names a route through it in simulation instead — PX4 SITL, a motor model, gerbers you never order — which costs the tactile half of the point and nothing else.
  • Every tutorial is built to about 30 minutes. The gates are not: they are measured in days, and the curriculum says which.

Where this comes from

Elecia White, Making Embedded Systems, 2nd ed. (O'Reilly, 2024)
The spine for tracks 1 and 2. Written specifically for a software engineer moving down to the metal, which is exactly this reader.
Quan Quan, Introduction to Multicopter Design and Control (Springer, 2017)
The spine for track 8. The only multirotor text that treats the whole vehicle as a design problem rather than a control problem with a vehicle attached.
Hayes & Horowitz, Learning the Art of Electronics (CUP, 2016)
The lab-course companion to The Art of Electronics, and the one that matters here — it is built around measuring things rather than deriving them.
Austin Hughes & Bill Drury, Electric Motors and Drives, 5th ed.
Track 3. Chosen over the theory-first machine texts because it is written for people specifying and driving motors rather than designing them.
Ned Mohan, Power Electronics: Converters, Applications and Design
The switching half of track 3 — why the bridge is built the way it is, and where the heat comes from.
Karl Astrom & Tore Hagglund, PID Controllers: Theory, Design and Tuning
Track 4, including everything Ziegler-Nichols gets wrong. The theory behind it is Course 1's job, not this one's.
Thrun, Burgard & Fox, Probabilistic Robotics (MIT Press, 2005)
Track 5's estimation material, and the reason the Kalman filter arrives as bookkeeping over a filter you already built.
Paul Groves, Principles of GNSS, Inertial and Multisensor Integrated Navigation, 2nd ed.
Sensor error models in track 5 — bias, drift and scale factor stated properly rather than as folklore.
Alexander Slocum, FUNdaMENTALs of Design (MIT, free)
Track 6, and specifically exact constraint. Free from MIT, and the source for MIT 2.72.
Henry Ott, Electromagnetic Compatibility Engineering (Wiley, 2009)
Track 7. The return path, ground planes and loop area, from the person who wrote the reference on it.
Beard & McLain, Small Unmanned Aircraft: Theory and Practice (Princeton, 2012)
Tracks 5, 8 and 9, with its BYU companion project. The book that carries the state estimation and guidance chain end to end.
Mellinger & Kumar, Minimum Snap Trajectory Generation and Control for Quadrotors (ICRA 2011)
Track 9's trajectory material, and the reason differential flatness is worth the page it takes.
Regulation (EU) 2019/947 and 2019/945
Track 9. The operating rules and the product class marks. Belgian implementation is via the BCAA; check both against the current text, since the class-mark transition dates have moved before.
Phil Koopman, Better Embedded System Software, and CMU 18-642
The discipline underneath track 2 — what makes embedded software reviewable, and the failure modes that are specific to it.
PX4, Betaflight, SimpleFOC and KiCad
Read as primary texts rather than used as black boxes. Where a tutorial explains a mechanism, the corresponding source file is worth the detour.
